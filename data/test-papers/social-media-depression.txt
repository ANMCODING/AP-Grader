Does Social Media Cause Depression in Teenagers?
AP Research
April 2025
Word Count: 3,654
Introduction
In recent years there has been growing concern about the effects of social media on the mental health of teenagers. Many teenagers use social media platforms like Instagram, TikTok, Snapchat, and Twitter for several hours every day. Researchers, parents, and mental health professionals have raised concerns that this heavy use of social media may be contributing to rising rates of depression and anxiety among young people. This paper aims to investigate whether social media use causes depression in teenagers and what factors might explain this relationship.
The research question guiding this paper is: Does social media use cause depression in teenagers?
This is an important question because rates of teenage depression have been rising steadily since around 2012 which coincides with the widespread adoption of smartphones and social media. If social media is causing depression then this is a public health crisis that needs immediate attention from policymakers, parents, schools, and social media companies themselves.
Literature Review
Jonathan Haidt and Jean Twenge have been the most prominent voices arguing that social media is causing a mental health crisis among teenagers. Haidt (2023) argues in his book The Anxious Generation that social media has fundamentally rewired adolescent social life in ways that are deeply harmful particularly for girls. Twenge (2017) similarly argues in her book iGen that teenagers who spend more time on social media are significantly more depressed than those who spend less time. Both authors point to the sharp increase in teenage depression rates beginning around 2012 as evidence of the harmful effects of social media.
However other researchers disagree. Valkenburg et al. (2022) argue that the relationship between social media and mental health is much more complex than Haidt and Twenge suggest and that for many teenagers social media use has no negative effect or even positive effects. Orben and Przybylski (2019) conducted a large study using data from over 350,000 teenagers and found that the effect of social media on wellbeing was very small comparable to the effect of eating potatoes or wearing glasses.
The debate about whether social media causes depression is ongoing and the evidence is mixed. Some studies find negative effects, others find no effects, and some find positive effects. This suggests that the relationship between social media and depression is complicated and may depend on factors like how social media is used, what content is consumed, and individual characteristics of the user.
Methodology
To investigate this question I designed a survey to collect data from teenagers at my high school about their social media use and their mental health. The survey asked students to report how many hours per day they use social media, which platforms they use most often, and how they feel about their mental health. For mental health I used a simple scale asking students to rate their overall mental health from 1 to 10 where 1 is very poor and 10 is excellent.
I distributed the survey to students at my school through Google Forms. A total of 67 students responded to the survey. I then calculated the correlation between hours of social media use and self-reported mental health rating using a simple correlation analysis.
I also planned to conduct interviews with some students to get more detailed information about their experiences with social media and mental health but I did not end up doing this because it was difficult to schedule and some students were not comfortable talking about their mental health.
Results
The correlation between hours of daily social media use and self-reported mental health rating was r = -0.23. This means that students who reported using more social media tended to report slightly lower mental health ratings. However this correlation was not statistically significant (p = .06) which means the result could be due to chance.
When I looked at the data by platform, students who primarily used Instagram reported the lowest average mental health ratings (6.1 out of 10) while students who primarily used YouTube reported the highest average mental health ratings (7.4 out of 10). However I did not run statistical tests on this breakdown because the group sizes were too small for meaningful analysis.
Most students (71.6%) reported using social media for more than 2 hours per day. The most popular platform was TikTok followed by Instagram and Snapchat.
Discussion
The results of my study show a small negative correlation between social media use and self-reported mental health but this correlation was not statistically significant. This means I cannot conclude from my data alone that social media causes depression in teenagers. This is consistent with the mixed findings in the existing literature where some studies find negative effects and others do not.
One reason my results might not be significant is that my sample size of 67 students was too small to detect a real effect if one exists. With a larger sample the correlation of r = -0.23 might reach statistical significance. Another reason is that I used a simple single-item mental health rating rather than a validated depression measure like the PHQ-9 which would give more reliable results.
Limitations
My study has several limitations. First my sample size was small and from only one school so the results cannot be generalized to all teenagers. Second I used a self-reported single-item mental health scale rather than a validated depression measure. Third the correlation I found could be due to reverse causation meaning that teenagers who are already depressed might use more social media rather than social media making them depressed. Fourth I was not able to conduct the interviews I planned which would have given me more detailed qualitative data. Fifth the survey was distributed online and students who responded may be different from those who did not respond in ways that affect the results.
Conclusion
This study found a small non-significant negative correlation between social media use and self-reported mental health in a sample of 67 high school students. The results are consistent with the mixed findings in the existing literature and do not allow me to conclude that social media causes depression in teenagers. More research with larger samples and validated depression measures is needed to answer this important question.
