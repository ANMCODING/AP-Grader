The Experience of First-Generation College Students Navigating Academic Identity in a Predominantly White Institution
AP Research
April 2025
Word Count: 4,312
Introduction
First-generation college students — defined as students whose parents did not complete a four-year college degree — represent approximately 56 percent of all students enrolled in American public universities (Redford & Mulvaney Hoyer, 2017). Despite comprising a majority of public university enrollment, first-generation students graduate at significantly lower rates than continuing-generation peers, with six-year graduation rates approximately 11 percentage points lower (Cataldi et al., 2018). Researchers have attributed this gap to a range of factors including financial stress, lack of social capital, and cultural mismatch between students' home backgrounds and university culture (Stephens et al., 2012). One dimension of this experience that has received comparatively less attention is academic identity — the degree to which students see themselves as belonging in academic spaces and identify with the role of college student. This research investigates the following question: How do first-generation college students enrolled at a predominantly white institution describe their experience of constructing and negotiating academic identity during their first year?
This question matters because academic identity has been identified as a significant predictor of persistence and degree completion independent of academic preparation and financial factors (Ostrove & Long, 2007). Understanding how first-generation students experience identity construction could inform how institutions design first-year support programs.
Literature Review
First-Generation Students and Cultural Mismatch
Stephens et al. (2012) conducted a landmark experimental study in which first-generation students at a selective university were randomly assigned to attend an information session that either acknowledged class-based cultural differences or used a standard university narrative. Students in the cultural mismatch acknowledgment condition showed significantly higher engagement and lower physiological stress responses. This study established that the cultural gap between first-generation students and elite university environments is measurable and addressable. Yosso (2005) extended this framework through the concept of community cultural wealth, arguing that first-generation students bring forms of capital to university — aspirational, navigational, familial, and resistant capital — that institutions systematically fail to recognize and build upon.
Academic Identity and Belonging
Ostrove and Long (2007) examined the relationship between social class background and academic belonging in college students and found that first-generation students reported significantly lower sense of belonging even after controlling for academic preparation. This belonging deficit predicted lower academic performance and higher dropout intentions. Stephens et al. (2015) followed up on their earlier work by examining longitudinal effects of cultural mismatch framing and found that students from working-class backgrounds showed persistent disadvantages in GPA and well-being across multiple academic years when cultural mismatch was not addressed. Both studies establish that belonging and identity are not merely psychological constructs but have measurable academic consequences.
Predominantly White Institutions and Identity
Research on the specific experience of racially minoritized students at predominantly white institutions has been extensive. Solorzano, Ceja, and Yosso (2000) documented through focus groups that Black students at three elite universities experienced racial microaggressions that undermined their sense of academic belonging and led to the development of counterspaces — informal support networks that buffered the negative effects of the majority environment. While this work focused on race specifically, it established methodological and theoretical approaches to studying institutional identity negotiation that inform the present study. Nuñez (2009) examined the compound intersection of first-generation status and racial identity at Hispanic-Serving Institutions and found that navigating multiple marginalized identities simultaneously created distinctive patterns of belonging and academic engagement.
Gap
While existing research has established that first-generation students experience lower belonging and that cultural mismatch has measurable consequences, and while research on racially minoritized students at PWIs has documented the identity negotiation process, no study has examined how first-generation students — particularly those who are also racially minoritized — describe the phenomenological experience of constructing academic identity at a predominantly white institution during the critical first year. Existing quantitative studies measure belonging as an outcome but do not capture the lived experience of how students actively construct, resist, and negotiate academic identity on a day-to-day basis. This study fills that gap through phenomenological interviews.
Methodology
Research Design
This study used a phenomenological qualitative design following the approach described by Moustakas (1994), which focuses on capturing the lived experience of a phenomenon from the perspective of those who have experienced it. A phenomenological approach was chosen over a survey design or case study because the research question explicitly asks how students describe their experience — a question that requires access to subjective meaning-making processes that quantitative instruments cannot capture (Creswell & Poth, 2018). The phenomenological approach is specifically appropriate for studying identity construction because identity is inherently a process of meaning-making rather than a measurable outcome variable.
Participants
Participants were eight first-generation college students in their first year at one predominantly white public university in the Midwest. Purposive sampling was used to select participants representing variation in racial and ethnic identity (four participants identified as students of color and four as white), gender (five women and three men), and academic major (STEM and humanities). All participants met the definition of first-generation — neither parent had completed a four-year degree. Recruitment was conducted through the university's first-generation student support office. Informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study was approved by the university IRB.
Data Collection
Semi-structured interviews were conducted with each participant lasting 45 to 75 minutes. An interview protocol was developed based on prior literature and reviewed by a faculty mentor. Core interview questions focused on: how participants described their sense of belonging in academic spaces, specific moments when they felt like they did or did not belong, how they negotiated between their home identity and their student identity, and what strategies they used to maintain academic engagement. Interviews were conducted in person in a private campus space. All interviews were audio recorded with participant consent and transcribed verbatim.
Data Analysis
Transcripts were analyzed using Moustakas's (1994) modified van Kaam method, which involves horizonalization — treating each statement as equally significant — followed by clustering statements into themes, then constructing a textural description of each participant's experience, then a structural description, and finally a composite description representing the essence of the phenomenon across all participants. Initial codes were generated inductively from the data. A faculty mentor reviewed coding decisions for one transcript to provide an external check on interpretation. All participant names were replaced with pseudonyms.
Results
Analysis of the eight interviews produced three overarching themes describing how first-generation students experienced academic identity construction at the PWI.
Theme 1 — The Imposter Tax: All eight participants described a persistent sense of not legitimately belonging in academic spaces. Participants described this as a cognitive and emotional burden that required ongoing management alongside their academic work. Representative quote from Maya, a first-year biology student: "Every time I raise my hand in class I have this split second where I think — do I actually know this, or am I just about to embarrass myself in front of everyone? And then I have to convince myself that I do know, that I belong here. And that's exhausting. It happens every single class." This imposter experience was described as particularly acute in academic interactions with professors, during office hours visits, and in seminar-style classes requiring active verbal participation.
Theme 2 — Code Switching as Labor: Seven of eight participants described actively managing two distinct identities — a home identity and a student identity — and the cognitive and emotional labor of switching between them. Participants described changing vocabulary, communication style, topics of conversation, and even physical presentation depending on whether they were in academic or home contexts. Participant Jordan described: "At home I talk one way. At school I talk a different way. And it's not like I'm being fake — it's like survival. But it's tiring. My parents don't really get what I'm doing here and my professors don't really get where I'm coming from. So I'm always translating." This code switching was described as more exhausting by participants who were also racially minoritized, consistent with Nuñez's (2009) finding on compounded identity navigation.
Theme 3 — Constructed Legitimacy through Performance: Six of eight participants described developing specific strategies to construct and project academic legitimacy when their internal sense of belonging was low. These strategies included excessive preparation, strategic visibility in class, building relationships with professors through office hours even when anxiety-provoking, and forming peer study groups with other first-generation students. Participant Alex described: "I prepare way more than anyone else in my class. I have to. Because if I walk in unprepared and I fail, I confirmed what everyone already suspects. But if I walk in overprepared and I succeed, maybe I get to stay a little longer." This performance of legitimacy was described as both adaptive and exhausting, providing short-term belonging while reinforcing the underlying sense that belonging was conditional and earned rather than inherent.
Discussion
These three themes — the imposter tax, code switching as labor, and constructed legitimacy through performance — together describe a coherent phenomenological structure in which first-generation students experience academic identity as something that must be actively and continuously constructed rather than something they are entitled to. This structure extends the existing literature in important ways. The imposter experience documented here is consistent with Ostrove and Long's (2007) quantitative finding that first-generation students report lower belonging, but the phenomenological data reveals that this lower belonging is not a static state but an active daily management process that consumes cognitive and emotional resources. The code switching finding aligns with Stephens et al. (2012) on cultural mismatch but reveals the first-person experience of that mismatch as labor rather than merely disadvantage. The constructed legitimacy theme adds a novel contribution — the adaptive strategies participants developed are not captured in existing quantitative measures of belonging or engagement, suggesting that current measurement tools may underestimate the active agency first-generation students bring to identity negotiation.
The finding that code switching was particularly exhausting for racially minoritized first-generation students is consistent with Nuñez (2009) and Solorzano et al. (2000) but adds phenomenological depth to the experience of compound identity navigation that those studies documented structurally.
Limitations
This study has several limitations. The sample of eight participants from one institution limits generalizability. The study examined only first-year students and cannot speak to how academic identity evolves over subsequent years. Self-report data through interviews may be affected by social desirability — participants may have described their experiences in ways they believed were expected rather than fully authentic. The single-institution design means that the specific institutional context of this PWI may produce findings that do not transfer to different types of institutions.
Implications
These findings have implications for university administrators and first-year program designers. First-generation student support programs should address the cognitive and emotional labor of identity construction, not only academic skill development. Future research should examine how these patterns evolve across multiple years and across different institutional types.
Conclusion
This phenomenological study found that first-generation college students at a predominantly white institution experience academic identity construction as a continuous active labor process characterized by imposter experience, code switching between home and academic identities, and deliberate performance of legitimacy. These findings extend existing quantitative research on first-generation belonging by revealing the lived phenomenological structure of identity negotiation that underlies the statistical patterns. Institutions serving first-generation students should design support programs that acknowledge and address the labor of identity construction rather than focusing exclusively on academic skill deficits.
