The Impact of Urban Green Space Proximity on Mental Health Outcomes in Low-Income Communities
AP Research
April 2025
Word Count: 3,891
Introduction
Urban green spaces including parks, community gardens, and tree-lined streets have been increasingly recognized as important determinants of mental health in urban populations. Research consistently shows that access to nature reduces stress, anxiety, and depression while improving mood, cognitive function, and social cohesion (Bratman et al., 2019). However access to green space is not equally distributed across urban areas. Low-income communities are disproportionately likely to have reduced access to quality green space compared to higher-income neighborhoods, a phenomenon sometimes referred to as environmental inequity (Rigolon, 2016). This research investigates the following question: To what extent does proximity to urban green space predict self-reported mental health outcomes in residents of low-income urban neighborhoods in Columbus, Ohio?
This question matters because urban planning and public health policy decisions about green space investment are rarely guided by community-specific mental health data. Understanding the relationship between green space proximity and mental health in low-income Columbus neighborhoods could provide evidence for targeted green space investment decisions that prioritize mental health equity.
Literature Review
Green Space and Mental Health
The relationship between green space and mental health is supported by multiple theoretical frameworks and empirical studies. Kaplan and Kaplan (1989) developed Attention Restoration Theory proposing that natural environments restore directed attention capacity depleted by urban stimulation, reducing cognitive fatigue and improving mood. Ulrich et al. (1991) provided early empirical support through an experimental study showing that viewing natural scenes produced faster physiological recovery from stress than viewing urban scenes. More recently Bratman et al. (2019) conducted a systematic review of 143 studies and found consistent positive associations between nature exposure and mental health outcomes including reduced anxiety, depression, and stress across diverse populations and methodologies. Twohig-Bennett and Jones (2018) conducted a meta-analysis of 143 studies and found that living in greener areas was associated with significantly reduced risk of type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, all-cause mortality, and improved mental health outcomes.
Green Space Equity
Access to green space is not equally distributed across urban areas. Rigolon (2016) conducted a systematic review of 45 studies examining the distribution of parks and green spaces across socioeconomic and racial lines in cities and found consistent evidence of environmental inequity — lower-income and minority communities have systematically less access to quality green space than higher-income and predominantly white communities. Wolch, Byrne, and Newell (2014) examined green space gentrification in Los Angeles and found that green space improvements in low-income neighborhoods often led to displacement of existing residents, suggesting that green space investment without housing policy coordination can paradoxically harm the communities it is intended to benefit.
Mental Health in Low-Income Communities
Low-income communities face elevated rates of mental health problems including depression and anxiety compared to higher-income populations, attributable to a complex mix of economic stress, reduced access to healthcare, trauma exposure, and environmental factors (Alegria et al., 2018). Gong et al. (2016) found that the mental health benefits of green space were stronger in lower-income populations than in higher-income populations, suggesting that green space access may be particularly important as a mental health resource in communities with fewer alternatives.
Gap
While existing research establishes associations between green space and mental health across diverse populations and documents green space inequity in urban areas, no study has examined the specific relationship between green space proximity and mental health outcomes in low-income neighborhoods in Columbus, Ohio. Columbus has experienced rapid population growth and significant urban development over the past decade, with documented disparities in green space distribution across neighborhoods. Understanding the local relationship between green space and mental health in this specific context could provide directly actionable evidence for Columbus urban planners and public health officials. This study addresses that gap.
Methodology
Research Design
This study was designed as a quantitative correlational study examining the relationship between residential proximity to urban green space and self-reported mental health outcomes among adult residents of low-income neighborhoods in Columbus, Ohio. The method involved two main data collection components: geographic measurement of green space proximity using publicly available GIS data from the Columbus Department of Recreation and Parks, and mental health outcome measurement using a validated survey instrument administered to community residents.
Geographic Analysis
Green space proximity was operationalized as the straight-line distance in meters from each participant's residential address to the nearest green space of at least one acre in size. Distance was calculated using ArcGIS software applied to the Columbus Recreation and Parks GIS dataset, which includes all public parks, community gardens, and designated green spaces within city limits. Participants were categorized into three proximity groups: high proximity (less than 200 meters), medium proximity (200 to 500 meters), and low proximity (greater than 500 meters) based on prior research suggesting that green spaces within walking distance produce stronger mental health benefits than those requiring transportation (Bratman et al., 2019).
Survey Instrument
Mental health outcomes were measured using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) for depression screening and the Generalized Anxiety Disorder scale-7 (GAD-7) for anxiety screening. Both instruments are widely validated across diverse populations including low-income urban communities and produce reliable scores (Kroenke et al., 2001; Spitzer et al., 2006). The survey also collected demographic information including age, gender, race, ethnicity, employment status, household income, and length of residence in the neighborhood.
Planned Recruitment and Data Collection
Surveys were to be distributed to adult residents of five low-income Columbus neighborhoods identified by census tract income data as being in the lowest quartile of household income in Franklin County. Recruitment was planned through community health centers, food banks, and community organizations in each neighborhood. The target sample size was 150 participants based on a power analysis indicating that this sample size would provide 80% power to detect a medium effect size (r = 0.30) at p less than 0.05.
However due to unforeseen challenges in obtaining community organization partnerships and IRB approval for research involving community health center patients, data collection was not completed during the research period. Surveys were distributed to approximately 40 individuals at one community event but the response rate was insufficient and the data collected was not representative of the target population. As a result no analysis of the primary research question was conducted.
Preliminary Observations
While formal data analysis was not conducted, informal observations during the one community event where surveys were distributed suggested that residents were aware of and concerned about green space access in their neighborhoods. Several participants mentioned specific parks they valued and noted challenges with safety, maintenance, and transportation access that limited their actual use of nearby green spaces even when they lived in close proximity. These informal observations, while not constituting formal data, suggest that the relationship between green space proximity and mental health may be moderated by green space quality and usability rather than proximity alone.
Limitations
The primary limitation of this study is that the planned data collection was not completed. Without a sufficient and representative sample the primary research question cannot be answered. The causes of this limitation include challenges in establishing community partnerships, IRB approval delays for research involving healthcare settings, and time constraints inherent to a year-long research project. Future research should begin the IRB approval and community partnership process significantly earlier in the research timeline to allow adequate time for data collection.
Implications
Despite the failure to complete data collection this study makes a contribution by clearly articulating a research design that could be implemented by future researchers. The geographic analysis framework using ArcGIS and the validated PHQ-9 and GAD-7 instruments provide a replicable template for future community-based research on green space and mental health in Columbus. Public health researchers interested in green space equity in Columbus should consider this design as a starting point for future work.
Conclusion
This study was designed to examine the relationship between urban green space proximity and mental health outcomes in low-income Columbus neighborhoods but was unable to complete data collection due to logistical and approval challenges. The research design is documented in sufficient detail for replication by future researchers. Informal observations suggest that green space quality and usability may be important moderators of any proximity-mental health relationship. Future research should prioritize early establishment of community partnerships and IRB approval to allow adequate time for data collection and analysis.
