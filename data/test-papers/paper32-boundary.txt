The Effect of pH on the Rate of Enzyme Activity in Amylase Extracted from Human Saliva
AP Research
April 2025
Word Count: 3,978
Introduction
Enzymes are biological catalysts that dramatically accelerate the rate of biochemical reactions by lowering the activation energy required for those reactions to occur. Each enzyme has an optimal pH range at which its activity is maximized, and deviations from this optimal range cause conformational changes in the enzyme's active site that reduce its catalytic efficiency. Amylase is an enzyme found in human saliva and pancreatic secretions that catalyzes the hydrolysis of starch into simpler sugars including maltose and glucose. Salivary amylase (alpha-amylase, EC 3.2.1.1) has an optimal pH of approximately 6.7 to 7.0 which corresponds to the normal pH range of human saliva (Butterworth et al., 2011). Understanding the pH sensitivity of amylase activity is important not only as a model for understanding enzyme function generally but also has practical implications for food science, digestion research, and the development of industrial applications of amylase in food processing. This research investigates the following question: To what extent does variation in pH from 4.0 to 9.0 affect the rate of amylase activity as measured by starch hydrolysis rate using the iodine color change method?
Literature Review
Enzyme Structure and pH Sensitivity
The relationship between enzyme activity and pH has been studied extensively since the early twentieth century. Michaelis and Menten (1913) developed the foundational kinetic model of enzyme activity that established the quantitative relationship between substrate concentration and reaction rate. Their model, which describes enzyme activity through the parameters Vmax (maximum reaction velocity) and Km (Michaelis constant), has been extended to describe how pH affects both parameters. Lehninger et al. (2017) described in the standard biochemistry reference text that pH affects enzyme activity through two primary mechanisms: direct effects on the ionization state of amino acid residues in the active site that are required for catalysis, and indirect effects on the overall three-dimensional conformation of the enzyme through changes in electrostatic interactions. Both mechanisms produce bell-shaped pH-activity curves with optima at the pH where the critical ionizable residues are in their catalytically active states.
Amylase Specifically
Amylase has been studied extensively as a model enzyme for understanding pH effects. Butterworth et al. (2011) conducted a comprehensive review of salivary amylase and found that human salivary amylase shows a sharp optimum at pH 6.9 with approximately 50 percent reduction in activity at pH 5.5 and pH 8.5. Ramasubbu et al. (1996) used X-ray crystallography to identify the specific active site residues of human salivary amylase and found that three residues — Asp197, Glu233, and Asp300 — are critical for catalysis, with their ionization states being pH-dependent. Understanding the structural basis of pH sensitivity in amylase provides a mechanistic explanation for the empirically observed pH-activity relationship.
Educational Applications
The pH-activity relationship in amylase has been widely used as a model experiment in biology education. Alias et al. (2016) examined the effectiveness of amylase pH experiments in developing students' understanding of enzyme function and found that hands-on experiments produced better conceptual understanding than demonstrations alone. However most educational amylase experiments use commercial amylase preparations rather than amylase extracted directly from human saliva, which differs in concentration, purity, and composition from commercial preparations and may produce different results.
Gap
While the pH-activity relationship in amylase is well documented in the biochemistry literature, most studies have either used commercial amylase preparations or examined a narrow pH range. No study has compared the pH-activity curve of amylase extracted directly from human saliva across the full pH range from 4.0 to 9.0 using the iodine color change assay in an educational laboratory setting to determine whether the well-documented pH optimum is reproduced under these conditions. This study fills that gap.
Methodology
Research Design
This study used a controlled experimental design examining amylase activity at six pH levels: 4.0, 5.0, 6.0, 7.0, 8.0, and 9.0. Each pH condition was tested in triplicate for a total of 18 trials. The independent variable was buffer pH, the dependent variable was the time for the iodine color change to disappear (indicating complete starch hydrolysis), and the controlled variables were amylase concentration, starch concentration, reaction temperature (37°C water bath to simulate body temperature), and reaction volume.
Materials
Human salivary amylase was obtained by having the researcher produce unstimulated saliva and diluting it 1:10 with distilled water. The same saliva preparation was used for all trials to control for individual variation in amylase concentration. Starch solution was prepared at 1% concentration. Buffer solutions at each pH were prepared using citrate-phosphate buffer (pH 4.0 to 7.0) and Tris-HCl buffer (pH 7.0 to 9.0) following standard laboratory protocols. Iodine solution (Lugol's solution) was prepared at standard concentration. All solutions were prepared fresh on the day of each experiment.
Procedure
For each trial 1 mL of diluted saliva was combined with 1 mL of the appropriate pH buffer and incubated for 2 minutes at 37°C to equilibrate. One mL of 1% starch solution was then added and the timer started. Every 30 seconds one drop of the reaction mixture was placed on a spot plate containing one drop of Lugol's solution and the color was recorded. The endpoint was defined as the first time point at which the iodine no longer turned blue-black indicating complete starch hydrolysis. The reaction time to endpoint was recorded in seconds. If no endpoint was reached within 30 minutes the trial was recorded as no reaction. All trials were conducted in a 37°C water bath maintained throughout the experiment.
Data Analysis
Mean reaction times for each pH condition were calculated across the three replicates. A one-way ANOVA examined whether pH condition significantly affected reaction time. Tukey HSD post-hoc comparisons identified which specific pH conditions differed significantly. Effect size was calculated using eta squared. All analyses were conducted in SPSS version 28. Significance threshold was set at p less than 0.05.

Tukey HSD post-hoc comparisons showed that pH 7.0 produced significantly faster reaction times than all other pH conditions (all p less than .05). pH 6.0 produced significantly faster times than pH 5.0, 8.0, and 9.0 (all p less than .05) but did not significantly differ from pH 8.0 (p = .18). pH 4.0 (no reaction) was excluded from statistical analysis.

Discussion
These results demonstrate a clear bell-shaped pH-activity relationship for human salivary amylase with optimal activity (fastest reaction time) at pH 7.0 and progressively reduced activity at both lower and higher pH values. The finding of an optimum between pH 6.0 and 7.0 is consistent with Butterworth et al.'s (2011) review reporting a pH optimum of approximately 6.9 for human salivary amylase, confirming that the iodine color change assay with human saliva reproduced the well-established pH-activity relationship under educational laboratory conditions.
The complete absence of reaction at pH 4.0 is consistent with strong acid denaturation of amylase at low pH values. The asymmetric shape of the curve — with slower reduction in activity at high pH compared to low pH — is consistent with the ionization state model proposed by Lehninger et al. (2017), where acidic conditions affect the active site residues more severely than basic conditions in this pH range.
Limitations
The primary limitations of using human saliva are variability in amylase concentration between samples and the inability to precisely quantify enzyme concentration. While the same saliva preparation was used for all trials within a single experimental session, the concentration may have differed between experimental sessions. The iodine endpoint method provides a categorical rather than continuous measure of reaction progress, potentially introducing measurement error. Temperature was maintained at 37°C using a water bath but small fluctuations may have occurred. The triplicate design with three replicates per condition provides limited statistical power for detecting small differences between conditions.
Implications
These findings are relevant for biology teachers designing inquiry-based enzyme laboratory experiences and for curriculum developers seeking accessible experiments that reproduce well-established biochemical relationships. The confirmation that human saliva produces a reproducible pH-activity curve using simple equipment suggests that this experiment is educationally valuable for demonstrating enzyme function in secondary school settings. Future research should quantify amylase concentration using Bradford or BCA assays to allow normalization of activity across conditions and sessions.
Conclusion
This controlled experiment found a clear bell-shaped pH-activity relationship for human salivary amylase with optimal activity at pH 7.0, complete loss of activity at pH 4.0, and progressive reduction in activity at both lower and higher pH values. The one-way ANOVA confirmed a statistically significant effect of pH on reaction time with a very large effect size (eta squared = .95). These results are consistent with the established biochemistry literature on amylase pH sensitivity and confirm that the iodine color change assay with human salivary amylase reproduces the expected pH-activity relationship under educational laboratory conditions.
