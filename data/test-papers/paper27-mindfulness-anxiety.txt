The Effect of Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction on Test Anxiety in High School Students
AP Research
April 2025
Word Count: 1,806
Introduction
Test anxiety is one of the most prevalent forms of anxiety among high school students, affecting an estimated 25 to 40 percent of the student population and producing measurable negative effects on academic performance independent of students' actual knowledge and preparation (von der Embse et al., 2018). The cognitive interference model of test anxiety proposes that anxious students experience intrusive worries and negative self-evaluative thoughts during testing that compete with cognitive resources needed for task performance, producing an anxiety-performance gap in which students perform below their actual capability (Eysenck et al., 2007). Mindfulness-based stress reduction was developed by Jon Kabat-Zinn and has been shown to be effective for reducing anxiety in adult clinical populations, with a meta-analytic effect size of approximately d = 0.55 (Hofmann et al., 2010). Recent research has begun examining whether mindfulness interventions can reduce test anxiety specifically in student populations, with promising preliminary findings. However most existing studies have examined college students rather than high school students, and most have used self-report measures of anxiety rather than combining self-report with performance-based measures that assess whether anxiety reduction translates to actual performance improvements. This research investigates the following question: To what extent does participation in a four-week mindfulness-based stress reduction program reduce self-reported test anxiety and improve performance on a standardized vocabulary test in high school juniors and seniors?
Literature Review
Test Anxiety Research
Test anxiety has been studied extensively since the 1950s. Zeidner (1998) provided a comprehensive review of test anxiety research across five decades and identified consistent negative relationships between test anxiety and academic performance across cultures, age groups, and subject areas. Von der Embse et al. (2018) conducted a meta-analysis of 238 studies on test anxiety and academic performance and found a consistent negative correlation of approximately r = −0.22, confirming the anxiety-performance relationship across a wide range of academic contexts. Putwain and Daly (2014) examined test anxiety specifically in high school students in the context of high-stakes examinations and found that test anxiety in this population was particularly consequential because high school examinations often have direct consequences for university admission and future opportunities.
Mindfulness Interventions in Educational Settings
Research on mindfulness interventions in school settings has grown substantially over the past decade. Zenner et al. (2014) conducted a meta-analysis of school-based mindfulness interventions and found a significant overall effect on stress and anxiety reduction (d = 0.40) but noted that effects on academic performance were smaller and less consistent. Klingbeil et al. (2017) conducted a larger meta-analysis of 76 school-based mindfulness studies and found significant effects on cognitive outcomes (d = 0.30) and socioemotional outcomes (d = 0.22). Both meta-analyses found smaller effects in school settings compared to clinical settings, which is expected given the less intensive implementation typical of school programs.
Test Anxiety and Mindfulness
Research specifically examining the effect of mindfulness on test anxiety is more limited. Dundas et al. (2016) conducted a randomized trial of a brief mindfulness intervention for university students with high test anxiety and found significant reductions in test anxiety scores compared to a waitlist control. Mrazek et al. (2013) found that a brief mindfulness training improved reading comprehension scores and working memory capacity in undergraduate students, suggesting that mindfulness may improve the cognitive resources undermined by test anxiety. However no study has examined a mindfulness intervention specifically designed to address test anxiety in high school students using both self-report and performance-based outcome measures simultaneously.
Gap
There is a gap in the research because most mindfulness and test anxiety studies have focused on college students rather than high school students. This study fills that gap by examining a mindfulness intervention for test anxiety in high school juniors and seniors using both self-report and performance measures.
Methodology
Research Design
This study used a pre-post single group design with a four-week mindfulness-based stress reduction program as the intervention. Participants completed measures of test anxiety and a standardized vocabulary test before and after the intervention. This design was chosen for feasibility given the constraints of a high school research project. A randomized controlled design would have been more appropriate for establishing causal inference but was not feasible given the difficulty of recruiting a control group.
Participants
The target sample was 20 to 25 high school juniors and seniors who self-reported high test anxiety. Recruitment was conducted through homeroom teachers and school counselors who distributed opt-in participation forms. The target sample of 20 to 25 participants was not reached. Despite multiple rounds of recruitment and the support of school counselors only two students agreed to participate in the study. Both participants completed the full four-week program and all assessments.
The researcher believes the low participation rate reflected several factors including the time commitment required (four weekly sessions of 45 minutes each plus daily home practice), concerns about missing other activities, and possible stigma associated with admitting to test anxiety among peers.
Mindfulness Intervention
The four-week mindfulness-based stress reduction program was adapted from the standard MBSR curriculum for adolescent populations following the protocol described by Biegel et al. (2009). Sessions covered: Week 1 — Introduction to mindfulness and body scan; Week 2 — Mindful breathing and attention to thoughts; Week 3 — Mindful movement and walking meditation; Week 4 — Applying mindfulness to test-taking situations. Sessions were conducted in a quiet room after school with both participants present simultaneously. All sessions were led by the researcher who had completed a 40-hour mindfulness facilitator training course.
Measures
Test anxiety was measured using the Westside Test Anxiety Scale (Driscoll, 2007), a validated 10-item measure of test anxiety with established reliability (Cronbach's alpha = 0.86). Scores range from 1 to 5 with higher scores indicating greater test anxiety. Academic performance was measured using a 50-item standardized vocabulary test developed from the school's SAT preparation materials. The same test was administered before and after the intervention.
Results
With a sample size of two participants no statistical analysis was possible or meaningful. The results reported below are descriptive only and should be interpreted with extreme caution as they cannot support any generalizable conclusions.
Participant 1 (junior, female) showed a decrease in test anxiety score from 3.8 to 2.9 (reduction of 0.9 points) and an increase in vocabulary test score from 36 to 41 out of 50.
Participant 2 (senior, male) showed a decrease in test anxiety score from 4.1 to 3.4 (reduction of 0.7 points) and an increase in vocabulary test score from 29 to 38 out of 50.
Both participants reported finding the mindfulness program helpful and said they planned to continue practicing mindfulness techniques independently. Participant 1 noted that the body scan exercise was particularly helpful before tests and Participant 2 found mindful breathing useful during moments of test panic.
Discussion
The descriptive results from the two participants suggest that both showed reductions in self-reported test anxiety and improvements in vocabulary test performance following the mindfulness intervention. These individual-level changes are consistent with the existing literature on mindfulness and test anxiety and with the cognitive interference model of test anxiety. However with only two participants no conclusions can be drawn about the effectiveness of the intervention for high school students with test anxiety generally. The results are illustrative only.
The low recruitment rate is itself a meaningful finding that future researchers should consider. The barriers to participation identified by the researcher — time commitment, competing activities, and possible stigma — represent real implementation challenges that any school-based mindfulness program would face.
Limitations
The primary limitation is the sample size of two participants which makes any statistical analysis impossible and any conclusion about program effectiveness unwarranted. The pre-post design without a control group would have limited causal inference even with a larger sample. The vocabulary test was not an independent validated instrument. The researcher served as both the mindfulness facilitator and the researcher which creates a dual-role concern. The mindfulness sessions were attended by both participants simultaneously which means participants were not independent.
Implications
Despite the severe methodological limitations this study provides a proof-of-concept protocol that future researchers could use with a larger and more representative sample. School counselors interested in mindfulness-based approaches to test anxiety should note that recruitment for voluntary after-school programs may be more challenging than expected and should consider whether embedding mindfulness instruction in the regular school day would reduce participation barriers.
Conclusion
This study examined the effect of a four-week mindfulness-based stress reduction program on test anxiety and vocabulary performance in high school juniors and seniors but was limited by a final sample of only two participants due to recruitment failure. Both participants showed individual-level reductions in test anxiety and improvements in vocabulary performance following the intervention but no statistical analysis was possible and no conclusions about program effectiveness can be drawn. The study provides a documented protocol for future research and highlights recruitment challenges that future researchers should address.
