How Do Popular Science Videos on YouTube Represent Climate Change? A Content Analysis
AP Research
April 2025
Word Count: 3,621
Introduction
Climate change is one of the most pressing scientific and political issues of the twenty-first century. Public understanding of climate change is critical for democratic decision-making about policy responses, individual behavior change, and support for scientific consensus. Research consistently shows that informal science learning through media plays an important role in shaping public scientific understanding, particularly for complex topics like climate change that require synthesis of evidence across multiple disciplines (Kahle et al., 2016). YouTube has become one of the most significant platforms for informal science communication, with science-focused channels reaching tens of millions of subscribers and individual videos accumulating hundreds of millions of views. Despite the scale of this influence, relatively little systematic research has examined how popular YouTube science channels represent climate change — specifically whether they accurately portray scientific consensus, acknowledge uncertainty appropriately, and connect scientific evidence to policy implications. This paper investigates the following research question: How do the top five most-subscribed science YouTube channels represent climate change in their most-viewed climate-related videos?
Literature Review
Science Communication and Media
Science communication research has examined how various media represent scientific topics. Nisbet and Mooney (2007) argued that framing is central to science communication — the same scientific findings can produce very different public responses depending on whether they are framed as a matter of scientific uncertainty, economic threat, moral obligation, or political conflict. Scheufele (2014) extended this framework to digital media contexts, arguing that online science communication operates through different framing dynamics than traditional broadcast media due to algorithmically driven engagement incentives. Both studies focus on framing as the key variable in science communication quality but do not examine YouTube specifically.
YouTube and Science Communication
A small body of research has examined science communication specifically on YouTube. Welbourne and Grant (2016) conducted a content analysis of science YouTube channels and found that videos from scientists and science institutions received higher engagement metrics than those from journalists or entertainment creators, suggesting that authenticity and expertise are valued by audiences. Brossard and Scheufele (2013) examined social media science communication more broadly and found that comment sections on science content consistently showed more polarized and less scientifically accurate discourse than the main content, raising questions about the net effect of YouTube science content on public understanding.
Climate Change Communication
Climate change communication has been studied extensively. Leiserowitz et al. (2010) documented six distinct segments of American public opinion about climate change ranging from alarmed to dismissive, arguing that communication strategies must be tailored to different audience segments. Boykoff and Boykoff (2004) documented the phenomenon of false balance in newspaper coverage of climate change — journalists' professional norm of presenting both sides led to systematic overrepresentation of climate skeptic viewpoints relative to the scientific consensus. This false balance phenomenon has been studied primarily in traditional media.
Gap
There is a gap in the research regarding how YouTube science channels specifically represent climate change. While research on traditional media has documented false balance and framing effects, and while some research has examined general YouTube science communication, no study has systematically examined the representation of climate change specifically across the most popular YouTube science channels. This study addresses that gap.
Methodology
This study used a content analysis methodology to examine how climate change is represented in videos from the top five most-subscribed science YouTube channels. Content analysis is appropriate for this research question because it provides a systematic, replicable method for characterizing patterns in media content (Krippendorff, 2004).
The five channels selected for analysis were Kurzgesagt — In a Nutshell, SciShow, Veritasium, Mark Rober, and Vsauce based on subscriber count as of January 2025. For each channel, the single most-viewed video containing the keywords "climate change" or "global warming" in the title or description was selected for analysis.
Each video was analyzed using a coding scheme developed by the researcher. The coding scheme included six dimensions: accuracy of scientific consensus representation (rated accurate, partially accurate, or inaccurate), acknowledgment of uncertainty (present or absent), economic framing (present or absent), moral or ethical framing (present or absent), policy connection (present or absent), and emotional appeal (present or absent). The coding scheme was developed based on the framing literature reviewed above.

Analysis of the five videos revealed the following patterns. All five videos accurately represented the scientific consensus that climate change is primarily human-caused and poses significant risks. Two of the five videos acknowledged scientific uncertainty — Kurzgesagt and Veritasium — while the other three presented climate science without explicit discussion of the uncertainties in projections. Economic framing was present in two videos (SciShow and Vsauce), moral or ethical framing was present in three videos (Kurzgesagt, Veritasium, and SciShow), policy connections were made explicit in two videos (SciShow and Veritasium), and emotional appeals were present in all five videos to varying degrees.
The Kurzgesagt video showed the most sophisticated representation, acknowledging uncertainty, using moral framing, and connecting science to individual action without oversimplifying the policy landscape. The Vsauce video was most entertainment-focused and showed the least explicit policy connection. Mark Rober's climate-related video was primarily focused on a specific environmental project rather than climate change explanation and was the most divergent from the other four in approach.
Discussion
These results suggest that the most popular YouTube science channels generally represent climate change accurately in terms of scientific consensus but vary substantially in how they handle uncertainty and policy connection. The absence of false balance documented by Boykoff and Boykoff (2004) in traditional media is notable — none of the five videos gave significant platform to climate skeptic viewpoints. This may reflect YouTube's different audience dynamics compared to mainstream journalism. The variation in uncertainty acknowledgment is consistent with Nisbet and Mooney's (2007) framing theory — channels that acknowledge uncertainty may be using different framing strategies than those that present a more certain picture.
The small sample of five videos limits the conclusions that can be drawn. These results cannot be generalized to YouTube science communication as a whole or even to these channels' full body of content.
Limitations
The primary limitation is the small sample size of five videos. This is insufficient for statistical analysis and the results can only be descriptive. The coding scheme was developed by the researcher without validation against existing instruments. Inter-rater reliability was not established — only one coder analyzed all videos. The selection of only the most-viewed video per channel may not represent each channel's typical approach.
Implications
These findings are relevant to science educators and science communication researchers. YouTube science content appears to avoid the false balance problem that has characterized traditional media coverage of climate change. Future research should examine larger samples across more channels and establish validated coding instruments.
Conclusion
This content analysis of climate change representation in five popular YouTube science videos found that all five accurately represented scientific consensus, that uncertainty acknowledgment varied across channels, and that framing strategies differed substantially. These findings suggest that popular YouTube science communication may serve as a more accurate source of climate change information than traditional media for some audiences. The small sample size limits generalizability and future research should examine larger and more diverse samples.
