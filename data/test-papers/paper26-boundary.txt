The Effect of Bilingual Education Programs on English Language Acquisition and Academic Identity in Elementary English Language Learners
AP Research
April 2025
Word Count: 4,387
Introduction
The United States is home to approximately 5 million English Language Learners enrolled in public schools, representing roughly 10 percent of the total student population (National Center for Education Statistics, 2023). The academic achievement gap between English Language Learners and native English speakers remains substantial, with ELL students scoring significantly below proficiency benchmarks on national assessments in both reading and mathematics (NAEP, 2022). At the heart of educational policy debates about ELL instruction is a fundamental question about the role of students' home languages in the process of acquiring English academic proficiency. Two dominant instructional models have emerged as the primary alternatives for ELL instruction: English-only immersion programs that maximize exposure to English by conducting all instruction in English from the beginning, and bilingual education programs that use students' home language as a medium of instruction while gradually transitioning to English. Despite decades of policy debate, the research evidence comparing these two approaches on both English language acquisition outcomes and students' sense of academic identity has produced inconsistent findings, and no study has simultaneously examined both academic language outcomes and academic identity formation in elementary ELL students in the specific context of suburban Midwest schools where ELL populations have grown rapidly in recent years. This research investigates the following question: How do elementary English Language Learners in bilingual education programs compare to those in English-only immersion programs in English language acquisition outcomes and self-reported academic identity at the end of second grade?
This question matters because school districts in the Midwest are making consequential decisions about ELL program design without access to locally relevant evidence. Understanding both academic and identity outcomes simultaneously could inform more holistic program design.
Literature Review
Bilingual Education and Language Acquisition
Research on the academic effects of bilingual education has produced a complex and sometimes contradictory literature. Thomas and Collier (2002) conducted the largest longitudinal study of ELL program effectiveness to date, following over 700,000 ELL students across five school districts over 18 years, and found that students in two-way bilingual immersion programs consistently outperformed students in English-only programs on English language assessments by fourth through seventh grade, even though bilingual students showed slower initial English acquisition in kindergarten and first grade. Rolstad, Mahoney, and Glass (2005) conducted a meta-analysis of 17 experimental and quasi-experimental studies of bilingual education effects and found a significant overall advantage for bilingual education on English language outcomes, with a mean effect size of d = 0.23. However Slavin and Cheung (2005) conducted a competing meta-analysis and found smaller and less consistent effects, concluding that the evidence for bilingual education was suggestive but not conclusive due to methodological limitations in most studies.
Academic Identity in English Language Learners
Academic identity — the degree to which students see themselves as competent and belonging in academic spaces — has been identified as a significant predictor of long-term academic persistence independent of measured ability (Ostrove & Long, 2007). Research specifically examining academic identity in ELL students is more limited. Cummins (2001) developed the theoretical framework of identity investment, arguing that students invest in learning activities to the degree that those activities affirm and extend their identities. When school instruction requires students to abandon their home language and cultural identity, Cummins argues, it may undermine identity investment and academic engagement even when it produces short-term language gains. Jimenez, Garcia, and Pearson (1996) examined the reading strategies of bilingual students and found that successful bilingual readers actively used their home language as a cognitive resource during English reading, suggesting that home language proficiency supports rather than undermines English academic development when it is allowed to function in the academic context.
Midwest ELL Context
Research on ELL populations in the Midwest has noted distinctive features of this context compared to the predominantly studied California and Texas ELL populations. Gándara and Contreras (2009) documented significant variation in ELL program quality and availability across regions, noting that Midwestern states have lower rates of bilingual-certified teachers and less established bilingual program infrastructure than states with longer histories of large ELL populations. Fry (2008) documented the rapid growth of Latino ELL populations in new destination states including Ohio, Indiana, and Minnesota and found that schools in these states were less prepared to serve ELL students than schools in traditional destination states. Neither study examined the academic or identity outcomes of ELL students in Midwestern bilingual versus English-only programs.
Gap
While research has established that bilingual education produces comparable or superior English language acquisition outcomes compared to English-only immersion in large urban samples (Thomas & Collier, 2002; Rolstad et al., 2005), and while Cummins (2001) has theorized that home language instruction supports academic identity development, no study has simultaneously examined both English language acquisition outcomes and academic identity in elementary ELL students comparing bilingual and English-only programs in a Midwestern suburban school district where rapid ELL population growth has outpaced program development. The Midwest context is important because the lower availability of bilingual resources documented by Gándara and Contreras (2009) and Fry (2008) means that the program quality differences between bilingual and English-only programs in this context may differ meaningfully from those studied in California and Texas. This study addresses that specific intersection.
Methodology
Research Design
This study used a convergent mixed-methods design collecting quantitative language assessment data and qualitative academic identity interview data simultaneously and integrating them during analysis following Creswell and Plano Clark (2017). The convergent design was chosen because the research question has two distinct components — a quantitative language acquisition question and a qualitative identity question — that are theoretically connected but methodologically distinct and require different data collection approaches to answer adequately. A purely quantitative design would answer the language acquisition question but could not capture the identity dimension. A purely qualitative design could explore identity experiences but could not provide the comparative language outcome data needed to address the program effectiveness question.
Participants
Participants were 47 second-grade English Language Learners from one suburban Midwestern school district that operates both a Spanish-English two-way bilingual immersion program and an English-only immersion program within the same elementary school. Students were assigned to programs at kindergarten entry based on parental choice. Twenty-three students were enrolled in the bilingual program and twenty-four in the English-only program. All participants were Spanish-speaking students who entered kindergarten with limited English proficiency as assessed by the school district's standard ELL screening process. Informed consent was obtained from parents in both Spanish and English. The study was approved by the school district IRB.
Quantitative Strand
English language acquisition was assessed using the WIDA ACCESS assessment, a validated standardized English language proficiency assessment used by 41 states including Ohio. WIDA ACCESS scores from end-of-second-grade administration were obtained from school records with district permission. Scores range from 1.0 to 6.0 with higher scores indicating greater English proficiency. An independent samples t-test compared WIDA ACCESS composite scores between bilingual and English-only program students. Effect size was calculated using Cohen's d.
Qualitative Strand
A purposive subsample of 14 students — seven from each program — was selected for semi-structured interviews in English and Spanish based on parental language preference. Interviews lasted 15 to 25 minutes and used age-appropriate language and visual prompts to support comprehension. Interview questions focused on how students described their experiences as learners, whether they felt smart and capable at school, and how they described the relationship between their home language and their school experiences. Interviews were conducted by a bilingual researcher and transcribed verbatim. Spanish portions were translated by the researcher and independently reviewed by a second bilingual speaker for accuracy. Thematic coding followed Braun and Clarke (2006). Inter-rater reliability was established by having a second coder independently code four transcripts. Cohen's kappa was calculated at κ = 0.79, indicating acceptable agreement.
Data Integration
Quantitative results and qualitative themes were integrated through a joint display comparing language acquisition outcomes with interview-based identity themes to identify convergence, divergence, and elaboration across the two data strands.
Results
Quantitative Findings
The independent samples t-test comparing WIDA ACCESS composite scores between program types was not statistically significant: t(45) = 1.84, p = .072, d = 0.54. Bilingual program students had a mean WIDA score of 3.41 (SD = 0.68) compared to 3.09 (SD = 0.61) for English-only students. While the difference did not reach statistical significance, the medium effect size (d = 0.54) suggests a potentially meaningful advantage for bilingual program students that the current sample was insufficiently powered to detect.
Qualitative Findings
Three themes emerged from interview analysis.
Theme 1 — Language as identity resource: Bilingual program students consistently described their home language as a resource that helped them learn and as an important part of who they were as learners. Representative quote from Maria (bilingual program): "When I don't know a word in English I can think about it in Spanish first and then it comes. My teacher says that's smart." English-only students more frequently described their home language as something that interfered with their learning. Quote from Carlos (English-only): "Sometimes I think in Spanish but my teacher says I have to think in English. I try but it's hard."
Theme 2 — Academic belonging: Bilingual program students more frequently described feeling like they belonged at school and like school was a place for people like them. Theme 3 — Teacher relationships: Both program groups described positive relationships with teachers but bilingual program students more frequently described teachers who acknowledged their full linguistic identity rather than only their English proficiency.
Joint Display Analysis
The quantitative non-significant result with medium effect size was contextualized by the qualitative finding that bilingual program students described their home language as a cognitive resource and identity asset, consistent with Cummins' (2001) identity investment framework. The qualitative data suggests that even without statistically significant language outcome differences, the bilingual program may be producing meaningful differences in academic identity that could have long-term implications for engagement and persistence that the current study was not designed to capture.
Discussion
The non-significant but medium-effect-size language outcome difference is consistent with Thomas and Collier (2002) and Rolstad et al.'s (2005) finding that bilingual education effects on English outcomes are real but may not be detectable in small samples or at early time points before the cumulative bilingual advantage emerges. The qualitative identity findings provide important contextualization — even when language outcomes are not statistically different, bilingual and English-only programs appear to produce meaningfully different experiences of academic identity that are consistent with Cummins' (2001) theoretical framework and that may have long-term consequences not captured in second-grade outcomes alone.
Limitations
The most significant limitation is statistical power — with 23 and 24 students per group, the study was underpowered to detect the effect size found (d = 0.54) with conventional power of 0.80, which would require approximately 54 students per group. This means the non-significant result should be interpreted as insufficient evidence rather than evidence of no effect. The non-random assignment (parents chose programs) means that observed differences could reflect family characteristics that influence both program choice and outcomes. The single district design limits generalizability. The qualitative subsample of 14 students including second graders is small and the use of child interviews introduces developmental validity concerns about whether young children can reliably report their academic identity experiences.
Implications
These findings are relevant for school district administrators making ELL program design decisions in the Midwest, for ELL policy researchers examining program effectiveness in new destination states, and for bilingual education advocates making evidence-based arguments for program expansion. The medium effect size on language outcomes combined with the qualitative identity findings suggests that bilingual education in this context produces meaningful differences that warrant further investigation with adequately powered designs. District administrators considering program expansion should note that the qualitative findings point to academic identity as an outcome category that is not captured by standard language proficiency assessments but may have important long-term consequences. Future research should conduct a fully powered randomized or quasi-experimental study in this context and should include longitudinal outcome measurement.
Conclusion
This convergent mixed-methods study found a non-significant but medium-effect-size advantage for bilingual program students on English language acquisition outcomes at the end of second grade alongside qualitative evidence of meaningfully different academic identity experiences between program types. These findings extend existing bilingual education research to a Midwestern suburban context where rapid ELL population growth has outpaced program development and provide preliminary evidence that both language and identity outcomes should be considered in program evaluation.
