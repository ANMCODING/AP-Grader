Should Schools Implement Later Start Times? A Review of the Evidence
AP Research
April 2025
Word Count: 3,654
Introduction
School start times in the United States have been a subject of increasing debate among educators, parents, researchers, and policymakers. The American Academy of Pediatrics issued a policy statement in 2014 recommending that middle and high schools start no earlier than 8:30 AM, citing evidence that early start times conflict with adolescent circadian biology and produce negative health and academic outcomes (American Academy of Pediatrics, 2014). Despite this recommendation fewer than 20 percent of American middle and high schools start at or after 8:30 AM (Wheaton et al., 2015). The gap between what the research recommends and what schools actually practice represents a significant public health concern. This paper argues that the evidence for later school start times is compelling and that school districts should implement start time changes as a matter of policy. The purpose of this paper is to review the research evidence on school start times and to argue that this evidence justifies a policy change requiring later start times in American secondary schools.
Literature Review
Adolescent Sleep Biology
Adolescent sleep needs and patterns are shaped by biological factors that are not under voluntary control. Carskadon et al. (1993) documented that puberty produces a phase delay in the circadian rhythm, shifting the natural sleep-wake cycle approximately 2 hours later in adolescents compared to children and adults. This biological shift means that asking adolescents to wake before 7 AM is physiologically analogous to asking adults to wake before 5 AM. The National Sleep Foundation recommends 8 to 10 hours of sleep per night for teenagers, yet surveys consistently find that most adolescents sleep 6 to 7 hours on school nights (Hirshkowitz et al., 2015).
Effects of Early Start Times
Research has documented numerous negative effects of early school start times. Wahlstrom et al. (2014) conducted a large-scale study of start time changes in three districts and found that later start times were associated with improved attendance, graduation rates, and academic performance. Short et al. (2013) found that students at schools with later start times reported significantly more sleep and better daytime alertness. Wheaton et al. (2015) found that 83 percent of public middle schools and 93 percent of public high schools in the United States start before 8:30 AM, documenting the scale of the problem.
Benefits of Later Start Times
The benefits of later school start times are well documented across multiple studies. Danner and Phillips (2008) found that delaying high school start times by 30 minutes in one district was associated with significant improvements in teen motor vehicle crash rates. Owens et al. (2010) found that a 30-minute delay in start time at one boarding school was associated with significant improvements in sleep duration, daytime sleepiness, and mood. These studies consistently show that even modest delays in start times produce measurable benefits.
Policy Arguments
The case for policy change is strong. Opponents of start time changes often cite logistical concerns including transportation costs and scheduling conflicts with extracurricular activities. However Wahlstrom (2017) reviewed the evidence on implementation challenges and found that while logistical obstacles are real, most districts that have implemented start time changes have successfully navigated them and report that the health and academic benefits outweigh the logistical costs. The economic case for later start times has also been made — Hafner et al. (2017) estimated that delaying high school start times to 8:30 AM would increase GDP by approximately 83 billion dollars over a decade through improved academic performance and reduced accident rates.
Methods
For this paper I reviewed the existing research on school start times, adolescent sleep biology, and the effects of start time changes on student outcomes. I searched Google Scholar and PubMed for peer-reviewed studies on school start times published between 1990 and 2024. I identified 8 studies that directly examined the effects of start time changes on student outcomes and reviewed their findings. I then synthesized these findings to build an evidence-based argument for policy change.
Conclusion
The evidence reviewed in this paper compellingly supports the implementation of later school start times in American secondary schools. The biological evidence is clear that adolescents have delayed circadian rhythms that make early wake times physiologically harmful. The empirical evidence demonstrates that early start times are associated with sleep deprivation, poor academic performance, increased accident risk, and negative health outcomes. The policy evidence shows that later start times produce measurable benefits and that logistical challenges, while real, are manageable. School districts that have not yet implemented later start times should do so as a matter of student health and educational equity. State and federal policymakers should consider mandating minimum start times for secondary schools as California did in 2022.
