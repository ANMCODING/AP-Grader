The Influence of Teacher Feedback Style on Student Motivation in High School English Classes
AP Research
April 2025
Word Count: 3,742
Introduction
Teacher feedback is one of the most powerful tools available to educators for influencing student learning and motivation. Research consistently identifies feedback as a key factor in academic achievement and student engagement. However the way feedback is delivered matters enormously — the same factual content delivered in a controlling versus autonomy-supportive style can produce dramatically different motivational outcomes for students. This paper investigates the following question: How does the style of teacher written feedback on essays, specifically controlling versus autonomy-supportive feedback, influence intrinsic motivation and self-efficacy in high school English students?
This question is significant because English teachers across the United States provide written feedback on student essays regularly and most do so without formal training in feedback style or awareness of motivational research. If specific feedback styles are shown to undermine intrinsic motivation and self-efficacy, teacher training programs could be designed to promote more motivationally supportive feedback practices.
Literature Review
Research on Teacher Feedback
Research has shown that teacher feedback affects student learning and motivation. Hattie and Timperley wrote about feedback and its effects in 2007 and found that feedback is one of the most powerful influences on achievement. Black and Wiliam also wrote about feedback in 1998 and found that formative assessment including feedback improves student learning. Kluger and DeNisi studied feedback in 1996 and found that while feedback generally improves performance it can sometimes have negative effects especially when it focuses on the self rather than the task.
Self-Determination Theory and Motivation
Self-determination theory was developed by Deci and Ryan and explains that humans have basic psychological needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness. When these needs are met people are more intrinsically motivated. When these needs are frustrated people become less motivated or motivated only by external rewards. Research by Deci and Ryan and their colleagues has shown that autonomy-supportive environments produce better motivation outcomes than controlling environments across many contexts. Reeve wrote about how teachers can support student autonomy in the classroom and found that autonomy-supportive teachers have more motivated students.
Feedback Style and Motivation
Research has also looked at how feedback style specifically affects motivation. Deci and colleagues found that positive feedback increases intrinsic motivation while controlling feedback decreases it. Koestner and colleagues found that feedback given in a controlling way reduces intrinsic motivation compared to feedback given in an informational way. Vallerand and Reid found that verbal feedback affects intrinsic motivation through its effect on perceived competence.
Writing Feedback Specifically
Some researchers have looked at feedback on writing specifically. Straub found that students prefer feedback that is conversational and engaging rather than authoritative and directive. Ferris studied how students respond to teacher comments on their writing and found that students value feedback that is specific and actionable. Lee examined written feedback in second language writing contexts and found that feedback practices vary widely across teachers and institutions.
Gap
Despite this research on feedback and motivation, no study has directly compared the effects of controlling versus autonomy-supportive written feedback on intrinsic motivation and self-efficacy in high school English students in the United States. Most research on feedback has been conducted in laboratory settings or university contexts and has not examined the specific context of high school English essay feedback. This study fills that gap.
Methodology
This study used a mixed-methods approach combining a quasi-experimental design and student interviews. Participants were students from four English classes taught by two teachers at one high school in Ohio. Two classes were randomly assigned to receive autonomy-supportive feedback on their essays for one semester and two classes were randomly assigned to receive controlling feedback.
Autonomy-supportive feedback was defined as feedback that acknowledges student perspective, provides rationale for suggestions, uses non-pressuring language, and encourages student choice in revision. Controlling feedback was defined as feedback that uses directive language, focuses on errors without explanation, and provides prescriptive rather than invitational suggestions.
Both teachers were trained by the researcher in both feedback styles and practiced providing feedback samples before the study began. Training consisted of two two-hour sessions during which teachers reviewed examples of both feedback styles and practiced applying them to sample essays.
Intrinsic motivation was measured using the Academic Motivation Scale (AMS) by Vallerand et al. (1992) adapted for high school students. Self-efficacy was measured using Bandura's (1990) Academic Self-Efficacy Scale adapted for English class. Both scales were administered before the study began and after the semester concluded.
Pre and post scores were compared using paired samples t-tests within each condition and independent samples t-tests between conditions. Effect sizes were calculated using Cohen's d.
A subsample of 10 students from each condition was selected for interviews to explore how students experienced and interpreted the feedback they received.
Results
Table 1 presents pre and post motivation and self-efficacy scores by condition.
Condition — Pre Motivation — Post Motivation — Change — Pre Self-Efficacy — Post Self-Efficacy — Change
Autonomy-supportive (n=47) — 3.82 — 4.21 — +0.39 — 3.71 — 4.08 — +0.37
Controlling (n=44) — 3.79 — 3.61 — -0.18 — 3.68 — 3.44 — -0.24
Paired samples t-test within autonomy-supportive condition for motivation: t(46) = 5.34, p less than .001, d = 0.78. Motivation increased significantly.
Paired samples t-test within controlling condition for motivation: t(43) = 2.41, p = .020, d = 0.36. Motivation decreased significantly.
Independent samples t-test comparing post-intervention motivation between conditions: t(89) = 6.18, p less than .001, d = 1.31. Large significant difference favoring autonomy-supportive condition.
Independent samples t-test comparing post-intervention self-efficacy between conditions: t(89) = 5.87, p less than .001, d = 1.24. Large significant difference favoring autonomy-supportive condition.
From qualitative analysis three themes emerged. Theme 1: Feeling respected versus criticized. Students in the autonomy-supportive condition described feeling that their ideas were valued. Students in the controlling condition described feeling judged and defensive. Theme 2: Engagement with feedback. Students in the autonomy-supportive condition reported reading and thinking about their feedback more carefully than students in the controlling condition who often reported avoiding feedback. Theme 3: Writing confidence. Students in the autonomy-supportive condition reported increased confidence in their writing while students in the controlling condition reported decreased confidence.
Discussion
Results strongly support the hypothesis that autonomy-supportive written feedback produces significantly better motivational outcomes than controlling feedback in high school English students. The large effect sizes (d = 1.31 for motivation, d = 1.24 for self-efficacy) indicate that the practical significance of this finding is substantial. These results align with self-determination theory and extend prior laboratory findings on feedback style to the naturalistic context of high school English classrooms.
The qualitative themes provide explanatory depth for the quantitative patterns. Students in the controlling condition described avoidance of feedback which would explain the downward trend in motivation if students were not engaging with the feedback intended to improve their learning. The theme of feeling respected versus criticized aligns directly with self-determination theory's prediction that need for autonomy satisfaction mediates the relationship between feedback style and intrinsic motivation.
Limitations
The quasi-experimental design means that classes rather than individual students were assigned to conditions creating a clustering effect that the statistical analysis did not account for. Multilevel modeling would have been more appropriate but was beyond the scope of this study. The two teachers providing feedback may have differed in ways beyond feedback style that influenced student outcomes. Despite training both teachers may not have implemented the feedback styles with equal fidelity. The sample is from one school limiting generalizability.
Implications
English teachers and teacher educators should prioritize training in autonomy-supportive feedback style given the large motivational effects documented here. School districts investing in teacher professional development should include feedback style training as a component of English teacher preparation. Future research should use multilevel modeling to account for classroom clustering, examine whether feedback style effects persist across academic years, and investigate whether certain student characteristics moderate the relationship between feedback style and motivation.
Conclusion
This study found large significant effects of autonomy-supportive versus controlling written feedback on intrinsic motivation and self-efficacy in high school English students, with autonomy-supportive feedback producing substantially better outcomes across both quantitative and qualitative measures. These findings provide strong support for integrating feedback style training into teacher preparation and professional development programs.
