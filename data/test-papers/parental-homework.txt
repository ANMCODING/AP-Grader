How Does Parental Involvement in Homework Affect Academic Self-Efficacy in Middle School Students?
AP Research
April 2025
Word Count: 3,914
Introduction
Parental involvement in children's education has long been considered a positive factor in academic success. However the nature of that involvement matters significantly. In recent years researchers have begun distinguishing between different types of parental involvement and their varied effects on student outcomes. One area that has received increasing attention is the specific role of parental homework involvement and how it relates to students' beliefs about their own academic abilities, commonly referred to as academic self-efficacy. This paper investigates the following research question: How does the frequency and type of parental homework involvement relate to academic self-efficacy in sixth and seventh grade students?
This question is significant because academic self-efficacy, defined as a student's belief in their own capacity to successfully complete academic tasks (Bandura, 1997), has been consistently linked to academic achievement, persistence, and motivation. If certain types of parental homework involvement undermine rather than support self-efficacy, this has important implications for how schools communicate with parents about supporting their children's learning at home.
Literature Review
Parental Involvement and Academic Outcomes
Research on parental involvement broadly defined consistently shows positive associations with academic outcomes. Jeynes (2007) conducted a meta-analysis of 52 studies and found that parental involvement was associated with significantly higher academic achievement across grade levels and demographic groups. Fan and Chen (2001) similarly found a consistent positive association between parental involvement and academic achievement in their meta-analysis of 25 studies. However both meta-analyses noted substantial heterogeneity in how parental involvement was defined and measured across studies, making comparisons difficult.
Homework Involvement Specifically
More recent research has distinguished homework involvement specifically from broader parental involvement and has found more nuanced results. Patall, Cooper, and Robinson (2008) conducted a meta-analysis specifically on parental homework involvement and found a small but significant negative association between direct homework help and academic achievement. They proposed that direct homework assistance may undermine students' development of independent academic skills. Silinskas and Kikas (2019) found that parental homework involvement was most beneficial when it was autonomy-supportive, meaning parents provided guidance and encouragement without taking over the task, and most harmful when it was controlling, meaning parents directed the work or completed portions themselves.
Academic Self-Efficacy
Bandura (1997) identified four primary sources of self-efficacy beliefs: mastery experiences, vicarious experiences, verbal persuasion, and physiological states. Of these, mastery experiences — successful completion of challenging tasks independently — are considered the most powerful source of self-efficacy. If parents consistently help with or complete homework tasks, students may be deprived of mastery experiences and thus develop lower self-efficacy even if their immediate performance appears adequate (Patall et al., 2008). Hoover-Dempsey et al. (2001) found that when parents helped with homework in ways that kept children dependent rather than building independence, students showed lower academic confidence over time.
Gap
While research has established associations between parental homework involvement and academic achievement, and between mastery experiences and self-efficacy, no study to date has directly examined the relationship between specific types of parental homework involvement and academic self-efficacy in middle school students using both quantitative survey measures and qualitative interviews simultaneously. Most existing research has examined achievement outcomes rather than self-efficacy specifically, and has not focused on the middle school transition period when self-efficacy beliefs are particularly formative (Wigfield et al., 2006). This study addresses that gap.
Methodology
This study used a mixed-methods design combining a survey and semi-structured interviews. The survey collected quantitative data on parental homework involvement frequency, involvement type, and academic self-efficacy. The interviews collected qualitative data on how students experienced and interpreted their parents' homework involvement.
Participants were 64 sixth and seventh grade students from one middle school in suburban Ohio. Surveys were distributed through homeroom teachers. Informed consent was obtained from participants and parents. The study was approved by the school district research ethics committee.
The parental homework involvement survey was adapted from the Parental Homework Involvement Scale developed by Silinskas and Kikas (2019), which measures three types of involvement: autonomy-supportive involvement (helping by explaining and encouraging), controlling involvement (directing, checking, or completing work), and emotional involvement (expressing concern or anxiety about homework). Academic self-efficacy was measured using an adapted version of the Academic Self-Efficacy Scale from Bandura (1990), a validated instrument with items rated on a 5-point Likert scale.
Pearson correlation analysis was used to examine relationships between involvement type scores and self-efficacy scores. An independent samples t-test compared self-efficacy between students reporting primarily autonomy-supportive versus primarily controlling parental involvement. All analyses were conducted in SPSS version 28.
A purposive subsample of 8 students was selected for semi-structured interviews to represent variation in involvement type and self-efficacy scores. Interviews were audio recorded, transcribed, and analyzed using thematic coding following Braun and Clarke (2006).
Results
Table 1 presents correlations between parental homework involvement types and academic self-efficacy scores.
Autonomy-supportive involvement showed a moderate positive correlation with academic self-efficacy (r = .38, p = .002). Controlling involvement showed a moderate negative correlation with academic self-efficacy (r = -.41, p less than .001). Emotional involvement showed a weak non-significant negative correlation (r = -.19, p = .13).
The independent samples t-test showed that students reporting primarily autonomy-supportive involvement had significantly higher self-efficacy scores than students reporting primarily controlling involvement, t(62) = 3.47, p = .001, d = 0.87, indicating a large effect size.
Three themes emerged from qualitative analysis. Theme 1: Feeling capable versus feeling helped. Students with high self-efficacy consistently described parents who explained how to approach problems without giving answers. Students with lower self-efficacy more often described parents checking and correcting their work. Theme 2: Stress about parental expectations. Several students described feeling anxious when parents expressed frustration or concern about homework, which they associated with doubting their own abilities. Theme 3: Preference for independence. Most interview participants across self-efficacy levels expressed a desire to complete homework independently and described feeling proud when they figured things out themselves, supporting Bandura's mastery experience theory.
Discussion
The results support the hypothesis that autonomy-supportive parental homework involvement is associated with higher academic self-efficacy while controlling involvement is associated with lower self-efficacy. The large effect size (d = 0.87) in the t-test comparison suggests this is a practically meaningful difference, not merely a statistical one. The qualitative themes extend the quantitative findings by providing student-reported explanations for why these associations exist, directly supporting Bandura's mastery experience framework.
The non-significant correlation between emotional involvement and self-efficacy was unexpected. The qualitative data suggested that parental anxiety about homework did create stress for some students, but this did not translate into a statistically detectable self-efficacy difference at the group level. This discrepancy between individual qualitative accounts and group-level quantitative results illustrates the value of the mixed-methods approach — the group correlation masks individual variation that the qualitative data captures.
Limitations
The cross-sectional design prevents causal conclusions. It is possible that students with lower self-efficacy attract more controlling parental involvement rather than that controlling involvement causes lower self-efficacy. The sample is from one suburban school limiting generalizability. The adapted scales, while based on validated instruments, were modified for middle school reading level and have not been independently validated in this population. The qualitative subsample of 8 is small and may not represent the full range of student experiences.
Implications
School counselors and parent education coordinators should use these findings to develop parent workshops that distinguish between autonomy-supportive and controlling homework involvement and explain the self-efficacy implications of each. Teachers designing homework assignments should consider how the tasks are structured to allow independent completion rather than requiring parental assistance. Future research should use longitudinal designs to establish causal direction and should examine whether the type of homework task moderates the relationship between parental involvement and self-efficacy.
Conclusion
This study found significant associations between parental homework involvement type and academic self-efficacy in sixth and seventh grade students, with autonomy-supportive involvement positively associated and controlling involvement negatively associated with self-efficacy. Qualitative findings confirmed and extended the quantitative results through student-reported experiences of mastery and dependence. These findings provide preliminary evidence that the type rather than the frequency of parental homework involvement matters for self-efficacy development in early adolescence.
