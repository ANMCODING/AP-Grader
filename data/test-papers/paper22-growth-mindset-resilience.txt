The Relationship Between Growth Mindset and Academic Resilience in High School Students Experiencing Academic Setbacks
AP Research
April 2025
Word Count: 4,103
Introduction
Academic setbacks — including failing grades, rejected applications, poor standardized test scores, and public academic disappointments — are an inevitable part of the educational experience for most students. How students respond to these setbacks varies dramatically and has significant consequences for their long-term academic trajectories. Some students respond to failure with increased effort, revised strategies, and sustained engagement — a pattern researchers call academic resilience. Others respond with withdrawal, disengagement, helplessness, and avoidance. Carol Dweck's theory of mindset proposes that beliefs about the malleability of intelligence are a key determinant of how individuals respond to challenge and failure (Dweck, 2006). Students who hold a growth mindset — believing that intelligence and ability can be developed through effort — are theorized to respond to setbacks with greater resilience than students who hold a fixed mindset — believing that intelligence is a stable trait. This research investigates the following question: What is the relationship between growth mindset scores and academic resilience scores in high school students who have experienced a significant academic setback in the past year?
This question is significant because growth mindset interventions have become widespread in American schools with many districts spending significant resources on mindset training programs. Understanding the relationship between mindset and resilience specifically in the context of setbacks rather than academic performance generally could help schools design more targeted and effective interventions.
Literature Review
Growth Mindset Theory
Dweck (2006) developed the theory of growth mindset through decades of research beginning with studies of how children respond to failure. Her core finding was that children who were praised for their intelligence (effort-independent) showed decreased persistence following failure while children praised for their effort showed increased persistence. Blackwell, Trzesniewski, and Dweck (2007) demonstrated in a longitudinal study of 373 seventh graders that growth mindset predicted an upward trajectory of grades across the transition to junior high while fixed mindset predicted a flat or declining trajectory. Yeager and Dweck (2012) reviewed the evidence for mindset interventions in education and found moderate positive effects on academic outcomes, particularly for students from lower socioeconomic backgrounds and for students facing academic transitions. These three studies collectively establish that growth mindset is a real and consequential construct with predictable relationships to academic outcomes.
Academic Resilience
Academic resilience is a related but distinct construct from academic achievement. Martin and Marsh (2006) developed the Academic Resilience Scale and defined academic resilience as the capacity for students to effectively deal with setbacks, challenges, and pressure in the academic context. Their validation study showed that academic resilience predicted academic achievement, attendance, and enjoyment of school beyond what general resilience or academic self-concept alone predicted. Cassidy (2016) reviewed 25 years of academic resilience research and found consistent associations between academic resilience and both academic achievement and psychological wellbeing, arguing that academic resilience deserves attention as a target of educational intervention in its own right rather than only as a predictor of achievement.
The Mindset-Resilience Connection
Despite the theoretical connection between growth mindset and academic resilience — both concern how students respond to difficulty — surprisingly few empirical studies have directly examined their relationship. Claro, Paunesku, and Dweck (2016) found in a large-scale study of Chilean students that growth mindset buffered the negative effects of poverty on academic achievement, which they interpreted as evidence that mindset supports resilience in the face of adversity. However this study measured academic achievement as the outcome not academic resilience directly. Schroder, Dawood, Yalch, Donnellan, and Moser (2015) examined mindset and neural responses to errors and found that growth mindset was associated with greater attention to error-related neural signals, suggesting a neurological basis for the relationship between mindset and learning from failure. Neither study directly measured the relationship between mindset and academic resilience as Dweck and Martin conceptualized them.
Gap
While growth mindset and academic resilience are both well-established constructs with strong theoretical connections, and while several studies have examined each construct independently, no study has directly examined the correlation between validated growth mindset scores and validated academic resilience scores specifically in high school students who have experienced a significant academic setback in the past year. The setback focus is important because it is precisely in the context of setbacks that the mindset-resilience relationship is theorized to be most relevant — growth mindset is not theorized to predict resilience uniformly but specifically in the context of challenge and failure. This study addresses that specific gap.
Methodology
Research Design
This study used a correlational survey design to examine the relationship between growth mindset and academic resilience in high school students who had experienced a recent academic setback. A survey design was chosen because it allows efficient data collection from a sufficient sample size to detect the moderate effect sizes expected based on prior research. The correlational design is appropriate because the research question asks about the relationship between two existing characteristics of participants rather than the effect of an intervention.
Participants
Participants were 78 high school students from one suburban high school in Ohio who reported having experienced a significant academic setback in the past year. A setback was defined as any of the following: receiving a failing grade on a major assignment or exam, failing a course, receiving a rejection from a program or competition they applied to, or scoring significantly below expectations on a standardized test. Recruitment was conducted through homeroom teachers. Informed consent was obtained from all participants and parental consent for participants under 18. The study was approved by the school district research ethics committee.
Measures
Growth mindset was measured using Dweck's (2006) implicit theories of intelligence scale, a validated 8-item measure with items rated on a 6-point Likert scale. Example item: "Your intelligence is something about you that you can't change very much." Items are reverse-scored so that higher scores indicate stronger growth mindset. The scale has established reliability with Cronbach's alpha typically reported around 0.85. Academic resilience was measured using the 30-item Academic Resilience Scale developed by Martin and Marsh (2006) with items rated on a 7-point scale. Example item: "When I experience a setback at school I soon recover and get back on track."
Procedure
Surveys were administered electronically through a secure school platform during homeroom period. Participants first answered a screening question about whether they had experienced a significant academic setback in the past year. Students who answered no were thanked and dismissed. Students who answered yes completed both the growth mindset scale and the academic resilience scale. Data were downloaded as a CSV and analyzed in SPSS version 28.
Data Analysis
Pearson correlation analysis examined the relationship between growth mindset scores and academic resilience scores. A multiple regression analysis examined whether growth mindset predicted academic resilience after controlling for grade level and gender. Effect size was calculated using r and R-squared. Significance threshold was set at p less than 0.05.
Results
Table 1 presents descriptive statistics for growth mindset and academic resilience scores.
The Pearson correlation between growth mindset scores and academic resilience scores was r = .47, p less than .001, indicating a moderate statistically significant positive relationship. Students with stronger growth mindsets reported higher academic resilience following academic setbacks.
Multiple regression analysis: growth mindset was a significant predictor of academic resilience after controlling for grade level and gender, beta = .44, p less than .001, R-squared = .23. Grade level and gender were not significant predictors (p = .34 and p = .61 respectively). Figure 1 displays the scatterplot of growth mindset scores against academic resilience scores showing the positive linear trend.
Discussion
These results demonstrate a moderate statistically significant positive relationship between growth mindset and academic resilience in high school students who have experienced recent academic setbacks. The finding that growth mindset predicted approximately 23 percent of the variance in academic resilience after controlling for demographic variables is consistent with the theoretical framework proposed by Dweck (2006) and extends prior research by directly measuring academic resilience rather than achievement as the outcome. The effect size (r = .47) is consistent with Yeager and Dweck's (2012) review of mindset effects and with Claro et al.'s (2016) buffering effect finding, suggesting that the mindset-resilience relationship found here is in the expected range based on prior literature.
The non-significant effects of grade level and gender are somewhat surprising given prior research suggesting that mindset effects may be stronger during academic transitions (Blackwell et al., 2007) and that gender differences in mindset have been documented in some contexts. The absence of these demographic effects in this sample may reflect the specific context of recent setback — it is possible that the mindset-resilience relationship is relatively uniform across demographic groups specifically in the context of setback experience, while demographic differences emerge more clearly in non-setback contexts.
Limitations
This study has several limitations. The correlational design prevents causal inference — it is possible that students who are more academically resilient develop stronger growth mindsets through their experience of navigating setbacks rather than that growth mindset causes resilience. The sample is from one suburban Ohio school and results may not generalize to more diverse populations. The self-report measures are subject to social desirability bias. The definition of academic setback was self-reported and participants may have applied the definition inconsistently.
Implications
These findings suggest that growth mindset and academic resilience are related constructs that may benefit from integrated intervention approaches. Schools implementing growth mindset programs should also assess academic resilience as an outcome. Future research should use longitudinal designs to examine whether growth mindset interventions improve academic resilience over time.
Conclusion
This study found a moderate statistically significant positive relationship between growth mindset and academic resilience in high school students who had experienced recent academic setbacks, with growth mindset predicting 23 percent of the variance in academic resilience after controlling for demographic variables. These findings extend prior mindset research by directly measuring academic resilience as the outcome and suggest that the mindset-resilience relationship is robust in the specific context of setback experience.
