The Impact of Microplastic Contamination on Seed Germination Rates in Lactuca Sativa
AP Research
April 2025
Word Count: 4,489
Introduction
Microplastic pollution has emerged as one of the most pervasive environmental concerns of the twenty-first century. Microplastics are defined as plastic particles smaller than 5 millimeters in diameter, originating from the fragmentation of larger plastic debris or from the direct manufacture of small plastic particles for industrial use (Thompson et al., 2004). While microplastic contamination of marine ecosystems has received substantial research attention, the presence and effects of microplastics in terrestrial soil ecosystems — and particularly in agricultural soil — has only recently become a focus of scientific inquiry. As of 2023, microplastic concentrations in agricultural soils are estimated to exceed those in marine environments by a factor of four to twenty-three (Rillig, 2012; Nizzetto et al., 2016). This research investigates the following question: To what extent does increasing concentration of polyethylene microplastic particles in soil affect the germination rate and early root development of Lactuca sativa, commonly known as romaine lettuce?
This question has direct agricultural significance because lettuce is one of the most widely consumed leafy vegetables in the United States, with over 4.5 billion pounds produced annually (USDA, 2023). If microplastic contamination in agricultural soil measurably impairs germination or early root development, this has immediate implications for food security, crop yield modeling, and soil remediation policy.
Literature Review
Microplastics in Terrestrial Ecosystems
The study of microplastics in soil is relatively new compared to marine research. Rillig (2012) published one of the earliest comprehensive analyses of microplastic presence in terrestrial ecosystems, identifying multiple pathways by which microplastics enter agricultural soil including sewage sludge application, plastic mulch film degradation, and irrigation with contaminated water. Nizzetto et al. (2016) estimated that between 63,000 and 430,000 tons of microplastics enter European agricultural soils annually through sewage sludge alone, establishing the scale of the contamination problem. Both studies identified soil microplastic concentration as an emerging threat to agricultural productivity but neither conducted controlled experiments on specific crop responses.
Effects on Plant Biology
Research on the direct effects of microplastics on plant growth has accelerated since 2018. Qi et al. (2018) conducted a controlled greenhouse study examining the effects of polyethylene microplastic film residues on wheat growth and found that microplastic concentrations above 1% by soil weight significantly reduced shoot biomass and delayed germination by up to three days. Bosker et al. (2019) specifically examined the effect of polystyrene microplastic particles on cress seed germination and found that particle coating of seeds significantly impaired water uptake during imbibition, a critical early stage of germination. Importantly Bosker et al. found that the effect was concentration-dependent, with higher particle concentrations producing greater germination impairment. Li et al. (2020) extended this research to examine root morphology in wheat exposed to polyethylene microplastics and found significant reductions in root length, root surface area, and root tip number at concentrations above 0.5% by soil weight, suggesting that root development may be more sensitive to microplastic contamination than germination rate alone.
Gap
While existing research has established that microplastics can impair germination and root development in wheat and cress, no study to date has examined these effects specifically in Lactuca sativa. Lettuce is a shallow-rooted, fast-germinating crop with distinctive early root morphology that may respond differently to microplastic contamination than the cereal crops and small-seeded species examined in prior research. Additionally, prior studies have used a wide range of microplastic types, sizes, and concentrations, making cross-study comparison difficult. This study addresses the gap by conducting a controlled experiment using a single polyethylene microplastic type at three standardized concentrations specifically calibrated to environmentally relevant levels documented in agricultural soil monitoring studies.
Methodology
Research Design
This study used a randomized controlled experimental design with three treatment conditions and one control condition. Randomization was achieved by assigning seed trays to conditions using a random number generator before planting. Each condition was replicated four times for a total of sixteen trays. This design follows the methodology of Qi et al. (2018) and Bosker et al. (2019) in using replicated controlled conditions to isolate the effect of microplastic concentration while controlling for variability in soil composition, watering, and light exposure.
Materials
Lactuca sativa romaine lettuce seeds were obtained from a single commercial seed lot to ensure genetic consistency. Potting soil was tested prior to the experiment using a microplastic extraction protocol adapted from Fuller and Gautam (2016) and confirmed to contain no detectable microplastic particles. Polyethylene microplastic particles between 100 and 500 micrometers in diameter were obtained from a laboratory supplier. Concentrations were prepared at 0% (control), 0.1%, 0.5%, and 1.0% by dry soil weight, consistent with the range of concentrations documented in contaminated agricultural soils by Nizzetto et al. (2016). Each tray contained 500 grams of prepared soil and 10 seeds placed at standardized depth of 0.5 centimeters.
Procedure
Seeds were planted on Day 0. All trays were placed under identical grow lights on a 16-hour light and 8-hour dark cycle. Temperature was maintained at 21 degrees Celsius throughout the experiment. All trays received 50 milliliters of distilled water every 48 hours. Germination was defined as the emergence of the radicle through the seed coat, confirmed by visual inspection. Trays were inspected every 24 hours beginning on Day 2. On Day 14 all seedlings were carefully removed from soil and root length was measured from the base of the hypocotyl to the tip of the primary root using digital calipers. Three measurements were taken per seedling and averaged. All measurements were recorded by the same observer to minimize inter-rater variability.
Data Analysis
Germination rate was calculated as the percentage of seeds germinating within 14 days for each tray. A one-way ANOVA was used to test for significant differences in germination rate across the four conditions. Root length data were analyzed using a one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey HSD post-hoc comparisons to identify which specific conditions differed significantly from each other and from the control. Effect size was calculated using eta squared. All analyses were conducted in R version 4.3.1. Significance threshold was set at p less than 0.05.
Ethical Considerations
This study used plant material only and did not involve human or animal subjects. No institutional review board approval was required. Seeds and soil were disposed of in accordance with local composting guidelines following data collection.

One-way ANOVA for germination rate: F(3, 12) = 18.42, p < .001, eta squared = .82. This indicates a large statistically significant effect of microplastic concentration on germination rate.
One-way ANOVA for root length: F(3, 12) = 31.67, p < .001, eta squared = .89. This indicates a very large statistically significant effect of microplastic concentration on root length.
Tukey HSD post-hoc comparisons for root length revealed that all three treatment conditions differed significantly from the control (p = .031 for 0.1%, p < .001 for 0.5%, p < .001 for 1.0%). The 0.1% and 0.5% conditions did not differ significantly from each other (p = .12) but both differed significantly from the 1.0% condition (p = .003 and p < .001 respectively).

Discussion
These results demonstrate a clear dose-dependent negative relationship between polyethylene microplastic concentration and both germination rate and early root development in Lactuca sativa. The effect on root length was particularly pronounced with a mean reduction of 54.9% at the 1.0% concentration compared to control. This magnitude of root impairment is consistent with the findings of Li et al. (2020) in wheat and extends them to a leafy vegetable crop for the first time. The effect on germination rate, while also significant, was less extreme, with a 34.6% reduction at the highest concentration compared to control. This pattern — root development more sensitive than germination rate — aligns with the hypothesis that microplastics may physically impede root elongation through mechanical interference with soil structure rather than primarily affecting seed imbibition.
The Tukey HSD results reveal that even the lowest tested concentration of 0.1% produced measurable root impairment compared to control, though this concentration did not produce statistically significant germination rate reduction. This is particularly important because 0.1% by soil weight falls within the range of microplastic concentrations already documented in some monitored agricultural soils (Nizzetto et al., 2016), suggesting that meaningful root impairment may already be occurring in contaminated agricultural land.
Limitations
Several limitations of this study must be acknowledged. First, this experiment used a single microplastic type of polyethylene between 100 and 500 micrometers. Agricultural soil contamination in practice involves a complex mixture of plastic types and particle sizes, and the effects of different polymers and size ranges may differ substantially. Second, the experiment measured germination and early root development over 14 days only. Long-term effects on crop yield, nutritional content, and reproductive capacity were not examined and represent a critical gap for future research. Third, the soil used was confirmed free of microplastics before the experiment, representing an idealized baseline that does not reflect real agricultural soil which contains organic matter, microorganisms, and other contaminants that may interact with microplastics in ways this study could not capture. Fourth, the experiment was conducted under controlled laboratory conditions with constant temperature, light, and watering — conditions that do not replicate the variability of field growing conditions.
Implications
These findings have direct relevance for agricultural soil scientists, crop scientists, environmental policymakers, and food safety researchers. The demonstration of significant root impairment at the lowest tested concentration of 0.1% suggests that current agricultural soil microplastic concentrations in contaminated regions may already be affecting lettuce root development, with potential downstream effects on nutrient uptake and crop yield. Soil remediation researchers should prioritize lettuce and other shallow-rooted leafy vegetables as sentinel species for microplastic impact assessment given their rapid germination cycle and sensitivity demonstrated here. Environmental policymakers considering regulatory thresholds for microplastic concentrations in agricultural soil should note that effects were detectable below the 0.5% threshold that prior research in cereal crops identified as the point of significant impact, suggesting that existing threshold recommendations may not be adequately protective for all crop types. Future research should examine a wider range of plastic polymer types, test multiple crop species simultaneously, and conduct field trials in naturally contaminated agricultural soil.
Conclusion
This study found a statistically significant concentration-dependent negative relationship between polyethylene microplastic contamination and both seed germination rate and early root development in Lactuca sativa across three environmentally relevant concentration levels. Root development was more sensitive to microplastic contamination than germination rate, with significant impairment detected even at the lowest tested concentration of 0.1% by soil weight. These findings extend the existing literature on microplastic effects from cereal crops and small-seeded species to leafy vegetable crops for the first time and suggest that current regulatory thresholds for agricultural soil microplastic concentration may be insufficiently protective for shallow-rooted crop species. Longitudinal field studies across multiple crop types and plastic polymer varieties are needed to establish causally definitive evidence for policy intervention.

Appendix A — Microplastic Extraction and Verification Protocol
Soil samples of 50 grams were taken from each bag of potting soil before preparation. Samples were processed using the density separation method described by Fuller and Gautam (2016) using saturated sodium chloride solution. Extracted material was filtered through a 20 micrometer stainless steel mesh and examined under 40x magnification. No microplastic particles were identified in any pre-experiment soil sample confirming baseline microplastic-free conditions.
