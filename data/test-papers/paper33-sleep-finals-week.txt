The Relationship Between Sleep Duration and Academic Performance in High School Students During Finals Week
AP Research
April 2025
Word Count: 3,891
Introduction
Sleep is essential for cognitive function, memory consolidation, and academic performance. Research consistently shows that adolescents require 8 to 10 hours of sleep per night for optimal health and cognitive functioning, yet surveys consistently find that most high school students sleep significantly less than this recommendation, particularly during high-stress periods like finals week (Wheaton et al., 2018). The relationship between sleep deprivation and academic performance has been studied in general contexts but the specific relationship during finals week — when students face the dual pressure of increased academic demands and the temptation to sacrifice sleep for additional study time — has not been directly examined in a high school population. This research investigates the following question: Is there a significant relationship between average nightly sleep duration during finals week and final exam scores among high school students?
This question matters because high school students, parents, and teachers make decisions about study strategies and sleep during finals week based on intuition rather than evidence. Understanding whether sleep duration during finals week predicts exam scores could inform evidence-based guidance for students preparing for high-stakes assessments.
Literature Review
Sleep and Academic Performance
Research has consistently documented negative relationships between insufficient sleep and academic performance. Curcio et al. (2006) reviewed research on sleep loss and daytime sleepiness in adolescents and found consistent associations between reduced sleep duration and lower academic performance across multiple measures. Dewald et al. (2010) conducted a meta-analysis of 16 studies examining sleep quality, sleepiness, and school performance in children and adolescents and found a significant overall negative relationship between daytime sleepiness and academic performance (r = −0.13 to −0.19). Wolfson and Carskadon (1998) examined sleep patterns of high school students and found that students with higher grades reported earlier bedtimes and more total sleep compared to lower-achieving students. These studies establish a general relationship between sleep and academic performance.
Sleep During High-Stress Periods
Research on sleep specifically during high-stress academic periods has produced relevant but limited findings. Hershner and Chervin (2014) examined sleep in college students during exam periods and found that sleep deprivation was common during exams and was associated with lower academic performance. However this study focused on college students rather than high school students and examined college-level exams rather than high school finals. Orzech et al. (2011) used actigraphy to examine sleep patterns of high school students across a full academic year and found that sleep declined significantly during periods of high academic demand but did not examine the relationship between this sleep reduction and academic performance during those specific periods.
Gap
Despite extensive research on sleep and academic performance in general and some research on sleep during exam periods in college students, no study has examined the specific relationship between sleep duration during finals week and final exam scores in high school students. This study addresses that gap by collecting both sleep data and exam score data from high school students during their actual finals week.
Methodology
This study used a correlational survey design to examine the relationship between sleep duration and academic performance during finals week. Participants were 83 students from one high school in suburban Ohio who completed a brief survey during the week following finals. The survey asked students to report their average nightly sleep duration during the preceding finals week, their scores on their final exams (reported as letter grades converted to a 4-point scale: A=4, B=3, C=2, D=1, F=0), and their usual sleep duration during non-finals weeks for comparison. The survey was distributed through homeroom teachers. Responses were anonymous. Informed consent was obtained.
A chi-square test of independence examined whether sleep category (less than 6 hours, 6 to 7 hours, 7 to 8 hours, more than 8 hours) was independent of exam performance category (below average defined as C or lower, average defined as B, above average defined as A). A Pearson correlation examined the linear relationship between reported sleep hours and GPA-equivalent exam scores. All analyses were conducted in Excel with statistical formulas.
Results
Table 1 presents the distribution of sleep categories and exam performance categories among the 83 participants.
Of the 83 participants 31 (37.3%) reported sleeping less than 6 hours per night during finals week. 29 (34.9%) reported 6 to 7 hours. 18 (21.7%) reported 7 to 8 hours. Only 5 (6.0%) reported more than 8 hours.
The chi-square test of independence was statistically significant: χ²(6) = 18.43, p = .005. Students who slept more during finals week tended to score higher on their final exams.
The Pearson correlation between sleep hours and exam performance was r = .38, p less than .001, indicating a moderate statistically significant positive relationship.
Figure 1 displays the distribution of exam performance categories by sleep category showing the trend toward higher performance with more sleep.
Discussion
These results demonstrate a statistically significant moderate positive relationship between sleep duration during finals week and final exam performance in high school students. Students who slept more during finals week tended to perform better on their final exams, consistent with the broader literature on sleep and academic performance reviewed by Curcio et al. (2006) and Dewald et al. (2010). The finding that 37 percent of students slept fewer than 6 hours during finals week is consistent with Orzech et al.'s (2011) finding of reduced sleep during high academic demand periods.
The cross-sectional correlational design prevents causal conclusions. It is possible that students who perform better academically also manage their time better and therefore sleep more, rather than that sleep causes better performance. The self-reported nature of both sleep and exam scores introduces measurement error.
Limitations
The sample from one school limits generalizability. Sleep duration and exam scores were both self-reported. The conversion of letter grades to a 4-point scale loses information. The study was conducted after finals week relying on retrospective recall which may be inaccurate.
Implications
These findings suggest that school counselors and teachers should advise students to prioritize sleep during finals week rather than sacrificing sleep for additional study time. Future research should use objective sleep measurement during actual finals week and collect official exam scores rather than self-reports.
Conclusion
This study found a statistically significant moderate positive relationship between sleep duration during finals week and final exam performance in high school students, consistent with the broader literature on sleep and academic performance. The correlational design limits causal conclusions but the findings provide preliminary evidence that sleep during finals week is associated with better exam performance.
