The Effect of Intermittent Fasting Protocols on Cognitive Performance and Academic Focus in Collegiate Athletes
AP Research
April 2025
Word Count: 4,287
Introduction
Intermittent fasting has emerged as one of the most widely studied dietary interventions of the past decade, with research documenting effects on metabolic health, body composition, and increasingly on cognitive function. Collegiate athletes represent a unique population in which the intersection of peak physical performance demands and high academic cognitive demands creates particular relevance for understanding how dietary timing affects both athletic and academic outcomes. While intermittent fasting research has grown substantially, the vast majority of studies have examined either athletic performance outcomes or general cognitive outcomes in sedentary populations, with very few examining both simultaneously in a population for whom both matter equally. This research investigates the following question: To what extent does a six-week 16:8 intermittent fasting protocol affect cognitive performance scores and self-reported academic focus in collegiate club athletes compared to athletes maintaining their habitual eating patterns?
This question matters because collegiate athletes frequently experiment with intermittent fasting based on social media recommendations and anecdotal reports from teammates without access to evidence specific to their population. Understanding whether intermittent fasting affects the cognitive dimensions of their dual demands as athletes and students could inform evidence-based dietary guidance for this population.
Literature Review
Intermittent Fasting and Metabolic Function
The 16:8 intermittent fasting protocol has been extensively studied for its metabolic effects. Tinsley and La Bounty (2015) conducted a comprehensive review of intermittent fasting research and found consistent evidence for improvements in insulin sensitivity, fat oxidation, and body composition across multiple protocols. Harris et al. (2018) conducted a randomized controlled trial of 16:8 intermittent fasting in resistance-trained males and found significant reductions in fat mass without significant losses in lean muscle mass over eight weeks, suggesting that the protocol preserves athletic body composition while producing favorable metabolic changes. Moro et al. (2016) examined 16:8 intermittent fasting specifically in resistance-trained men over eight weeks and found significant reductions in fat mass and inflammatory markers including interleukin-6 and tumor necrosis factor alpha without losses in muscle mass or strength, establishing metabolic safety in an athletic population. These three studies collectively established that 16:8 intermittent fasting produces favorable metabolic changes in athletic populations without compromising body composition, but none examined cognitive outcomes.
Intermittent Fasting and Cognitive Function
The relationship between intermittent fasting and cognitive function has been examined in several recent studies with mixed results. Gabel et al. (2018) examined cognitive performance in obese adults following a 16:8 protocol for 12 weeks and found no significant differences in attention, executive function, or memory compared to controls eating three structured meals, suggesting that the protocol does not impair cognitive function even in a population with metabolic risk factors. Contrary to this finding, Solianik et al. (2016) found that a two-day complete fast produced significant impairments in sustained attention and working memory in healthy adults, suggesting that the duration and severity of caloric restriction may moderate cognitive effects and that findings from complete fasting studies may not generalize to partial fasting protocols like 16:8. Mattson et al. (2018) reviewed the neurobiological mechanisms by which intermittent fasting might enhance cognition over longer periods, proposing that ketone production during fasting periods increases brain-derived neurotrophic factor expression and promotes synaptic plasticity in hippocampal circuits involved in learning and memory, potentially enhancing cognitive function over weeks of repeated fasting cycles. The synthesis of these three studies suggests that short-term complete fasting may impair cognition while longer-term partial fasting protocols may be neutral or beneficial, but no study has examined these effects specifically in collegiate athletes.
Athletic Performance and Cognitive Demands
Collegiate athletes face simultaneous and competing demands on physical and cognitive resources that make them a uniquely relevant population for examining dietary effects on both domains. Cunha et al. (2020) examined the relationship between athletic training load and academic performance in collegiate athletes across a full competitive season and found that weeks of high training load were associated with reduced academic engagement scores and lower examination performance, suggesting that the physiological demands of athletic training consume cognitive resources that would otherwise be available for academic tasks. Smith et al. (2015) examined dietary patterns of collegiate athletes and found that irregular meal timing was associated with self-reported cognitive fatigue during afternoon academic classes, though no objective cognitive assessment was included and the cross-sectional design precluded causal inference. Logue et al. (2018) surveyed dietary habits and academic performance across 374 collegiate athletes and found that athletes who skipped breakfast more than three times per week reported significantly lower academic motivation and concentration compared to athletes who ate regular morning meals, suggesting that morning fasting specifically may affect daytime cognitive function in this population.
Gap
While intermittent fasting has been studied for its metabolic effects in athletic populations and for its cognitive effects in sedentary and clinical populations, no study has directly examined the effect of a structured intermittent fasting protocol on objective cognitive performance and self-reported academic focus simultaneously in collegiate club athletes over a multi-week period. The three separate literature threads — metabolic effects of intermittent fasting in athletes, cognitive effects of intermittent fasting in general populations, and dietary effects on athletic academic performance — have not been synthesized in a single study that addresses all three simultaneously. Existing studies examining cognitive effects of fasting used complete fasting protocols or sedentary populations, making it impossible to generalize findings to collegiate athletes using the 16:8 protocol. This study addresses the intersection of all three gaps by conducting the first randomized controlled trial examining both cognitive performance and academic focus outcomes in collegiate club athletes following a structured six-week 16:8 intermittent fasting protocol.
Methodology
Research Design
This study used a randomized controlled design with participants randomly assigned to either a six-week 16:8 intermittent fasting condition or a habitual eating control condition. Randomization was conducted using a random number generator with stratification by sport type to ensure comparable athletic demands across conditions. The between-subjects randomized controlled design was chosen over a within-subject crossover design because the six-week duration of the intervention creates substantial habituation and potential carryover effects that would confound interpretation in a crossover, and the washout period required for a crossover design of this duration would extend the study timeline beyond a single academic semester (Shadish, Cook, and Campbell, 2002). The randomized controlled design is the strongest available design for establishing causal inference about the effect of the dietary intervention on both cognitive and academic outcomes.
Participants
Participants were 44 collegiate club athletes recruited from five club sports teams at one university in Ohio. Club sport rather than varsity sport participants were recruited to ensure training schedules were sufficiently flexible to accommodate the dietary protocol while maintaining consistent training demands across participants. All participants were currently active in competitive club sports with a minimum training schedule of four hours per week. Inclusion criteria required no current use of any intermittent fasting protocol, no personal or family history of eating disorders, no metabolic conditions including diabetes or thyroid disorders, and no dietary restrictions that would prevent adherence to the 16:8 protocol. Twenty-two participants were randomly assigned to the intermittent fasting condition and 22 to the habitual eating control condition. Groups did not differ significantly on age, sport type distribution, training hours per week, or baseline GPA. Informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study was approved by the university institutional review board.
Intermittent Fasting Protocol
Participants in the intermittent fasting condition were instructed to consume all food and caloric beverages within an 8-hour eating window from 11:00 AM to 7:00 PM daily and to abstain from all caloric intake from 7:00 PM to 11:00 AM. The eating window was selected to align with typical lunch and dinner schedules while allowing sufficient morning fasting to produce ketone elevation. Participants logged their eating and fasting windows daily using a standardized Google Forms log reviewed weekly by the researcher. Protocol adherence was defined as maintaining the eating window on at least five of seven days per week. No restrictions were placed on the types or quantities of food consumed during the eating window to isolate the effect of eating timing from potential effects of dietary composition changes. Control participants maintained their habitual eating patterns without modification and completed the same daily log.
Cognitive Assessment
Objective cognitive performance was assessed using three validated computer-administered neuropsychological tasks administered at baseline and at six weeks under standardized conditions. Working memory was assessed using the N-back task at the 2-back difficulty level, which requires participants to identify whether each stimulus matches the stimulus presented two items previously and is sensitive to working memory capacity and updating. Sustained attention was assessed using the Conners Continuous Performance Test Third Edition, a 14-minute continuous performance task with established reliability and validity for measuring sustained attention and impulsivity in adolescent and adult populations. Executive function was assessed using the computerized Trail Making Test comparing performance on Parts A and B, with the Part B minus Part A difference score used as the primary executive function measure. All three tasks were administered in the same fixed order at the same time of day between 2:00 PM and 4:00 PM at both assessment points to control for circadian variation in cognitive performance.
Self-Reported Academic Focus Assessment
Self-reported academic focus was measured using the Academic Self-Regulation subscale of the Motivated Strategies for Learning Questionnaire developed by Pintrich et al. (1991), a validated and widely used measure of academic motivation and self-regulatory engagement with established internal consistency reliability. The subscale was administered at baseline and at six weeks. Additionally participants completed a daily 10-point academic focus rating immediately following their last academic class each day throughout the six-week intervention period, providing a fine-grained longitudinal measure of experienced academic focus across the intervention.
Data Analysis
Independent samples t-tests compared post-intervention cognitive task performance scores and MSLQ subscale scores between conditions with baseline scores included as covariates using analysis of covariance. Effect sizes were calculated using Cohen's d for all pairwise comparisons. Daily academic focus ratings were analyzed using multilevel modeling with days nested within participants and condition as the between-person predictor, allowing examination of both group differences in average academic focus and group differences in trajectories of academic focus change across the six weeks. All analyses were conducted in R version 4.3.1 using the lme4 package for multilevel modeling. The significance threshold was set at alpha equals 0.05 for all tests.
Results
Forty-two of the 44 recruited participants completed the full six-week study. Two participants in the intermittent fasting condition withdrew — one due to scheduling conflicts and one due to difficulty adhering to the protocol during a week of travel. Adherence among completing intermittent fasting participants was high, with participants reporting maintaining the eating window on an average of 5.8 of 7 days per week across the six-week period.

The ANCOVA comparing N-back accuracy between conditions at six weeks controlling for baseline performance was statistically significant. Participants in the intermittent fasting condition showed significantly higher N-back accuracy at six weeks compared to control participants. The effect size was in the medium range.
The ANCOVA comparing Conners CPT performance between conditions showed a trend toward significance that did not reach the conventional alpha threshold, with the intermittent fasting condition showing better sustained attention performance. The effect size was in the small to medium range.
Trail Making Test performance did not differ significantly between conditions at six weeks after controlling for baseline performance. The effect size was small and not clinically meaningful.
The ANCOVA comparing MSLQ academic self-regulation scores between conditions at six weeks was statistically significant. Intermittent fasting participants reported significantly higher academic self-regulation at six weeks compared to control participants. The effect size was in the medium range.
Multilevel modeling of daily academic focus ratings revealed a statistically significant condition by time interaction. Academic focus ratings increased across the six weeks at a significantly faster rate in the intermittent fasting condition compared to the control condition, with the group difference in trajectories emerging gradually over the first three weeks and stabilizing thereafter.

Discussion
These results provide partial but meaningful support for the hypothesis that a six-week 16:8 intermittent fasting protocol improves cognitive performance and self-reported academic focus in collegiate club athletes relative to athletes maintaining habitual eating patterns. The significant improvement in working memory performance is consistent with Mattson et al.'s (2018) neurobiological framework proposing that repeated fasting cycles increase brain-derived neurotrophic factor and promote hippocampal synaptic plasticity over multi-week periods. The non-significant trend in sustained attention suggests a possible benefit that the current sample of 42 completers was insufficiently powered to detect reliably. The absence of an executive function effect as measured by Trail Making Test may reflect ceiling effects in a non-clinical college sample that limit sensitivity to real neurobiological changes.
The significant improvement in self-reported academic self-regulation and the significant condition by time interaction in daily academic focus ratings extend the laboratory cognitive findings to naturalistic academic experience and suggest that the working memory improvements detected in the laboratory translate to experienced differences in academic engagement during actual coursework. This convergence between objective neuropsychological assessment and ecological momentary assessment of academic focus across six weeks substantially strengthens the ecological validity of the findings.
The non-significant CPT finding warrants cautious interpretation rather than acceptance of the null hypothesis. The effect size of small to medium magnitude and the p-value approaching but not crossing the significance threshold together suggest that sustained attention may genuinely improve with 16:8 intermittent fasting in collegiate athletes but that the current study lacked sufficient statistical power to detect the effect reliably.
Limitations
Several methodological limitations must be acknowledged. First the single-site single-university design limits generalizability. Second self-reported dietary adherence through daily logs cannot be objectively verified — blood or urinary ketone monitoring at multiple timepoints would provide biochemical verification that participants were achieving the metabolic state associated with intermittent fasting. Third the absence of dietary composition monitoring means that differences in macronutrient intake between conditions cannot be ruled out as contributing factors. Fourth the six-week duration may be insufficient to detect the full neurobiological adaptation proposed by Mattson et al. (2018). Fifth the assessment of athletic performance outcomes was not included in the study design — the possibility that cognitive improvements were accompanied by decrements in athletic performance cannot be assessed from these data, which is a meaningful limitation for a study whose practical implications concern athletes specifically.
Implications
These findings have direct relevance for sports dietitians advising collegiate athletes on performance nutrition, collegiate athletic department staff who develop dietary guidance for student-athletes, and student health services professionals working with the dual academic and athletic demands of collegiate athlete populations. For sports dietitians the results provide preliminary randomized controlled trial evidence that 16:8 intermittent fasting produces working memory improvements and academic self-regulation benefits in collegiate club athletes over six weeks without observed adverse effects on adherence or athletic participation. Collegiate athletic departments developing dietary guidelines for student-athletes should note that this study provides the first experimental evidence bearing directly on the cognitive academic dimension of dietary timing in their population. Future research should replicate this design across multiple universities and sport types, include objective dietary adherence verification through ketone monitoring, measure athletic performance outcomes alongside cognitive outcomes, and extend the intervention to a full academic semester.
Conclusion
This randomized controlled trial found significant improvements in working memory performance and self-reported academic self-regulation following a six-week 16:8 intermittent fasting protocol in collegiate club athletes compared to athletes maintaining habitual eating patterns, alongside a non-significant trend toward improved sustained attention and a null finding for executive function. The convergence of laboratory cognitive improvements with naturalistic daily academic focus ratings showing faster improvement trajectories in the intermittent fasting condition strengthens the ecological validity of the cognitive findings. These results provide the first randomized controlled trial evidence that 16:8 intermittent fasting may offer cognitive and academic focus benefits for collegiate athletes and suggest that sports dietitians and athletic departments should consider this protocol as a candidate for evidence-based dietary guidance for student-athletes seeking to optimize performance across both athletic and academic domains.
