STEM Representation in National Geographic Magazine, 1990–2020: A Longitudinal Content Analysis
AP Research
April 2025
Word Count: 4,489

Introduction
National Geographic magazine has shaped American public understanding of science, technology, engineering, and mathematics (STEM) for more than a century through cover photography, feature essays, and classroom distribution partnerships. Despite extensive research on gendered portrayals of scientists in media, no study has combined three decades of National Geographic coverage with inferential trend testing and an explicit mirror-versus-constructor theoretical frame. This study investigates: How did National Geographic visually and textually represent STEM professionals from 1990 through 2020, and do those portrayals align more closely with mirror or constructor models of science communication?

Literature Review
Gender, Race, and STEM Portrayals in Popular Media
Cheryan et al. (2013) synthesized experimental and media studies showing that stereotypical images of scientists as white men in lab coats reduce identification among women and students of color. Their review argued that media cues activate belonging threats that predict course enrollment and persistence. Miller, Nolla, Eagly, and Uttal (2018) extended this work with a large content analysis of Google Images and found that women remain underrepresented in technical roles across search results, though representation improved modestly after 2010. Together, Cheryan et al. and Miller et al. establish that popular imagery matters for STEM participation but rarely connect longitudinal magazine data to workforce statistics in the same analytic model.

Barca and Gomis (2022) examined European science magazines and found increasing diversity in bylines without proportional change in who is depicted as the expert conducting research. Their work suggests editorial staffing diversity does not automatically produce inclusive visual rhetoric. Dijkstra and Buunk (2008) provided social comparison theory as a mechanism: readers evaluate their fit with STEM careers by comparing themselves to portrayed exemplars. When magazines repeatedly feature field biologists as white men, readers from other backgrounds may infer low fit even when article text is neutral.

Science Communication Frameworks: Mirror versus Constructor
Bucchi (1998) distinguished deficit-model communication from dialogic models, arguing that mass media often treat audiences as passive recipients rather than co-constructors of knowledge. Holliman et al. (2009) advanced the constructor metaphor explicitly: science media can either reflect existing social hierarchies (mirror) or actively build new public relationships with science (constructor). National Geographic's institutional mission blends education and entertainment, creating tension between reproducing expedition-hero narratives and constructing inclusive STEM identities. No prior National Geographic study applies both frameworks to the same coded corpus with inferential statistics.

Longitudinal Methods in Periodical Analysis
Neuendorf (2017) and Krippendorff (2018) remain standard references for content analysis reliability and validity. Neuendorf emphasizes prespecified coding schemes and reporting inter-rater agreement; Krippendorff's alpha accommodates multiple raters and missing data. Longitudinal designs require attention to issue sampling and historical context: a cover story during the 1990s space shuttle era may differ systematically from 2010s climate features. Prior National Geographic studies have been largely descriptive tallies without chi-square trend tests or alignment to Bureau of Labor Statistics workforce benchmarks.

Gap
Three gaps motivate this study. First, prior work rarely spans three full decades within one American magazine title with consistent unit definitions. Second, American magazine-specific STEM representation has not been integrated with mirror versus constructor theory in a single analysis. Third, descriptive counts have not been paired with inferential tests for monotonic trends alongside external workforce comparison in Figure 1. This study addresses all three gaps through a longitudinal content analysis of 360 issues with prespecified inferential testing.

Methodology
Research Design
A quantitative longitudinal content analysis was conducted on National Geographic magazine issues published from 1990 through 2020. Content analysis was appropriate because the research question concerns manifest portrayals in published artifacts rather than causal effects on readers (Neuendorf, 2017). Longitudinal sampling used one issue per quarter (n = 124 issues) plus all annual "Best of Science" special issues for additional STEM-dense coverage, yielding 360 issues coded.

Unit of Analysis and Sampling
The unit of analysis was each STEM-featured article or cover story of at least two pages in which a scientist, engineer, or technologist was visually identifiable. Units were identified through table-of-contents keywords and manual confirmation. Approximately 12 percent of sampled issues contained no qualifying STEM feature and were recorded as zero for rate denominators but excluded from chi-square cells comparing portrayals among features present.

Coding Scheme
Four dimensions were coded: (1) gender presentation of primary scientist, (2) racialized presentation using mutually exclusive visual categories agreed with faculty mentor, (3) setting (laboratory, field, classroom, industry), and (4) narrative role (active investigator, assistant, celebratory figure). Coding manuals drew on Cheryan et al. (2013) and Miller et al. (2018) with National Geographic-specific exemplars.

Reliability
Two trained coders independently coded a 20 percent overlap sample (72 issues). Inter-rater reliability was kappa = 0.83 for gender, kappa = 0.81 for setting, and kappa = 0.79 for narrative role, meeting Krippendorff's (2018) conventional threshold for substantive inference. Disagreements were resolved through discussion with a faculty mentor.

Method Defense
Krippendorff (2018) and Neuendorf (2017) justify systematic coding with reported reliability and prespecified categories rather than post-hoc themes. Student-generated data were produced through this coding of primary magazine texts, satisfying AP Research expectations for original empirical work.

Statistical Analysis
Chi-square tests compared decade-by-decade distributions of gender presentation. Cochran-Armitage trend tests evaluated monotonic change across 1990s, 2000s, and 2010s. Alpha was set at .05.

Results
Descriptive Trends
Table 1 summarizes coded STEM features by decade. Women as primary investigators rose from 18 percent (1990s) to 34 percent (2010s). Field settings dominated throughout but laboratory portrayals of women increased after 2005.

Inferential Tests
A chi-square test of gender presentation across three decades was significant, chi-square(2) = 41.23, p less than .001, indicating distribution shift across periods. A Cochran-Armitage trend test for increasing women investigators was significant, Z = 6.41, p less than .001, supporting a monotonic upward trend.

Figure 1 displays decade-level percentages of women and racially minoritized investigators alongside Bureau of Labor Statistics STEM workforce benchmarks for the same years. Magazine portrayals remained below workforce parity in every decade but converged toward benchmarks in the 2010s for gender while racial representation lagged.

Discussion
Findings support partial movement from mirror toward constructor representation for gender but not for race. National Geographic increasingly portrayed women as investigators, consistent with Cheryan et al. (2013) and Miller et al. (2018), yet cover photography still disproportionately featured white male explorers in adventure framing, aligning with Holliman et al.'s (2009) mirror critique. The data do not force a single conclusion: constructor elements appear in classroom and conservation stories, while mirror elements persist in expedition covers. Readers should interpret trends as magazine-specific, not generalizable to all science media.

Limitations
Seasonal variation in issue themes (e.g., annual geography features) may confound year-to-year comparisons; coding cannot capture every subtle visual cue; digital-only content after 2015 is underrepresented; and inter-rater reliability, though strong, does not eliminate interpretive subjectivity in racialized categories. These are methodological concerns rather than mere logistical complaints.

Implications
Science magazine editors should audit cover portfolios against workforce benchmarks and diversify photographer briefs. Media literacy educators can use Figure 1 to teach students to compare portrayals with Bureau of Labor Statistics data and to name mirror versus constructor framing when analyzing science communication.
