To what extent does peer mentoring improve first-year college students' sense of academic belonging?

Prior research on college transition has established that belonging predicts retention and academic performance. Smith (2019) found that first-year students who reported higher belonging also earned higher GPAs in their second semester. Jones and Lee (2020) demonstrated that peer support programs reduced dropout intentions among underrepresented students. Martinez et al. (2021) argued that mentoring relationships provide social capital that formal advising cannot replicate. Chen (2022) concluded that institutions should invest in structured peer networks rather than only faculty-led orientation. Williams (2023) noted that belonging interventions work best when they are sustained across the first year rather than offered only during welcome week. Davis and Kim (2020) reported that peer mentors helped students navigate registration and financial aid processes. Thompson (2021) showed that near-peer mentors were especially effective for first-generation students. Patel (2022) established that mentoring frequency correlated with persistence into the second year. Nguyen et al. (2023) suggested that mentoring programs should include training on inclusive communication. Roberts (2024) indicated that students valued mentors who shared similar academic interests. Anderson (2023) found that mentor training improved the quality of peer interactions in residence halls. Lee and Park (2022) demonstrated that structured peer programs increased first-year persistence rates in large public universities. Taken together, these studies suggest a measurable gap: few studies have combined survey-based belonging measures with interviews about how students experience peer mentoring in their first semester. This gap motivates the present mixed-methods design.

For this study I recruited 30 first-year students at a public university through course announcements and email lists. Participants completed an online survey about belonging and mentoring experiences. I administered the survey during weeks four and five of the fall semester. Interviews were conducted with a purposive subsample of twelve participants. Each participant received a unique identifier, and all responses were stored in a password-protected database. Each interview lasted 30 to 45 minutes and was audio recorded with consent. I used thematic analysis to code transcripts. First, I read all transcripts for familiarization. Then I generated initial codes. Next, I searched for themes across codes. Finally, I reviewed themes against the research question.

The survey showed that 73 percent of participants reported at least one meaningful peer mentoring interaction in their first semester. Analysis revealed a significant positive association between mentoring frequency and belonging scores (r = 0.52, p < 0.01). Theme 1 emerged from interviews: students described mentors as "someone who already survived the class." Theme 2 indicated that informal hallway conversations mattered as much as scheduled meetings. Participants reported that mentoring reduced isolation during midterm weeks. Students who met with mentors weekly scored higher on belonging than students who met monthly. Qualitative excerpts highlighted that mentors normalized academic struggle during the first semester.

These findings suggest that peer mentoring supports belonging in ways consistent with Smith (2019) and Jones and Lee (2020). One possible explanation is that near-peer mentors reduce status distance compared with faculty advisors. Another interpretation is that mentors provide navigational knowledge about campus resources that formal orientation does not cover. A limitation of this study is the convenience sample from one campus. The results cannot establish causation because the design was cross-sectional. Self-report measures may overstate mentoring frequency. Future studies should include longitudinal follow-up across multiple institutions. Practitioners should document mentoring activities carefully so that programs can be evaluated with consistent measures across sites.

In conclusion, this study found that structured peer mentoring is associated with stronger belonging among first-year students. The mixed-methods evidence strengthens confidence in the pattern observed in the survey and interviews together. Taken together, the survey and interview data answer the research question affirmatively. Future research should test mentoring interventions with randomized assignment and measure persistence into the second year. Educators and student affairs staff can use these findings to design first-year programs that pair new students with trained peer mentors. Policymakers should consider funding peer mentoring as a scalable strategy for improving first-year retention at public universities nationwide. In summary, peer mentoring remains a promising practice for first-year student success.
