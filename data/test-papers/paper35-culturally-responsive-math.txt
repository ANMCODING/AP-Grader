The Effect of Culturally Responsive Assessment Practices on Mathematics Achievement and Math Identity in Black Middle School Students
AP Research
April 2025
Word Count: 4,812
Introduction
Mathematics achievement gaps between Black students and white students remain among the most persistent and well-documented inequities in American education. As of 2022 Black eighth graders scored an average of 26 points below white eighth graders on the National Assessment of Educational Progress mathematics assessment, a gap that has narrowed only marginally over the past two decades despite significant policy attention (NAEP, 2022). Researchers have proposed numerous explanations for this gap including resource inequities, teacher quality disparities, stereotype threat effects, and the cultural mismatch between the dominant forms of mathematical knowledge valued in schools and the mathematical knowledge and reasoning practices present in Black communities (Martin, 2009). One dimension that has received increasing research attention is assessment practice — specifically whether the ways mathematics is assessed in schools systematically disadvantages Black students not because of differences in mathematical knowledge but because of differences between the cultural contexts in which mathematical knowledge is assessed and the cultural contexts in which Black students develop and demonstrate their knowledge. This research investigates the following question: To what extent do culturally responsive assessment practices produce differential effects on mathematics performance scores and mathematics identity in Black sixth and seventh grade students compared to standard assessment practices at one Title I middle school?
This question matters because mathematics assessment reform is a lever that classroom teachers can directly implement without systemic policy changes. If culturally responsive assessment practices produce measurably better mathematics performance and identity outcomes in Black students, this provides direct guidance for how mathematics teachers can address achievement gaps within their existing classrooms.
Literature Review
The Mathematics Achievement Gap and Its Causes
The Black-white mathematics achievement gap has been studied extensively. Ladson-Billings (2006) argued in her landmark paper that achievement gaps are actually manifestations of an education debt — the accumulated historical underfunding of schools serving Black students — and that focusing on gaps obscures the systemic causes of differential outcomes. Martin (2009) developed a framework of mathematics identity, arguing that Black students' engagement with mathematics is shaped not only by their mathematical competence but by their sense of whether mathematics is a domain that is available, appropriate, and affirming for people like them. These two frameworks — education debt and mathematics identity — suggest that addressing achievement gaps requires attending to cultural and identity dimensions of mathematics learning that are not captured by standard assessments.
Culturally Responsive Teaching and Assessment
Gay (2010) developed the framework of culturally responsive teaching, defining it as using students' cultural knowledge, prior experiences, and performance styles to make learning more appropriate and effective. The assessment dimension of culturally responsive practice has been less studied than instructional dimensions. Montenegro and Jankowski (2017) reviewed culturally responsive assessment in higher education and argued that standard assessment practices assume a monocultural norm that systematically disadvantages students whose knowledge is expressed differently than the dominant form. Hammond (2015) specifically addressed culturally responsive assessment in K-12 contexts and argued that standard mathematics assessments often require language proficiency and familiarity with specific cultural contexts that function as construct-irrelevant variance — penalizing students for their cultural background rather than measuring their mathematical knowledge.
Mathematics Identity in Black Students
Mathematics identity — the beliefs students hold about their belonging, competence, and value in mathematics — has been identified as a significant predictor of mathematics persistence and performance beyond measured ability. Nasir et al. (2008) examined the mathematics identities of Black high school students and found that students who developed strong mathematics identities despite attending schools where Black students were underrepresented in advanced mathematics courses drew on community and family resources that schools did not recognize. Berry (2008) conducted a phenomenological study of eight Black middle school boys who were successful in mathematics and found that their success was sustained by strong mathematics identities developed through family support, community recognition, and relationships with teachers who recognized their mathematical competence beyond test performance.
Stereotype Threat in Mathematics Assessment
Stereotype threat — the threat of confirming a negative stereotype about one's group — has been shown to undermine performance on standardized assessments specifically for groups targeted by negative stereotypes. Steele and Aronson (1995) conducted the landmark experiments demonstrating that Black college students performed significantly worse on verbal tests when race was made salient compared to when it was not. Walton and Spencer (2009) conducted a meta-analysis of stereotype threat studies and found that removing stereotype threat cues from assessment contexts produced performance gains equivalent to closing the achievement gap, suggesting that standard assessment contexts themselves may suppress Black student performance below actual capability.
Gap
While Gay (2010), Hammond (2015), and Montenegro and Jankowski (2017) have theorized that culturally responsive assessment practices should produce better outcomes for marginalized students, and while Steele and Aronson (1995) and Walton and Spencer (2009) have demonstrated that stereotype threat in assessment contexts suppresses Black student performance, no study has directly tested whether implementing culturally responsive assessment practices in a regular middle school mathematics classroom produces measurable differences in both mathematics performance scores and mathematics identity simultaneously, using a comparison design that controls for teacher effects. The gap between theoretical frameworks and empirical classroom evidence is the central motivation for this study.
Methodology
Research Design
This study used a quasi-experimental comparison design with a mixed-methods component. In the comparison condition students completed standard end-of-unit mathematics assessments. In the intervention condition students completed assessments that had been redesigned using culturally responsive assessment principles. The quasi-experimental design was chosen because random assignment of students to assessment conditions within a single classroom was not feasible — all students in each class received the same assessment. Instead two sections of sixth grade mathematics and two sections of seventh grade mathematics taught by the same teacher were used, with one sixth grade section and one seventh grade section receiving standard assessments and the other sections receiving culturally responsive assessments. This controlled for teacher effects while maintaining classroom integrity.
Culturally Responsive Assessment Design
Standard assessments were redesigned using the culturally responsive assessment framework described by Hammond (2015). Redesign principles included: embedding mathematical problems in contexts drawn from Black cultural and community life rather than generic mainstream contexts, eliminating construct-irrelevant language complexity that penalized English Language Learners and students with non-mainstream language backgrounds, providing multiple response formats including written explanation, oral justification, and visual representation, and removing stereotype threat cues identified through Steele and Aronson's (1995) research including reducing the salience of race and testing contexts in the assessment environment. The redesigned assessments were reviewed by a mathematics education specialist and a Black community advocate for cultural authenticity.
Participants
Participants were 94 Black sixth and seventh grade students at one Title I middle school in a large Midwestern city. Forty-seven students were in the standard assessment condition and 47 in the culturally responsive assessment condition. The school served a student population that was 78 percent Black and 89 percent eligible for free or reduced-price lunch. Informed consent was obtained from all participants and parental consent in both English and Spanish. The study was approved by the school district IRB.
Quantitative Strand
Mathematics performance was measured using scores on four unit assessments administered during the study period. Both conditions took parallel assessments covering the same mathematical content at the same level of difficulty — the redesign affected only the cultural contexts and response formats not the mathematical rigor. An independent samples t-test compared mean assessment scores between conditions. A one-way ANCOVA controlled for prior mathematics performance using the previous semester's mathematics grades. Effect size was calculated using Cohen's d.
Mathematics identity was measured using the Mathematics Identity Scale adapted from Nasir et al. (2008), a validated 15-item instrument measuring belonging, competence, and value in mathematics rated on a 5-point Likert scale.
Qualitative Strand
A purposive subsample of 16 students (8 per condition) was selected for semi-structured interviews exploring how students experienced the assessments, how they described their relationship with mathematics, and whether they noticed differences between the assessment formats. Interviews were conducted in students' preferred language, audio-recorded, and transcribed verbatim. Thematic coding followed Braun and Clarke (2006). Inter-rater reliability was established by having a second coder independently code four transcripts. Cohen's kappa = 0.82, indicating strong agreement.
Data Integration
Quantitative results and qualitative themes were integrated through a joint display following Creswell and Plano Clark (2017) to identify convergence, divergence, and elaboration across the two data strands.
Results
Quantitative Findings
The independent samples t-test comparing mathematics assessment scores between conditions was statistically significant: t(92) = 3.47, p = .001, Cohen's d = 0.71. Students in the culturally responsive assessment condition scored significantly higher (M = 82.4, SD = 11.3) than students in the standard assessment condition (M = 74.8, SD = 12.1). The ANCOVA controlling for prior mathematics performance remained significant: F(1, 91) = 10.23, p = .002, partial eta squared = .10. The effect persisted after controlling for pre-existing differences in mathematics achievement.
Mathematics identity scores were also significantly higher in the culturally responsive assessment condition: t(92) = 2.89, p = .005, d = 0.59. Students in the CRA condition reported stronger mathematics identity (M = 3.87, SD = 0.71) compared to the standard condition (M = 3.42, SD = 0.79).
Qualitative Findings
Three themes emerged from interview analysis.
Theme 1 — Recognition versus alienation: Students in the CRA condition frequently described feeling recognized and seen in the assessment contexts, noting that problem contexts drawn from their communities made the mathematics feel relevant and accessible. Quote from Jaylen (CRA condition): "The math was about things I know. Like the problem about the barbershop, I understood that right away. I could actually think about the math instead of trying to figure out what they were talking about." Students in the standard condition more frequently described feeling alienated from assessment contexts.
Theme 2 — Confidence and identity: CRA condition students more frequently described feeling capable and confident during assessments. Quote from Amara (CRA condition): "I felt like I was actually good at math when I was taking those tests. Not like I was just trying to get through it."
Theme 3 — Awareness of assessment fairness: Both conditions showed awareness that assessments could be more or less fair. Several standard condition students explicitly identified cultural context as a source of unfairness. Quote from Marcus (standard condition): "Sometimes the problems are about stuff I don't know about. Like golf or sailing. I don't know anything about that. But if it was about basketball or music I'd be fine."
Joint Display
The quantitative performance advantage for the CRA condition was confirmed and extended by qualitative Themes 1 and 2 — students in the CRA condition described experiencing the very mechanisms theorized by Hammond (2015) and Steele and Aronson (1995), providing phenomenological evidence for the pathways through which CRA practices produce performance benefits.
Discussion
These findings provide empirical support for the theoretical frameworks linking culturally responsive assessment to performance and identity outcomes in Black middle school mathematics students. The medium-large effect size on mathematics performance (d = 0.71) and the medium effect size on mathematics identity (d = 0.59) after controlling for prior achievement suggest that assessment redesign alone — without changes to instruction, curriculum, or other dimensions of schooling — produces meaningful differences in both performance and identity outcomes. The qualitative findings reveal the mechanisms: students in the CRA condition experienced recognition and reduced construct-irrelevant cognitive load, consistent with Hammond's (2015) framework and Steele and Aronson's (1995) stereotype threat research.
The finding that students in the standard condition explicitly identified cultural context as a source of unfairness — recognizing that assessments about golf and sailing disadvantaged them while assessments about basketball and music would not — is particularly striking because it suggests that students have sophisticated intuitions about construct-irrelevant variance that align with the theoretical frameworks developed by academic researchers.
Limitations
The quasi-experimental design with non-random assignment to conditions means that observed differences could reflect pre-existing differences between classes beyond prior mathematics achievement, which was controlled statistically but not through randomization. The single school single teacher design limits generalizability — the effect may be specific to this teacher's implementation of CRA principles, this school's specific student population, or this specific combination of mathematical content and cultural contexts. The mathematics identity instrument was adapted rather than newly validated for this age group and population. The study examined four units over approximately 12 weeks — it is unknown whether the performance and identity effects would persist or grow over a full academic year. The qualitative subsample of 16 students represents a small fraction of the full sample and qualitative findings may not represent all student experiences.
Implications
These findings have direct relevance for mathematics teachers working with Black students, mathematics curriculum developers, school administrators evaluating assessment policies, and teacher educators designing culturally responsive pedagogy training. For mathematics teachers the results suggest that assessment redesign using culturally responsive principles is a feasible within-classroom intervention that produces meaningful performance and identity benefits. The effect size found here (d = 0.71 on performance) is comparable to high-impact instructional interventions studied in the broader education research literature, suggesting that assessment reform deserves attention alongside instructional reform in efforts to address mathematics achievement gaps. Mathematics curriculum developers should consider how assessment contexts in standardized unit tests default to mainstream cultural contexts that function as construct-irrelevant variance for Black students. Future research should conduct randomized controlled trials across multiple schools and teachers, examine effects across a full academic year, and investigate whether effects generalize to standardized state mathematics assessments.
Conclusion
This quasi-experimental mixed-methods study found significant positive effects of culturally responsive assessment practices on both mathematics performance (d = 0.71) and mathematics identity (d = 0.59) in Black sixth and seventh grade students after controlling for prior achievement. Qualitative findings revealed that students in the culturally responsive condition experienced recognition and reduced cognitive interference that they explicitly attributed to the cultural relevance of assessment contexts. These findings provide the first empirical evidence in a K-12 classroom setting that assessment redesign using culturally responsive principles produces meaningful differences in both performance and identity outcomes and suggest that assessment reform deserves serious attention in efforts to address mathematics achievement gaps.
