Epigenetics and Disease: A Comprehensive Review of DNA Methylation in Cancer and Neurodegeneration
AP Research
April 2025
Word Count: 3,450
Introduction
Epigenetics refers to heritable changes in gene expression that do not involve alterations to the underlying DNA sequence. DNA methylation, histone modification, and chromatin remodeling are central mechanisms through which environmental exposures and developmental timing influence disease risk. Cancer and neurodegenerative disorders such as Alzheimer's disease have been linked to aberrant methylation at promoter regions and repetitive elements. This paper aims to review current understanding of how epigenetic dysregulation contributes to oncogenesis and neuronal loss, synthesizing findings from human cohort studies, animal models, and in vitro work published over the last fifteen years.
Literature Review
DNA Methylation in Cancer
Hypermethylation of CpG islands at tumor suppressor promoters is a hallmark of many solid tumors (Jones and Baylin, 2007). Global hypomethylation of repetitive sequences may promote genomic instability. Studies in colorectal, breast, and lung cancer consistently report coordinated shifts in the methylome during progression from adenoma to carcinoma.
Neurodegeneration and Epigenetics
In Alzheimer's disease, methylation changes at genes involved in amyloid processing and synaptic plasticity have been documented in postmortem cortex (Lardenoije et al., 2015). Environmental factors including stress and diet may accelerate epigenetic aging clocks in the brain.
Gap
While individual pathways are well described, integrated models linking methylation, chromatin state, and clinical phenotype across disease types remain incomplete. This review addresses that synthesis need without collecting new primary data.
Methods
Peer-reviewed articles were identified through PubMed and Google Scholar using search terms including epigenetics, DNA methylation, cancer, and neurodegeneration. Inclusion criteria required human or mammalian model data published between 2010 and 2024. Sources were summarized thematically; no statistical meta-analysis was performed.
Discussion
Cross-disease comparisons suggest shared machinery but distinct locus-specific vulnerabilities. Clinical translation through methylation biomarkers remains promising but validation cohorts are limited.
Limitations
This review relies on published secondary sources only. Search strategy was not preregistered. Language bias toward English-language journals may omit relevant work.
Implications
Clinicians and researchers should interpret methylation findings in tissue-specific context. Future work should prioritize longitudinal designs.
Conclusion
Epigenetic mechanisms are central to both cancer and neurodegeneration; this review consolidates major themes without proposing a new empirical investigation.
