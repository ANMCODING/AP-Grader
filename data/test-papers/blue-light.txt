The Effect of Blue Light Exposure Duration on Melatonin Suppression and Sleep Onset Latency in High School Athletes
AP Research
April 2025
Word Count: 4,521
Introduction
Sleep is one of the most critical factors in athletic recovery and performance. High school athletes are a population particularly vulnerable to sleep disruption given the combined demands of academic work, training schedules, and social obligations. Over the past decade, the proliferation of smartphones, tablets, and laptop screens has introduced a novel environmental factor into adolescent sleep patterns — exposure to short-wavelength blue light in the hours before sleep. Blue light in the wavelength range of 450 to 490 nanometers is known to suppress melatonin secretion by the pineal gland through its action on intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells, delaying sleep onset and reducing total sleep duration (Cajochen et al., 2011; Gooley et al., 2011). Despite this established mechanism in adult populations, the dose-dependent relationship between blue light exposure duration and measurable sleep outcomes in adolescent athletes specifically has not been examined in a controlled setting. This research investigates the following question: To what extent does the duration of blue light screen exposure in the 90 minutes before sleep onset affect sleep onset latency and self-reported sleep quality in high school varsity athletes aged 15 to 18?
This question matters because athletic performance recovery is directly tied to sleep quality and duration (Mah et al., 2011), and coaches and sports medicine professionals currently lack empirical evidence specific to adolescent athletes to guide screen use recommendations before competition and training.
Literature Review
Blue Light and Melatonin Suppression
The mechanism by which blue light suppresses melatonin is well established. Intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells containing the photopigment melanopsin are maximally sensitive to light at approximately 480 nanometers, precisely within the blue light spectrum emitted by most LED screens (Berson et al., 2002). When stimulated, these cells send signals directly to the suprachiasmatic nucleus, the brain's master circadian clock, suppressing melatonin secretion and shifting the circadian phase toward wakefulness (Cajochen et al., 2011). Gooley et al. (2011) conducted a controlled experiment in which participants were exposed to room light or dim light in the 8 hours before sleep and found that room light exposure suppressed melatonin by 71.4% and shortened melatonin duration by 90 minutes. This established the sensitivity of the melatonin system to light exposure duration.
Blue Light and Adolescent Sleep
Adolescents are particularly vulnerable to circadian disruption for two reasons. First, puberty produces a biological shift in circadian phase preference toward eveningness, naturally pushing sleep onset later (Carskadon et al., 2004). Second, adolescents spend more time on screens than any other age group, with average daily screen time estimated at 7.7 hours in 2023 (Common Sense Media, 2023). Christensen et al. (2016) examined electronic device use before sleep in 9,846 Norwegian adolescents and found a significant dose-dependent relationship between pre-sleep screen time and sleep onset latency, with each additional hour of screen use associated with a 24-minute increase in the time to fall asleep. However Christensen et al. relied entirely on self-reported screen time and did not control for blue light wavelength specifically or for athletic conditioning as a variable.
Sleep and Athletic Performance
Athletic performance is acutely sensitive to sleep quality and duration. Mah et al. (2011) conducted a landmark study in which Stanford basketball players extended their sleep to 10 hours per night for five to seven weeks and found significant improvements in sprint times, shooting accuracy, and reaction time. Fullagar et al. (2015) conducted a systematic review of 19 studies and concluded that sleep deprivation consistently impairs reaction time, decision making, and maximal strength output in athletes. Vitale et al. (2019) specifically examined the sleep quality of collegiate athletes and found that 42% reported poor sleep quality as measured by the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index, with pre-sleep device use identified as the most frequently cited disruptive behavior.
Gap
While the mechanism of blue light melatonin suppression is established and the association between pre-sleep screen use and sleep outcomes in general adolescent populations is documented, no study has examined the dose-dependent effect of specifically controlled blue light exposure duration on objectively measured sleep onset latency in high school varsity athletes. Prior research has relied on self-reported screen time rather than controlled exposure conditions, has not distinguished blue light specifically from general screen use, and has not focused on athletic populations at the high school level. This study addresses all three gaps through a controlled within-subject repeated measures design using standardized blue light exposure conditions.
Methodology
Research Design
This study used a within-subject repeated measures design with three blue light exposure conditions tested across three separate evenings separated by 72-hour washout periods. The within-subject design was chosen because individual variation in baseline melatonin levels and sleep onset latency is substantial, and using each participant as their own control eliminates this variability and increases statistical power without requiring a larger sample (Cajochen et al., 2011). Each participant completed all three conditions in counterbalanced order using a Latin square design to control for order and carryover effects.
Participants
Participants were 18 varsity athletes from one high school in central Ohio, aged 15 to 18, with a mean age of 16.8 years. Inclusion criteria required active varsity sport participation, no diagnosed sleep disorder, no use of melatonin supplements or sleep medications, and habitual sleep onset between 10:00 PM and midnight as confirmed by a two-week actigraphy baseline period. Exclusion criteria included participants who had shifted time zones within the previous 30 days or who had consumed caffeine within 8 hours of any testing session. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants and parental consent was obtained for participants under 18. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the school district research ethics committee.
Materials and Procedure
Blue light exposure was standardized using identical tablet devices displaying a white screen at fixed brightness of 200 lux measured at eye level. The three conditions were 30 minutes of blue light exposure, 60 minutes of blue light exposure, and 90 minutes of blue light exposure, all ending 10 minutes before the participant's self-identified habitual sleep time. Participants wore blue-light-blocking glasses during all other evening hours to control for ambient light exposure outside of the experimental condition. Participants were instructed to remain in a dimly lit room (less than 10 lux) during all non-exposure periods on testing evenings. Sleep onset latency was measured using a consumer-grade wrist actigraphy device validated against polysomnography in adolescent populations (Meltzer et al., 2012). Sleep quality was self-reported using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index administered the morning after each testing session. Participants were instructed to avoid exercise, alcohol, and caffeine on all testing days.
Data Analysis
A one-way repeated measures ANOVA was used to test for significant differences in sleep onset latency across the three conditions. Sleep quality scores were analyzed using a Friedman test because the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index produces ordinal data that does not meet the normality assumptions of parametric tests. Effect size for the ANOVA was calculated using partial eta squared. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons used Bonferroni correction. All analyses were conducted in JASP version 0.18. Significance threshold was set at p less than 0.05.
Results
Table 1 presents mean sleep onset latency and mean Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index scores by condition.
Condition — Mean SOL (min) — SD — Mean PSQI Score — SD
30 min blue light — 18.4 — 7.2 — 5.1 — 1.8
60 min blue light — 26.7 — 8.9 — 6.4 — 2.1
90 min blue light — 38.2 — 11.4 — 8.2 — 2.6
One-way repeated measures ANOVA for sleep onset latency: F(2, 34) = 22.18, p less than .001, partial eta squared = .57, indicating a large statistically significant effect of blue light exposure duration on sleep onset latency.
Friedman test for sleep quality: chi-square(2) = 19.44, p less than .001, indicating a statistically significant effect of blue light exposure duration on self-reported sleep quality.
Bonferroni post-hoc comparisons for sleep onset latency revealed significant differences between all three conditions: 30 vs 60 minutes p = .018, 30 vs 90 minutes p less than .001, 60 vs 90 minutes p = .003. This confirms a linear dose-dependent relationship between blue light exposure duration and sleep onset latency.
Figure 1 displays mean sleep onset latency by condition as a bar graph with error bars representing standard deviation.
Figure 2 displays individual participant sleep onset latency trajectories across conditions showing that 16 of 18 participants showed monotonically increasing sleep onset latency with increasing blue light exposure duration.
Figure 3 presents the correlation between sleep onset latency and Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index scores across all conditions and participants, r = .61, p less than .001, confirming that longer sleep onset was associated with worse self-reported sleep quality.
Discussion
These results demonstrate a clear dose-dependent positive relationship between blue light exposure duration in the 90 minutes before sleep and sleep onset latency in high school varsity athletes. The large effect size (partial eta squared = .57) indicates that blue light duration is a practically meaningful predictor of sleep onset delay, not merely a statistically significant one. The linear pattern confirmed by Bonferroni post-hoc comparisons indicates that each additional 30 minutes of blue light exposure produces measurable additional sleep onset delay, without evidence of a ceiling effect within the tested range.
The correlation between sleep onset latency and Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index scores (r = .61, p less than .001) confirms that the measured objective delay in sleep onset translated into subjectively experienced worse sleep quality, strengthening the ecological validity of the actigraphy-based sleep onset measure. Two participants showed no dose-dependent pattern, suggesting that individual differences in circadian sensitivity to blue light may exist within athletic populations and warrant further investigation through genetic or chronotype analysis.
The magnitude of sleep onset delay observed at 90 minutes of blue light exposure (mean 38.2 minutes compared to 18.4 minutes at 30 minutes) is clinically significant for athletic populations given the established sensitivity of athletic performance metrics to sleep disruption (Mah et al., 2011; Fullagar et al., 2015). A mean sleep onset delay of nearly 40 minutes represents a substantial reduction in total sleep opportunity for high school athletes who must wake at fixed times for school.
Limitations
Several limitations must be acknowledged. First the sample of 18 athletes from a single school limits demographic generalizability. The findings may not apply to athletes in urban or rural settings, different sports, or different age ranges within adolescence. Second consumer-grade actigraphy, while validated in adolescent populations (Meltzer et al., 2012), is less precise than polysomnography and may misclassify some wake epochs as sleep or vice versa. Third the experiment controlled only for screen brightness and duration and did not manipulate wavelength directly. All blue light exposure used identical white screens at identical brightness, meaning the study cannot isolate the effect of the blue wavelength specifically from other aspects of screen stimulation such as cognitive engagement with content. Participants were asked to watch neutral nature documentaries to minimize cognitive engagement but complete standardization of content engagement was not possible. Fourth the three testing sessions occurred over nine days, and unmeasured variables such as academic stress, training load, and social events during that period could have influenced sleep outcomes independently of the experimental conditions.
Implications
These findings have direct practical relevance for coaches, athletic trainers, sports medicine professionals, and school administrators developing pre-competition and pre-training recovery protocols for adolescent athletes. The demonstration of a significant dose-dependent effect at all three tested durations suggests that any level of blue light exposure in the 90 minutes before sleep is associated with measurable sleep onset delay, with greater impact at longer exposures. Coaches establishing screen curfew recommendations for athletes before competition nights should note that even 30 minutes of screen exposure was associated with a mean 18.4-minute sleep onset delay. Athletic programs should consider implementing structured pre-sleep protocols that include a minimum 60-minute screen-free period before sleep. Future research should examine whether blue-light-blocking glasses during screen use can mitigate the observed effects, whether the dose-dependent relationship extends to exposures beyond 90 minutes, and whether sport-specific differences in circadian sensitivity exist across different athletic disciplines.
Conclusion
This study found a statistically significant dose-dependent positive relationship between blue light screen exposure duration and sleep onset latency in high school varsity athletes across three controlled exposure conditions, with a large effect size and linear dose-response pattern confirmed by post-hoc analysis. Self-reported sleep quality deteriorated in parallel with objective sleep onset delay, strengthening the ecological validity of the findings. These results provide the first controlled evidence of dose-dependent blue light effects on sleep outcomes specifically in adolescent athletic populations and offer an empirical foundation for pre-competition screen curfew recommendations currently lacking in sports medicine guidelines for high school athletes.

Appendix A — Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index Administration Protocol
The PSQI was administered via paper form the morning after each testing session between 7:00 and 8:00 AM. Participants completed the questionnaire independently without assistance. Forms were collected and scored by the researcher using the standard seven-component scoring protocol. Total scores range from 0 to 21 with scores above 5 indicating poor sleep quality.
Appendix B — Actigraphy Validation Reference
The actigraphy devices used in this study are the Philips Actiwatch Spectrum Plus, validated against polysomnography in adolescent populations by Meltzer et al. (2012) with a sensitivity of 88% and specificity of 64% for sleep-wake detection.
Appendix C — Latin Square Counterbalancing
Six condition orders were used across 18 participants with three participants assigned to each order. Order 1: 30 minutes, 60 minutes, 90 minutes. Order 2: 60 minutes, 90 minutes, 30 minutes. Order 3: 90 minutes, 30 minutes, 60 minutes. Order 4: 30 minutes, 90 minutes, 60 minutes. Order 5: 90 minutes, 60 minutes, 30 minutes. Order 6: 60 minutes, 30 minutes, 90 minutes.
