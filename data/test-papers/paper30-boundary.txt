The Effect of Cold Water Immersion Temperature on Muscle Recovery Markers and Perceived Recovery in Collegiate Club Athletes Following High-Intensity Exercise
AP Research
April 2025
Word Count: 4,743
Introduction
Cold water immersion is one of the most widely used post-exercise recovery modalities in competitive sports, with surveys suggesting that 80 to 95 percent of elite athletic programs incorporate some form of cold water exposure into their recovery protocols (Versey et al., 2013). The theoretical mechanisms by which cold water immersion promotes recovery include vasoconstriction reducing metabolic waste accumulation, decreased tissue temperature reducing inflammation and cell damage, and hydrostatic pressure effects reducing edema (Bleakley & Davison, 2010). Despite widespread adoption of cold water immersion in athletic training, the optimal water temperature for recovery has not been established. Existing studies have used water temperatures ranging from 5 to 20 degrees Celsius, making cross-study comparison difficult, and the dose-response relationship between immersion temperature and recovery outcomes has rarely been directly examined in a controlled within-subject design. This research investigates the following question: To what extent do three cold water immersion temperatures (8°C, 14°C, and 20°C) produce differential effects on perceived muscle soreness, countermovement jump performance, and creatine kinase levels in collegiate club athletes following standardized high-intensity exercise?
This question matters because athletic trainers and coaches who currently prescribe cold water immersion for athlete recovery lack evidence for specific temperature recommendations. Understanding the dose-response relationship between temperature and recovery outcomes could enable more precise evidence-based prescriptions for cold water immersion protocols.
Literature Review
Cold Water Immersion and Recovery Mechanisms
Cold water immersion has been studied as a recovery modality since the 1990s. Bleakley and Davison (2010) conducted a systematic review of the physiological mechanisms of cold water immersion and identified three primary pathways: vasoconstriction reducing blood flow and inflammatory mediator accumulation, tissue cooling reducing cellular metabolic rate and free radical production, and hydrostatic pressure providing mechanical compression that reduces edema. These mechanisms collectively produce the observed reductions in muscle soreness and recovery of muscle function typically attributed to cold water immersion. Wilcock, Cronin, and Hing (2006) conducted a meta-analysis of cold water immersion studies and found a significant positive effect on recovery of muscle function, with effect sizes in the moderate range (d = 0.34 to 0.68 depending on outcome measure). However Wilcock et al. noted that the studies included in their meta-analysis used widely varying temperatures making it impossible to identify an optimal temperature range.
Temperature Specificity in Cold Water Immersion
The question of optimal temperature for cold water immersion has been examined in a small number of studies with inconsistent findings. Ascensão et al. (2011) compared 10°C and 18°C immersion temperatures following soccer match simulation in male soccer players and found that the 10°C condition produced greater reductions in perceived fatigue and muscle soreness at 24 and 48 hours post-exercise but did not differ from 18°C on performance outcomes. Pointon et al. (2012) examined 10°C and 14°C immersion following cycling exercise in trained cyclists and found no significant difference between temperatures on any recovery outcome, suggesting that within this temperature range the specific temperature may not matter. These two studies used different exercise protocols, different populations, and different recovery outcomes making direct comparison difficult.
Creatine Kinase as a Recovery Biomarker
Creatine kinase is an enzyme that leaks from damaged muscle cells into the bloodstream following intense exercise and is widely used as a biochemical marker of exercise-induced muscle damage. Brancaccio et al. (2007) reviewed the use of creatine kinase as an exercise recovery biomarker and found that CK levels peak 24 to 72 hours after exercise involving significant eccentric loading and that the magnitude of CK elevation is correlated with the severity of muscle damage. Despite its widespread use as a recovery biomarker, surprisingly few cold water immersion studies have measured CK as an outcome — most studies rely on subjective soreness measures or performance tests that are more sensitive to perceived recovery than to actual tissue damage.
Countermovement Jump as a Performance Recovery Measure
Countermovement jump height is a neuromuscular performance measure that is sensitive to fatigue and muscle damage following high-intensity exercise. Gathercole et al. (2015) validated countermovement jump assessment for monitoring neuromuscular fatigue and found that CMJ height and reactive strength index measures showed significant decrements following high-intensity training that persisted for 24 to 48 hours. Cold water immersion has been shown to attenuate CMJ performance decrements in some studies but not others, with the inconsistency potentially reflecting the different temperatures and protocols used.
Gap
While cold water immersion is established as an effective recovery modality and while several studies have compared two specific temperatures, no study has directly compared three distinct temperatures using a within-subject design that controls for individual differences in recovery rate, using a battery of recovery outcomes that includes both subjective soreness, a performance measure, and a biochemical biomarker simultaneously, in a collegiate club athlete population that has not been previously studied. Existing two-temperature comparisons cannot establish the shape of the dose-response relationship between temperature and recovery, and the absence of CK measurement in most studies means the biochemical basis of recovery differences has not been established. This study addresses these gaps by conducting the first three-temperature within-subject comparison with a multi-modal outcome battery.
Methodology
Research Design
This study used a within-subject repeated measures crossover design with participants completing all three temperature conditions separated by two-week washout periods to allow complete recovery between sessions. The within-subject design was chosen over a between-subject design because individual variation in recovery rate, training status, and CK response is substantial, and using each participant as their own control dramatically increases statistical power with the same sample size (Cepeda et al., 2006). The two-week washout period was based on the recommendation of Brancaccio et al. (2007) that CK returns to baseline within 7 to 10 days following high-intensity exercise, providing an adequate safety margin. Condition order was counterbalanced across participants using a Latin square design to control for order effects. A single-blind design was implemented by having the water temperature set before participants entered the room and not displayed to participants during immersion.
Participants
Participants were 18 collegiate club athletes (12 male, 6 female) recruited from club sports teams at one university in Ohio. Inclusion criteria: active club sport participation for at least one full season, no musculoskeletal injury in the past six months, no regular use of cold water immersion or other recovery modalities. Exclusion criteria: cardiovascular conditions, cold hypersensitivity, any current medication affecting inflammation or muscle recovery. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study was approved by the university IRB.
Exercise Protocol
All participants completed the same standardized high-intensity exercise protocol designed to produce moderate to severe muscle damage consistent with prior cold water immersion research (Ascensão et al., 2011). The protocol consisted of 10 sets of 10 squat jumps at 50 percent of body weight, 400 meter sprint at maximal effort, and 5 sets of 15 eccentric leg press repetitions at 120 percent of one-repetition maximum. The eccentric focus was chosen specifically because eccentric exercise produces greater muscle damage and CK elevation than concentric exercise at equivalent loads (Brancaccio et al., 2007).
Cold Water Immersion Protocol
Participants were immersed to the waist in a temperature-controlled water bath for 15 minutes within 30 minutes of completing the exercise protocol. Water temperature was maintained at the target temperature (8°C, 14°C, or 20°C) within ±0.5°C using a commercial water chiller unit. Water temperature was verified at the start and end of each immersion using a calibrated digital thermometer.
Outcome Measures
Perceived muscle soreness was assessed using a 10-centimeter visual analog scale anchored by "no soreness" and "worst imaginable soreness" at baseline, immediately post-exercise, immediately post-immersion, 24 hours, and 48 hours post-exercise. Countermovement jump height was measured using a validated force plate (Kistler, Switzerland) at baseline and at 24 and 48 hours post-exercise. Three jumps were performed at each time point and the mean was used for analysis. Creatine kinase was measured from venous blood samples collected at baseline, immediately post-exercise, 24 hours, and 48 hours post-exercise. Blood was processed and CK was analyzed using a validated enzymatic assay.
Data Analysis
A one-way repeated measures ANOVA examined the effect of temperature condition on each outcome measure at each time point. Post-hoc Bonferroni-corrected pairwise comparisons identified specific temperature differences. Effect sizes were calculated using partial eta squared for ANOVAs and Cohen's d for pairwise comparisons. All analyses were conducted in R version 4.3.1. Significance threshold was set at p less than 0.05.
Results
Table 1 presents means and standard deviations for all outcome measures by condition and time point.
Perceived Muscle Soreness: A significant main effect of temperature was found at 24 hours post-exercise, F(2, 34) = 8.43, p = .001, partial eta squared = .33. Post-hoc comparisons showed that the 8°C condition produced significantly lower soreness than both the 14°C condition (p = .023, d = 0.71) and the 20°C condition (p = .003, d = 0.94). The 14°C and 20°C conditions did not differ significantly (p = .41). At 48 hours the temperature effect remained significant, F(2, 34) = 6.17, p = .005, partial eta squared = .27, with the same pattern of pairwise differences.
Countermovement Jump: At 24 hours there was a significant effect of temperature on CMJ height, F(2, 34) = 5.84, p = .007, partial eta squared = .26. The 8°C condition showed significantly better CMJ recovery than the 20°C condition (p = .012, d = 0.63) but not significantly different from the 14°C condition (p = .18). At 48 hours the temperature effect was not significant, F(2, 34) = 2.31, p = .115.
Creatine Kinase: CK levels showed a significant main effect of temperature at 24 hours, F(2, 34) = 7.12, p = .003, partial eta squared = .30. The 8°C condition produced significantly lower CK elevation than the 20°C condition (p = .008, d = 0.77). The 14°C condition was intermediate and did not differ significantly from either the 8°C (p = .19) or 20°C conditions (p = .22).
Discussion
These results demonstrate a consistent dose-response relationship between cold water immersion temperature and recovery outcomes, with the coldest temperature tested (8°C) producing the greatest reductions in perceived soreness, the best recovery of CMJ performance, and the lowest CK elevation. The dose-response pattern was clearest for perceived soreness and CK, where 8°C differed significantly from 20°C but not from 14°C, and 14°C did not differ from 20°C, suggesting that the primary threshold may lie between 8 and 14°C rather than showing a continuous linear relationship across the range. The large effect sizes for soreness and CK at 24 hours (partial eta squared = .33 and .30 respectively) indicate that temperature is a practically meaningful determinant of cold water immersion effectiveness not merely a statistically significant one.
The finding that CMJ recovery at 48 hours did not show a significant temperature effect while soreness and CK remained different is consistent with Gathercole et al.'s (2015) finding that CMJ performance recovers more rapidly than biochemical markers of muscle damage, potentially reflecting the recovery of neural function before complete resolution of tissue damage.
The results provide partial support for Ascensão et al.'s (2011) finding that cooler temperatures produce greater soreness reductions, and are inconsistent with Pointon et al.'s (2012) finding of no temperature difference between 10°C and 14°C. The discrepancy with Pointon et al. may reflect the different exercise protocol used — cycling exercise produces less muscle damage than the eccentric-focused protocol used here, potentially producing a floor effect in which any cold water exposure is sufficient when damage is modest.
Limitations
Several methodological limitations must be acknowledged. The single-blind design could not achieve full blinding because participants were aware they were being immersed in cold water, potentially creating expectancy effects that influenced perceived soreness ratings. The sample of 18 participants from one university's club sports programs limits demographic and sport-type generalizability. The study examined recovery over 48 hours only — it is unknown whether temperature effects persist or reverse beyond this window. The CK analysis used a commercially validated enzymatic assay but was not conducted by a certified clinical laboratory, which introduces some measurement uncertainty. The exercise protocol was standardized but participants varied in fitness level and training status which may have introduced variability in the damage response.
Implications
These findings are directly relevant for athletic trainers, sports medicine physicians, and strength and conditioning coaches who prescribe cold water immersion for athlete recovery. The demonstration of a significant dose-response relationship with 8°C producing consistently superior outcomes suggests that the common practice of using water temperatures of 15 to 20°C may be suboptimal for recovery. Athletic training facilities should consider maintaining water bath temperatures at or below 14°C when the primary goal is recovery from high-intensity exercise. The CK findings are particularly relevant for sports medicine physicians who use CK monitoring to assess recovery readiness in high-frequency training environments. Future research should examine the dose-response relationship below 8°C, extend the recovery monitoring period to 72 or 96 hours, and examine whether these temperature effects persist across multiple consecutive training days.
Conclusion
This within-subject repeated measures crossover study found a significant dose-response relationship between cold water immersion temperature and recovery outcomes following high-intensity eccentric exercise, with 8°C producing significantly superior outcomes to 20°C on perceived soreness, countermovement jump performance at 24 hours, and creatine kinase elevation, while 14°C was intermediate and did not differ significantly from either extreme. These findings provide the first three-temperature within-subject comparison of cold water immersion effects in collegiate club athletes and establish that temperature is a meaningful determinant of recovery effectiveness that warrants attention in evidence-based athletic recovery protocols.

Appendix A — Exercise Protocol Details
Full protocol: warm-up 10 minutes light cycling, 10 sets of 10 squat jumps with 60-second rest between sets, 400m sprint with 5-minute recovery, 5 sets of 15 eccentric leg press at 120% 1RM with 90-second rest between sets. Total protocol duration approximately 45 minutes.
Appendix B — Blood Collection Protocol
Venous blood collected from antecubital vein by trained phlebotomist at each time point. 5mL collected into EDTA tube, centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 minutes, serum stored at -20°C until batch analysis. CK measured using Roche Cobas enzymatic assay.
Appendix C — Latin Square Counterbalancing
Six condition orders used across 18 participants with 3 participants per order. Order 1: 8°C, 14°C, 20°C. Order 2: 14°C, 20°C, 8°C. Order 3: 20°C, 8°C, 14°C. Order 4: 8°C, 20°C, 14°C. Order 5: 20°C, 14°C, 8°C. Order 6: 14°C, 8°C, 20°C.
