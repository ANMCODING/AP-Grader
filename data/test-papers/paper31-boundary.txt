The History and Development of Artificial Intelligence: From Alan Turing to Large Language Models
AP Research
April 2025
Word Count: 3,156
Introduction
Artificial intelligence is one of the most transformative technologies in human history. From its theoretical foundations in the 1950s to the large language models that power today's AI assistants, the development of artificial intelligence has followed a path marked by periods of rapid progress, technological limitations, and renewed breakthroughs. Understanding the history of AI is important for understanding both the current capabilities and the future trajectory of this technology. This paper will provide an overview of the major milestones in the development of artificial intelligence from Alan Turing's foundational theoretical work to the emergence of large language models in the early 2020s.
Literature Review
Alan Turing and the Foundations of AI
Alan Turing is widely considered the father of modern computer science and artificial intelligence. In 1950 Turing published his landmark paper Computing Machinery and Intelligence in which he proposed the question Can machines think and proposed what he called the imitation game, now known as the Turing Test, as a way of evaluating whether a machine could exhibit intelligent behavior indistinguishable from a human (Turing, 1950). The Turing Test became foundational to AI research and philosophy of mind. Turing also contributed the theoretical framework of Turing machines which established the mathematical foundations of computation. Without Turing's foundational contributions the development of artificial intelligence as a field would have taken a very different path.
The Dartmouth Conference and Early AI
The field of artificial intelligence as an academic discipline is generally traced to the Dartmouth Summer Research Project on Artificial Intelligence in 1956. McCarthy et al. (1955) wrote the proposal for the Dartmouth conference and introduced the term artificial intelligence. The conference brought together leading researchers including John McCarthy, Marvin Minsky, Claude Shannon, and Nathaniel Rochester and established the major research agenda of early AI. Early AI research focused on symbolic reasoning, game playing, and problem solving. Early AI programs included the Logic Theorist developed by Newell and Simon which could prove mathematical theorems and the General Problem Solver which attempted to model human problem solving. These early programs demonstrated that computers could perform tasks that had previously been considered to require human intelligence.
AI Winters and Expert Systems
Following the initial optimism of early AI research, the field experienced periods of reduced funding and interest known as AI winters. The first AI winter occurred in the 1970s following the Lighthill Report which concluded that AI had failed to achieve its promised results and recommended significant reduction in AI funding in the United Kingdom. A second AI winter occurred in the late 1980s following the collapse of the expert systems market. Expert systems were AI programs that encoded human expertise in specific domains as rules and were commercially successful in the 1980s but proved to be brittle and expensive to maintain. The limitations of expert systems led to reduced investment in AI and a period of relative stagnation.
Machine Learning and Neural Networks
The revival of AI research in the 1990s and 2000s was driven primarily by advances in machine learning, particularly neural networks. LeCun et al. (1989) demonstrated that convolutional neural networks could learn to recognize handwritten digits, establishing the potential of neural network architectures for pattern recognition. The availability of large datasets and increased computational power in the 2000s enabled the training of increasingly large and capable neural networks. Krizhevsky, Sutskever, and Hinton (2012) demonstrated that deep neural networks trained on large datasets could dramatically outperform traditional computer vision approaches in the ImageNet challenge, triggering a wave of interest in deep learning.
Large Language Models
The most recent development in AI has been the emergence of large language models trained on massive text corpora. Vaswani et al. (2017) introduced the transformer architecture which became the foundation for modern large language models. Brown et al. (2020) demonstrated that GPT-3, a language model with 175 billion parameters, could perform a wide range of language tasks with little or no task-specific training, suggesting that scale alone could produce general language capabilities. The release of ChatGPT in November 2022 brought large language models to mainstream public attention and sparked widespread discussion of the potential and risks of AI.
Conclusion
The development of artificial intelligence has proceeded from Alan Turing's foundational theoretical contributions through the symbolic AI of the 1950s and 1960s, the AI winters of the 1970s and 1980s, the machine learning revolution of the 1990s and 2000s, and the current era of large language models. This history demonstrates that AI progress has been marked by cycles of optimism, disappointment, and renewed breakthrough driven by advances in algorithms, computational resources, and data availability. Understanding this history is essential for anyone seeking to understand where AI is today and where it may be heading in the future.
