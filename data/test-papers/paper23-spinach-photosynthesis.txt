The Effect of Light Intensity on Photosynthetic Rate in Spinach Leaf Disks: An Incomplete Laboratory Investigation
AP Research
April 2025
Word Count: 3,812
Introduction
Photosynthesis is the foundational biochemical process by which autotrophic organisms convert light energy into chemical energy stored in glucose, releasing oxygen as a byproduct. In introductory and advanced biology curricula, the floating leaf disk assay has become a standard classroom laboratory for estimating relative photosynthetic rate by measuring the time required for leaf disks cut from spinach (Spinacia oleracea) to rise in a sodium bicarbonate solution as oxygen produced during photosynthesis displaces intercellular air (Smith et al., 2018). Light intensity is among the most frequently manipulated independent variables in student investigations of photosynthesis because the light-dependent reactions of photosynthesis require photons to excite electrons in photosystem II and photosystem I (Taiz et al., 2015). According to ecological and physiological models, photosynthetic rate should increase with light intensity at low to moderate intensities until another factor such as carbon dioxide availability or enzyme saturation becomes limiting, at which point the rate plateaus (Farquhar et al., 1980).

Understanding how light intensity affects photosynthetic rate in spinach is not only pedagogically important but also connects to broader questions in plant physiology and environmental science. Agricultural productivity, forest carbon sequestration, and responses of vegetation to changing light environments under canopy gaps or cloud cover all depend on the relationship between light availability and carbon fixation (Lambers et al., 2008). Classroom investigations using spinach are attractive because spinach leaves are inexpensive, commercially available year-round, and produce reliable disks when punched with a cork borer.

This research was designed to investigate the following question: How does light intensity, manipulated across five levels using a laboratory light bank, affect the rate of photosynthesis in spinach leaf disks as measured by the floating leaf disk assay? The study was conducted over a four-week period in the advanced research laboratory at Lincoln High School. However, the experiment could not be completed due to catastrophic equipment failure in the laboratory light bank and associated data logging system. No usable photosynthetic rate data were obtained. The present paper documents the theoretical background, planned methodology, the equipment failure that prevented data collection, and the implications of an incomplete investigation for answering the research question.

Literature Review
The Floating Leaf Disk Assay
The floating leaf disk assay was adapted for classroom use from methods developed for measuring oxygen evolution in leaf tissue (Steucek et al., 1993). In the standard protocol, leaf disks are vacuum-infiltrated with a sodium bicarbonate solution so that air spaces within the mesophyll are replaced with solution, causing the disks to sink. During photosynthesis, oxygen accumulates in the intercellular spaces and the disks become buoyant. The number of disks floating at a given time interval, or the ET50 (estimated time for 50 percent of disks to float), serves as a proxy for relative photosynthetic rate (Smith et al., 2018). Smith et al. (2018) validated the assay against more precise oxygen electrode measurements and found strong rank-order correlation between ET50 and independently measured oxygen production under controlled conditions, supporting the assay as a reasonable classroom measure when absolute rates are less important than comparative rates across conditions.

Steucek et al. (1993) originally demonstrated the method with spinach and noted that disk thickness, vacuum infiltration duration, and bicarbonate concentration all influence baseline flotation. Their work established that incomplete infiltration is the most common source of experimental error because disks that retain air pockets may float without photosynthesis. For this reason, rigorous protocols specify repeated vacuum cycles until all disks sink reliably before the timed trial begins.

Light Intensity and Photosynthesis
The relationship between light intensity and photosynthetic rate has been characterized extensively using more precise instrumentation than the floating disk assay. Farquhar et al. (1980) developed a biochemical model of photosynthesis that predicts a roughly linear increase in electron transport rate at low light followed by saturation when ribulose bisphosphate regeneration or phosphate metabolism limits carbon fixation. Taiz et al. (2015) summarize classroom-relevant expectations: at very low light, rate should be near zero; as intensity increases, rate should rise; at high intensity, rate should plateau. Student investigations using LED arrays or incandescent lamps with neutral density filters have replicated this general pattern, though absolute values vary with leaf age, temperature, and CO2 concentration (Smith et al., 2018).

Lambers et al. (2008) emphasize that light response curves measured on intact leaves in research settings control temperature, humidity, and CO2, whereas classroom assays often hold only light constant and leave other variables ambient. This does not invalidate comparative classroom studies but limits direct comparison to published light saturation points from field or greenhouse research.

Spinach as a Model Organism in Classroom Research
Spinacia oleracea is widely used in photosynthesis laboratories because its mesophyll is relatively uniform and disks of standard diameter (typically 6–8 mm) produce consistent surface area. Eastwood et al. (2010) compared photosynthetic responses across common classroom species and found spinach disks among the most reproducible for flotation-based assays when leaves were sourced from the same commercial supplier and used within 48 hours of purchase. They cautioned that refrigerated storage beyond three days reduced flotation reliability, a factor the present study planned to control through same-day leaf preparation.

Gap
While the floating leaf disk assay and the general effect of light on photosynthesis are well documented in the pedagogical literature, few published student-led investigations have systematically examined five precisely calibrated light intensities using a research-grade light bank with integrated irradiance logging in a single controlled laboratory session. Most classroom reports compare only two or three light levels using uncalibrated desk lamps (Smith et al., 2018). This study was designed to fill that gap by using a laboratory light bank with verified photosynthetic photon flux density at five levels and synchronized timing software. Because the experiment could not be completed and no usable data were collected, the gap remains unaddressed by this investigation.

Methodology
Research Design
This study was planned as a controlled experiment with light intensity as the independent variable and photosynthetic rate as the dependent variable. Photosynthetic rate was operationalized as the ET50 value from the floating leaf disk assay: the time in minutes at which 50 percent of ten leaf disks in a syringe barrel had floated to the surface of the bicarbonate solution. Five light intensity levels were planned: 0, 200, 400, 600, and 800 micromoles per square meter per second, verified with a quantum sensor before each trial. The 0 condition served as a dark control to confirm that disks did not float without photosynthesis. Each intensity level was planned to have four replicate trials for a total of twenty trials, following Smith et al.'s (2018) recommendation for minimum replication in classroom comparative studies.

A completely randomized order of trials across intensities was planned to reduce order effects, with ambient room temperature and leaf batch held constant within each experimental day.

Materials
Materials included fresh organic spinach purchased from the same grocery supplier for all trials, a cork borer (7 mm diameter), 0.2 percent sodium bicarbonate solution prepared fresh daily, 60 mL syringes for vacuum infiltration, a Pasco PS-2230 laboratory light bank with five independently controlled LED panels, a LI-COR quantum sensor connected to the light bank's data port, stopwatches synchronized to the light bank timer, and laboratory notebooks for backup manual recording.

Participants and Setting
The investigation was conducted by one AP Research student under supervision of the AP Biology laboratory coordinator. No human subjects were involved. The school science department approved use of the research laboratory during fourth period for four consecutive weeks in March 2025.

Planned Procedure
On each experimental day, thirty leaf disks were to be punched from the same spinach leaf, avoiding major veins. Disks were to be vacuum-infiltrated in bicarbonate solution using three 30-second vacuum cycles until all disks sank. Ten disks were to be transferred to each syringe barrel filled with 40 mL bicarbonate solution. Syringes were to be placed under the light bank at the assigned intensity for 15 minutes of equilibration, then timing was to begin. Every 60 seconds, the number of floating disks was to be recorded manually and automatically via the light bank's camera module. ET50 was to be calculated by linear interpolation between the time point immediately below and above 50 percent flotation.

Planned Data Analysis
One-way analysis of variance was planned to compare mean ET50 across the five light levels, with post-hoc Tukey HSD tests if the omnibus test was significant at p less than 0.05. Assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance were to be checked using Shapiro-Wilk and Levene tests. Graphs of mean ET50 by light intensity with standard error bars were planned to visualize the light response curve.

Results
The experiment could not be completed. No usable photosynthetic rate data were obtained.

On March 12, 2025, during the second scheduled experimental day, the Pasco PS-2230 laboratory light bank malfunctioned catastrophically. During Trial 7 at the planned 600 micromoles intensity, the unit emitted a burning smell, displayed an error code (E-44 overheating cascade), and shut down. The integrated LED driver board was visibly damaged when inspected by the laboratory coordinator. The school's facilities technician confirmed equipment failure and red-tagged the unit as unsafe to operate. All subsequent trials were cancelled.

Prior to complete shutdown, Trials 1 through 6 were attempted on March 10 and March 12. However, these trials produced no usable data for the following reasons. Trials 1 and 2 (200 and 400 micromoles) suffered from erratic light output before shutdown: the quantum sensor logged fluctuating irradiance between 120 and 310 micromoles despite the display reading a stable setpoint, violating the planned calibration protocol. Trials 3 and 4 (0 and 200 micromoles on March 12) were interrupted when the light bank reset mid-trial, zeroing the timer and corrupting the automated flotation log. Trial 5 (600 micromoles) was abandoned when only two of ten disks had been infiltrated successfully due to a faulty vacuum pump gasket discovered after the light bank failure. Trial 6 was not started.

Manual backup records from Trials 1 and 2 were incomplete because the student recorded flotation counts on a worksheet that was discarded when the fire alarm sounded during an unrelated drill, and the student was not permitted to re-enter the laboratory for the remainder of the period. No ET50 values could be calculated from partial counts because fewer than three time points were recorded before interruption.

In summary: the experiment could not be completed due to equipment failure. The light bank was nonfunctional for the remainder of the study window. Replacement equipment was not available within the AP Research timeline. No usable data remain to test the hypothesis that light intensity affects photosynthetic rate in spinach leaf disks.

Discussion
Because no usable data were collected, this study cannot contribute empirical findings to the literature on light intensity and photosynthesis in spinach. The planned investigation aligned with established methodology (Smith et al., 2018; Steucek et al., 1993) and would have addressed a modest gap in calibrated multi-level classroom light studies, but equipment failure foreclosed any analysis. From a scientific standpoint, an incomplete experiment differs from a null result: a null result would indicate that measured differences were not statistically significant, whereas the present situation offers no evidence regarding the research question at all.

The failure highlights dependence of student laboratory research on institutional equipment maintenance. Eastwood et al. (2010) note reproducibility problems in disk assays even under ideal conditions; unreliable irradiance during partially functioning trials would have introduced unquantifiable error even if the light bank had not failed completely. Reporting the failure transparently is preferable to fabricating or selectively reporting partial data, but it limits the paper to a methodological narrative rather than a research contribution.

Limitations
The primary limitation is that the experiment could not be completed due to equipment failure, leaving no usable data. This is not a limitation of sampling or statistics but of feasibility. Secondary limitations include reliance on a single light bank model unavailable for replacement during the study period, single-investigator data collection with no blinded redundancy, and use of one spinach supplier without testing leaf batch effects. The vacuum pump gasket failure on March 12 suggests that ancillary equipment also required maintenance that was not completed before data collection. Generalizability to other species, assays, or light sources cannot be assessed. The four-week timeline did not allow rescheduling after equipment red-tagging.

Implications
For the AP Research program at Lincoln High School, this investigation demonstrates the importance of equipment verification before committing to an experimental calendar. Future students planning photosynthesis labs should confirm backup light sources and complete pilot infiltration trials before formal data collection. For science departments, the incident supports budget requests for redundant calibrated light sources and routine maintenance of vacuum pumps used in infiltration protocols.

No policy or pedagogical recommendations about light intensity and photosynthesis can be drawn from this study because no results were obtained.

Conclusion
This paper described a planned investigation of the effect of light intensity on photosynthetic rate in spinach leaf disks using the floating leaf disk assay with five calibrated irradiance levels and replicated trials. The experiment could not be completed due to catastrophic equipment failure in the laboratory light bank and related loss of usable trial records. No usable data were collected, and the research question remains unanswered by this study. Transparent documentation of equipment failure and incomplete data collection is reported here so that readers and evaluators understand that the absence of results reflects feasibility constraints rather than a tested null hypothesis. Future research should replicate the planned design with verified equipment, backup irradiance sources, and completed vacuum infiltration before timed trials begin.
