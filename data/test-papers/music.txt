The Effects of Music on Study Habits in Teenagers
AP Research
April 2025
Word Count: 3,847
Introduction
Music is something that almost every teenager listens to on a daily basis. Many students report listening to music while doing homework or studying. However, it is unclear whether music actually helps or hurts academic performance. This paper aims to explore whether listening to music while studying has a positive or negative effect on the academic habits of teenagers. The research question guiding this study is: Does listening to music while studying affect the academic performance of high school students?
This is an important question because students spend many hours each week studying and many of them listen to music during this time. If music is shown to help students study better then schools could potentially encourage its use. If it is shown to hurt studying then students should be advised to stop listening to music while doing homework.
Literature Review
Many researchers have looked at the relationship between music and cognitive performance. Rauscher, Shaw, and Ky (1993) published a famous study that became known as the Mozart Effect showing that listening to Mozart briefly improved spatial reasoning scores in college students. This study became very popular and led many people to believe that music makes you smarter. However, Chabris (1999) conducted a meta-analysis of 16 studies and found that the Mozart Effect was very small and not practically significant in most cases. Nantais and Schellenberg (1999) replicated the original study and found that the effect disappeared when students listened to a story instead of Mozart, suggesting that arousal and mood rather than the music itself caused the improvement.
More recent research has looked at music and studying specifically. Perham and Currie (2014) found that students who listened to liked music while reading comprehension tasks performed worse than those in silence, because liked music causes more distraction than disliked music or silence. Thompson, Schellenberg, and Husain (2001) found that music in a major key at a fast tempo improved performance on spatial tasks more than music in a minor key at a slow tempo, suggesting that the emotional content of music matters.
There is a gap in the research because most studies have been done in laboratory settings with adult participants. Few studies have looked at real high school students studying their actual schoolwork in natural settings. This study attempts to fill that gap by surveying high school students about their music listening habits and their academic performance.
Methodology
This study used a survey design to collect information about music listening habits and self-reported academic performance from high school students. A survey was chosen because it allows data to be collected from many participants quickly. The survey was created using Google Forms and distributed to students at my high school through social media and email.
The survey asked students to report how often they listen to music while studying (never, sometimes, often, always), what type of music they typically listen to (pop, classical, rap, rock, other), whether they feel music helps or hurts their studying (helps, hurts, no effect), and their approximate GPA (below 2.0, 2.0 to 2.9, 3.0 to 3.9, 4.0).
A total of 43 students responded to the survey. Responses were anonymous. The data was organized in a spreadsheet and percentages were calculated for each response category.
Results
Of the 43 respondents, 14 said they always listen to music while studying (32.6%), 18 said they often listen to music (41.9%), 8 said they sometimes listen (18.6%), and 3 said they never listen (7.0%). This shows that the majority of students in this sample listen to music while studying.
Of the respondents, 27 students (62.8%) said they feel music helps their studying, 9 students (20.9%) said it hurts their studying, and 7 students (16.3%) said it has no effect. This shows that most students believe music helps them study.
When looking at GPA, students who always listened to music had an average self-reported GPA category of 3.0 to 3.9. Students who never listened to music also had an average self-reported GPA category of 3.0 to 3.9. There did not appear to be a clear difference in GPA between students who always listened to music and those who never did based on self-reported data.
The most popular type of music reported was pop at 44.2% followed by rap at 27.9% and classical at 9.3%.
Discussion
The results of this study suggest that most high school students listen to music while studying and most of them believe it helps them. However the GPA data did not show a clear difference between students who always listen to music and those who never do. This suggests that music may not have a significant effect on academic performance in this sample, which is somewhat consistent with the mixed findings in the existing literature.
One limitation of this study is that the sample size was small at only 43 students from one school. This means the results cannot be generalized to all high school students. Another limitation is that GPA was self-reported and students may not accurately remember or report their GPA. A third limitation is that the survey only captured whether students listen to music while studying but did not measure actual academic performance outcomes like test scores.
Future research could use a larger sample from multiple schools and could measure actual academic performance rather than self-reported GPA. It would also be interesting to conduct an experiment where some students study with music and others study in silence and then compare their performance on a test.
The implications of this research are that students and teachers should be aware that music affects different students differently. Some students may benefit from listening to music while studying while others may not.
Conclusion
This study found that most high school students listen to music while studying and most believe it helps them. However GPA data did not show a clear difference between music listeners and non-listeners. The results are limited by a small sample size and self-reported data. More research is needed to understand the true relationship between music and academic performance in high school students.
