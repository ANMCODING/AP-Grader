The Effect of Culturally Responsive Teaching Practices on Academic Engagement in Latino High School Students
AP Research
April 2025
Word Count: 4,671
Introduction
Latino students represent the fastest-growing demographic group in American public schools, comprising approximately 28 percent of all K-12 enrollment as of 2023 (National Center for Education Statistics, 2023). Despite this growth, Latino students continue to experience significant disparities in academic outcomes compared to white and Asian peers, including lower graduation rates, lower standardized test scores, and lower rates of college enrollment (Krogstad & Lopez, 2023). Educational researchers have identified culturally responsive teaching as a promising approach to addressing these disparities. Culturally responsive teaching, defined as pedagogy that recognizes and leverages students' cultural backgrounds, languages, and lived experiences as assets in the learning process rather than deficits to be overcome (Gay, 2010), has been associated with improved academic outcomes in several studies. However the specific mechanisms through which culturally responsive teaching affects academic engagement in Latino high school students — and whether students themselves perceive and value these practices — remain underexplored. This research investigates the following question: To what extent do Latino high school students perceive culturally responsive teaching practices in their English Language Arts classrooms, and how do these perceptions relate to their self-reported academic engagement?
This question is significant because while the research on culturally responsive teaching has grown substantially, most studies have examined teacher implementation rather than student perception. Understanding how students experience and interpret culturally responsive practices provides a student-centered perspective that is currently underrepresented in the literature and could inform how teachers communicate the cultural relevance of their instruction.
Literature Review
Culturally Responsive Teaching
Culturally responsive teaching as a framework was developed primarily through the work of Gloria Ladson-Billings and Geneva Gay. Ladson-Billings (1995) introduced the concept of culturally relevant pedagogy through her landmark study of eight successful teachers of African American students, identifying three criteria: academic success, cultural competence, and critical consciousness. Gay (2010) extended this framework to create culturally responsive teaching, emphasizing the importance of using students' cultural knowledge, prior experiences, and performance styles to make learning more appropriate and effective. While both frameworks have been highly influential, Hammond (2015) notes that culturally responsive teaching is often implemented superficially — through surface-level inclusion of multicultural content — rather than at the deeper level of instructional strategy and relational trust that produces academic gains.
Student Engagement and Cultural Relevance
Academic engagement is defined as students' behavioral, emotional, and cognitive investment in school and learning tasks (Fredricks et al., 2004). Fredricks et al. conducted a comprehensive review of engagement research and argued that these three dimensions must be assessed simultaneously for a complete picture of student engagement. Skinner and Pitzer (2012) found that engagement is highly context-sensitive — students who are disengaged in one classroom may be highly engaged in another depending on how the teacher structures interaction and relevance. Relevant to the present study, Cammarota (2007) found that Latino students showed significantly higher engagement in curricula that incorporated their community histories and cultural narratives compared to standard Eurocentric curricula, suggesting that cultural relevance is a specific driver of engagement for this population.
Latino Students and Schooling
Latino students' academic experiences are shaped by multiple intersecting factors including language background, immigration status, generational status, and socioeconomic conditions (Valenzuela, 1999). Valenzuela's ethnographic study of a Houston high school found that the school's subtractive assimilation practices — requiring students to abandon their cultural identities to succeed academically — produced disengagement and resistance among Mexican-American students. In contrast Yosso (2005) developed the concept of community cultural wealth, arguing that Latino students bring multiple forms of cultural capital to school including aspirational, navigational, social, familial, linguistic, and resistant capital that teachers can leverage for academic engagement. Both Valenzuela and Yosso argue that schools systematically fail to recognize and build on these assets.
Gap
While Ladson-Billings, Gay, and Hammond have established the theoretical framework for culturally responsive teaching and Cammarota and Valenzuela have documented engagement effects at the school level, no study has directly measured the relationship between student perceptions of culturally responsive teaching and self-reported academic engagement specifically in high school English Language Arts classrooms with Latino students using both quantitative survey instruments and qualitative student interviews simultaneously. Existing studies have either measured teacher implementation without student perception or examined engagement broadly across school contexts rather than within specific classroom interactions. This study addresses that specific intersection.
Methodology
Research Design
This study employed a convergent mixed-methods design collecting quantitative survey data and qualitative interview data simultaneously and integrating them during analysis. The quantitative strand measured the extent to which students perceived culturally responsive teaching practices in their ELA classrooms and the extent to which they reported academic engagement. The qualitative strand explored how students described and interpreted specific teaching practices in relation to their own cultural backgrounds and engagement. The convergent mixed-methods design was chosen following Creswell and Plano Clark (2017) because neither quantitative nor qualitative data alone could answer both parts of the research question — the extent question requires quantitative measurement while the how question requires qualitative exploration. Combining both methods provides triangulation that strengthens validity (Creswell & Plano Clark, 2017).
Participants
Participants were 73 self-identified Latino students enrolled in English Language Arts courses at one high school in southwestern Ohio with a Latino enrollment of approximately 34 percent. Students were recruited through ELA teachers who distributed opt-in participation forms. Inclusion criteria required self-identification as Latino or Hispanic and current enrollment in an ELA course. Informed consent was obtained from all participants and parental consent was obtained for participants under 18. The study was approved by the school district research ethics committee. To protect participant confidentiality no identifying information was collected and all interview participants were assigned pseudonyms.
Quantitative Instruments
Perception of culturally responsive teaching was measured using the Culturally Responsive Teaching Survey (CRTS) adapted from Siwatu (2007), a validated 20-item instrument measuring four dimensions: cultural inclusion, relevance, student voice, and high expectations. Items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale. Academic engagement was measured using the 12-item Student Engagement Instrument (SEI) developed by Appleton et al. (2006), which measures cognitive and affective engagement with established reliability and validity in high school populations (Cronbach's alpha = .87 and .83 respectively). Both instruments were translated into Spanish and back-translated to English by bilingual research assistants to ensure equivalence for Spanish-dominant students.
Qualitative Strand
A purposive subsample of 14 students was selected for semi-structured interviews representing variation in CRTS scores (low, medium, high perception of CRT) and SEI scores (low, medium, high engagement). Interviews lasted 25 to 45 minutes, were conducted in the student's preferred language (English or Spanish), and were audio recorded with participant consent. Interview questions explored specific classroom experiences, how students interpreted teacher practices in relation to their own cultural backgrounds, and what they believed made ELA relevant or irrelevant to their lives. Interviews were transcribed verbatim and translated where necessary. Analysis used thematic coding following Braun and Clarke (2006) with initial codes generated inductively from the data. A second coder independently coded four transcripts and inter-rater reliability was calculated at Cohen's kappa = 0.81, indicating strong agreement.
Data Integration
Quantitative results and qualitative themes were integrated through a joint display comparing statistical findings with illustrative interview excerpts that confirmed, extended, or contradicted the quantitative patterns.
Results
Quantitative Findings
Table 1 presents descriptive statistics for CRTS and SEI scores across the full sample.
The Pearson correlation between overall CRTS scores and SEI engagement scores was r = .52, p less than .001, indicating a moderate to strong statistically significant positive relationship. Students who perceived higher levels of culturally responsive teaching reported higher academic engagement. Table 2 presents correlations between each CRTS subscale and SEI engagement.
Among the four CRTS subscales, cultural inclusion showed the strongest correlation with engagement (r = .49, p less than .001) followed by student voice (r = .43, p less than .001), relevance (r = .38, p = .001), and high expectations (r = .29, p = .013).
A multiple regression analysis examined whether CRTS subscales predicted engagement while controlling for grade level and gender. The model was statistically significant, F(6, 66) = 8.34, p less than .001, R-squared = .43. Cultural inclusion was the strongest unique predictor, beta = .31, p = .009.
Qualitative Findings
Three primary themes emerged from interview analysis.
Theme 1 — Seeing yourself in the curriculum. Students with high CRTS scores frequently described moments when teachers included texts by Latino authors, incorporated Spanish vocabulary, or referenced historical events relevant to Latino communities. Representative quote from a participant with high CRTS and high SEI scores: "When Ms. Garcia assigned Cisneros I actually stayed up reading. It was the first time a book felt like it was written about my family." Students with low CRTS scores described feeling that curriculum was "not for us."
Theme 2 — Being heard versus being tolerated. Students distinguished between teachers who genuinely invited their perspectives and teachers who tolerated cultural difference without engaging with it. "She asks what we think but she already knows what the right answer is. It doesn't feel real." This theme aligns with the high expectations subscale finding — superficial inclusion without genuine intellectual engagement produced lower engagement.
Theme 3 — Language as identity. Several students described code-switching in academic settings as emotionally costly and described teachers who accepted or even celebrated translanguaging as particularly engaging. This theme was unexpected and not captured by the CRTS instrument, representing a qualitative extension of the quantitative findings.
Joint Display Analysis
The quantitative finding that cultural inclusion was the strongest predictor of engagement was directly confirmed and extended by qualitative Themes 1 and 2 — students described cultural inclusion not as tokenistic representation but as intellectual recognition of their identities. The unexpected Theme 3 on language suggests that the CRTS instrument may underestimate the importance of linguistic responsiveness for Latino students whose home language is Spanish.
Discussion
These findings suggest that Latino students' perception of culturally responsive teaching practices is moderately to strongly associated with their academic engagement in ELA classrooms, with cultural inclusion and student voice as the strongest drivers. The joint display analysis reveals that students make nuanced distinctions between surface-level and deep cultural responsiveness, consistent with Hammond's (2015) warning that superficial implementation produces limited engagement gains. The unexpected emergence of language as an engagement driver extends the existing framework and suggests that future CRT measurement tools should more explicitly address translanguaging practices.
Limitations
Several limitations must be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design prevents causal conclusions — it is possible that more engaged students are more attentive to culturally responsive practices rather than that CRT practices cause engagement. The sample is from one school in southwestern Ohio with a specific demographic context that may not generalize to urban or predominantly Latino schools where different dynamics may operate. The CRTS instrument was adapted rather than newly validated for this population and the translation process, while rigorous, may have introduced semantic shifts in some items. The qualitative subsample of 14, while sufficient for thematic saturation at this site, limits the transferability of qualitative findings.
Implications
These findings are relevant for ELA teachers working with Latino students, teacher educators designing pre-service and in-service training, and school administrators evaluating curriculum adoption decisions. The finding that cultural inclusion is the strongest predictor of engagement suggests that teachers and curriculum designers should prioritize deep integration of culturally responsive content rather than surface-level multicultural additions. The language theme suggests that professional development for teachers of Latino students should specifically address translanguaging practices. Future research should use longitudinal designs, examine other content areas beyond ELA, and develop more comprehensive CRT measurement tools that explicitly capture linguistic responsiveness.
Conclusion
This study found a moderate to strong significant positive relationship between Latino high school students' perception of culturally responsive teaching practices and their self-reported academic engagement in ELA classrooms, with cultural inclusion as the strongest predictor. Qualitative findings revealed that students distinguish between surface and deep cultural responsiveness and identified language acceptance as an unexpected additional engagement driver. These findings provide student-centered evidence for the effectiveness of culturally responsive teaching and suggest specific instructional priorities for improving Latino student engagement in ELA.
