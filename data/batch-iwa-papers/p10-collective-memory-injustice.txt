Introduction
In 2021, the city of Tulsa, Oklahoma commemorated the centennial of the 1921 Tulsa Race
Massacre — an event in which a prosperous Black neighborhood known as Black Wall Street
was burned to the ground by a white mob, killing between 100 and 300 people and leaving
10,000 Black residents homeless. For seventy years, this event had been systematically erased
from Oklahoma history textbooks, excluded from public commemoration, and actively
suppressed from both official and informal memory. When survivors and descendants finally
achieved recognition, what they were demanding was not only justice for a specific historical
wrong but the right to have their collective memory acknowledged as real.
Johan Norberg (2021), analyzing how memory shapes our understanding of the present, argues
that we systematically construct versions of the past that serve our current needs —
emphasizing what confirms our preferred self-narrative and suppressing what contradicts it.
What Norberg identifies as an individual psychological tendency, scholars of collective
memory have documented as a systematic social and political process. Societies construct
official memories that legitimate existing power arrangements and suppress memories that
challenge them. The question this paper investigates is whether the deliberate, evidence-based
reconstruction of suppressed collective memory is a precondition for genuine societal
reconciliation, or whether it produces destabilization that undermines rather than advances

social healing. This paper argues that the deliberate reconstruction of suppressed collective
memory is a necessary precondition for genuine reconciliation — but that its effectiveness
depends critically on the quality and integrity of implementation.
Memory, Power, and Suppression
Paul Connerton (2008), a sociologist at Cambridge University who published in Memory
Studies, distinguishes between seven types of forgetting, arguing that the most socially
significant is what he calls "repressive erasure" — the deliberate suppression of collective
memory by those with power to do so (p. 59). Repressive erasure differs from ordinary
forgetting in that it is intentional, structured, and maintained through institutions — textbooks
that omit, archives that classify, monuments that commemorate only some (Connerton, 2008,
p. 60). The Tulsa Massacre represents a textbook case of repressive erasure, but the pattern
extends across societies: the denial of the Armenian Genocide in Turkish official history, the
suppression of apartheid-era atrocities in South African school curricula before the Truth and
Reconciliation Commission, the absence of slavery from the American memory of the Civil
War.
Howe and Knott (2015), memory researchers at the University of Portsmouth who published in
Memory, establish the psychological stakes of this social process: when individuals are
prevented from integrating difficult memories into their self-narrative, the result is not healing
but fragmentation — a sense of identity built on unresolved contradictions that produce
ongoing psychological harm (p. 641). Scaled to communities, this individual process becomes
collective: societies that cannot acknowledge traumatic history are bound to it, unable to move
forward because the unacknowledged wound continues to shape present behavior in ways that
cannot be understood or addressed without naming them.
Reconciliation and the Evidence from Truth Commissions
The most systematic evidence for the relationship between collective memory reconstruction
and reconciliation comes from transitional justice research. Gibson (2004), a political scientist
at Washington University in St. Louis who published in the American Journal of Political
Science, analyzed the South African Truth and Reconciliation Commission using survey data
from 3,727 South Africans. He found that exposure to TRC truth-telling — acknowledging
specific crimes, perpetrators, and victims — significantly increased reconciled attitudes among
both Black and white South Africans, and that the relationship between truth acknowledgment

and reconciliation was causal: those who received more information about specific truths
showed greater reconciliation regardless of their prior attitudes (p. 392).
Clark (2010), a transitional justice researcher at Oxford University who published in The
International Journal of Transitional Justice, analyzed the Rwandan Gacaca court system,
which combined truth-telling, accountability, and community-level reconciliation processes
for genocide-related crimes. Clark found that communities that engaged most fully with the
Gacaca process showed the greatest improvements in interethnic relations and collective
wellbeing five years later, though he also documented cases where poorly implemented
truth-telling processes exacerbated rather than healed community divisions (p. 812).
The tension between Gibson's findings and Clark's more qualified conclusions reveals an
important condition: collective memory reconstruction supports reconciliation only when it is
conducted with genuine commitment to accuracy and procedural fairness. Selective
truth-telling — acknowledging some harms while suppressing others, or conducting processes
that feel performative rather than genuine — may reproduce the dynamics of repressive
erasure rather than providing an alternative.

Conclusion
The evidence from political science and memory studies converges on a nuanced conclusion:
the deliberate reconstruction of suppressed collective memory is a necessary but not sufficient
condition for genuine reconciliation. Gibson's data demonstrates the causal relationship
between truth-acknowledgment and reconciled attitudes. Clark's more qualified findings show
that implementation quality matters enormously. Connerton and Howe and Knott together
establish the mechanism: unacknowledged collective wounds do not heal through suppression
but through integration into a shared historical narrative that all affected communities can
recognize as genuine. The experience of Tulsa, South Africa, Rwanda, and Chile suggests that
societies capable of facing their suppressed memories directly — however uncomfortable that
process — are more capable of reconciliation than those that perpetuate selective forgetting.
What Norberg identifies as the cognitive tendency to construct flattering memories of the past,
societies must learn to resist collectively if they are to build futures that are not condemned to
repeat the injustices of suppressed histories.
