Introduction
The World Economic Forum estimates that automation and artificial intelligence will displace
approximately 85 million jobs globally by 2025 while creating 97 million new ones (World
Economic Forum, 2020). The net gain of 12 million jobs sounds reassuring until one considers
the distribution problem: the jobs eliminated are disproportionately concentrated in
middle-skill, routine, and physical labor categories — truck drivers, cashiers, factory workers,
data entry clerks — while the jobs created require advanced technical skills that displaced
workers may lack and take years to acquire. Universal basic income (UBI) — a
government-provided unconditional cash payment to all citizens — has emerged as a
prominent policy proposal for managing this transition. This paper argues that while UBI
addresses important dimensions of automation-driven displacement, it is insufficient as a
standalone solution and must be paired with retraining and education investments.
Norberg's Warning About Nostalgia for Lost Jobs
Johan Norberg's (2021) analysis of how people idealize economic arrangements from the past
is directly relevant to how policymakers and workers think about automation. Norberg argues
that we systematically overestimate how good the past was and underestimate the adaptability
of economies to technological change. Applied to automation, this nostalgic framing explains
why policy debates are often dominated by fear of job loss rather than by the more productive

Evidence For UBI
The most rigorous evidence for UBI's potential comes from Finland's 2017-2018 randomized
controlled trial, the first government-run UBI experiment in Europe. Two thousand
unemployed individuals randomly received a monthly payment of €560 regardless of
employment status. Compared to a matched control group receiving standard unemployment
benefits, UBI recipients reported significantly higher wellbeing and mental health scores,
greater trust in social institutions, and marginally better employment outcomes (Kangas et al.,
2020, p. 18). This last finding surprised many skeptics who predicted that unconditional cash
would reduce work incentives.
Research by Damon Jones and Ioana Marinescu (2022), economists at the University of
Chicago who published in the American Economic Review, analyzed Alaska's Permanent
Fund Dividend — a real-world UBI providing Alaska residents an annual cash payment from
oil revenues. They found no significant reduction in employment due to the dividend, with
recipients actually showing modest increases in part-time work (p. 83).
The Case Against UBI as Sufficient
However, UBI's critics raise important concerns about whether cash transfers alone can
address automation-driven displacement. Daron Acemoglu and Pascual Restrepo (2019),
economists at MIT who published in the Journal of Economic Perspectives, argue that the
central challenge of automation is not income adequacy but skill mismatch: displaced workers
often lack the technical skills required for the jobs created by automation, and cash transfers do
not bridge this gap (p. 7). A truck driver receiving a UBI check still cannot write the software
that automated their route.
The cost problem is also significant. A UBI of $1,000 per month for all 258 million American
adults would cost approximately $3.1 trillion annually — roughly 75% of the entire current
federal budget. Even accounting for elimination of some existing programs, funding a
meaningful UBI would require substantial tax increases, a redistribution of productivity gains
through robot taxes, or other major fiscal changes.

Conclusion
UBI addresses the income adequacy dimension of automation-driven displacement and may
reduce the psychological harm of job loss by decoupling basic survival from employment
status. The Finnish experiment and Alaska's Permanent Fund both suggest that cash transfers
do not destroy work incentives. However, UBI alone does not solve the skill mismatch
problem that automation creates, nor does it address the geographic concentration of
displacement in manufacturing and logistics communities. A comprehensive response to
automation requires UBI paired with massively expanded workforce retraining, universal
community college access, and portable benefits that follow workers rather than jobs.
