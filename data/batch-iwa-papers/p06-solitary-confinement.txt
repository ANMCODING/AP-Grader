Introduction
On any given day, approximately 80,000 people in the United States are held in solitary
confinement — isolated in a cell for 22-24 hours per day, with minimal human contact, often
for weeks, months, or even years (American Civil Liberties Union, 2014). The United Nations
Standard Minimum Rules for the Treatment of Prisoners — known as the Nelson Mandela
Rules — define solitary confinement exceeding 15 days as cruel, inhuman, or degrading
treatment. By this international standard, which the United States has endorsed, a significant
portion of American solitary confinement practices constitutes torture. This paper argues that
widespread solitary confinement in the United States constitutes a human rights violation and
that legislative reform mandating strict time limits and prohibiting its use for vulnerable
populations is urgently necessary.
The Psychological Evidence
The psychological literature on solitary confinement is remarkably consistent: extended
isolation produces severe mental health consequences in the vast majority of those who
experience it. Stuart Grassian, a psychiatrist at Harvard Medical School who has conducted the
most extensive clinical research on solitary confinement, documented a specific syndrome in
isolated prisoners: hypersensitivity to stimuli, perceptual distortions and hallucinations,

paranoia, difficulties with thinking and concentration, and overt psychiatric breakdown
(Grassian, 2006, p. 330). Critically, Grassian found these symptoms in prisoners who had no
prior psychiatric history, demonstrating that isolation itself — not pre-existing mental illness
— produces psychiatric harm.
Craig Haney, a social psychologist at the University of California Santa Cruz who published a
systematic review in the American Journal of Public Health, synthesized decades of research
and found that even relatively short periods of isolation — as few as a few days — can produce
lasting psychological changes (Haney, 2003, p. 125). These changes include cognitive deficits
that persist after release, social withdrawal, and difficulty forming relationships — deficits that
undermine reintegration and increase recidivism.
The Racial Dimension
Solitary confinement is not applied neutrally. Black prisoners are incarcerated in solitary
confinement at 3.8 times the rate of white prisoners, and Hispanic prisoners at 1.9 times, even
after controlling for disciplinary infractions (Casella and Ridgeway, 2012). This racial
disparity suggests that solitary confinement functions not merely as a security measure but as a
mechanism of racial control within already racially imbalanced prison systems.
The Counterargument: Safety and Security
Prison administrators consistently defend solitary confinement as necessary for institutional
safety. The argument is straightforward: some prisoners are genuinely dangerous to staff and
other prisoners, and removing them from general population protects others. This is not a
frivolous concern. Prison violence is real, and staff safety is a legitimate institutional interest.
However, this argument proves too little. The question is not whether any form of separation is
ever justified but whether 22-24 hours of daily isolation for extended periods — including for
nonviolent disciplinary infractions, for pretrial detainees who have not been convicted of
anything, and for people with serious mental illness — is a proportionate and necessary
response. Evidence from states that have reformed their solitary confinement practices
suggests that safety can be maintained with much less isolation. Colorado reduced its solitary
confinement population by 85% between 2011 and 2017 while prison violence rates remained
stable (Colorado Department of Corrections, 2017).

Conclusion
The psychological evidence that extended solitary confinement causes severe mental harm is
overwhelming and consistent. The racial disparities in its application suggest that it functions
as a mechanism of discrimination within already inequitable systems. The experience of states
that have reformed their practices demonstrates that safety goals can be achieved with far less
isolation. Legislative reform mandating strict time limits on solitary confinement, prohibiting
its use for people with mental illness, pregnant women, and juveniles, and requiring
meaningful human contact and programming represents a necessary and achievable step
toward conforming American prison practice with basic human rights standards.
