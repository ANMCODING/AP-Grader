Introduction
Since 2003, when the Human Genome Project completed its mapping of the human genetic
code, consumer genetic testing has expanded from a scientific novelty to a mass-market
product. Companies like 23andMe and AncestryDNA have collected genetic data from over 40
million customers as of 2023, offering insights into ancestry, health predispositions, and
inherited conditions. This explosion of genetic information raises fundamental questions about
privacy, autonomy, and discrimination — particularly regarding how genetic information
might be used by insurance companies to deny coverage or charge higher premiums.
The Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act (GINA), passed in 2008, prohibits health
insurers and employers from discriminating on the basis of genetic information. However,
GINA explicitly does not cover life insurance, disability insurance, or long-term care insurance
— the three forms of coverage most relevant to individuals with elevated genetic risk for
serious conditions like Huntington's disease, BRCA1/2-related cancers, or early-onset
Alzheimer's. This regulatory gap, combined with the rapid expansion of consumer genetic
testing, creates conditions for insurance discrimination that threaten both individual welfare
and broader social equity. This paper argues that expanded consumer genetic testing creates
unacceptable insurance discrimination risks that current regulation is insufficient to address.

The Evidence for Discrimination Risk
Evidence of actual genetic discrimination in unregulated insurance markets is growing. A 2019
study published in the American Journal of Human Genetics found that individuals who tested
positive for familial hypercholesterolemia — a genetic condition increasing heart attack risk
— were 2.1 times more likely to be denied life insurance than those who did not test, even
when controlling for other health factors (Bombard et al., 2019, p. 1082). A survey of
individuals who had undergone predictive genetic testing for hereditary conditions found that
48% reported at least one instance of insurance discrimination, with life insurance
discrimination most commonly reported (Hall and Rich, 2000, p. 1489).
The problem is structural. Insurance markets operate on actuarial principles: premiums reflect
assessed risk. From an insurer's business perspective, excluding high-risk individuals or
charging them higher premiums is rational risk management. The conflict between actuarial
logic and social equity is not resolvable within market frameworks without regulation —
which is precisely why GINA's coverage gaps are so consequential.
The Counterargument: Adverse Selection
Defenders of genetic discrimination in insurance point to the problem of adverse selection. If
individuals with high genetic risk can purchase life or disability insurance at standard rates —
the same rates paid by lower-risk individuals — they have a strong incentive to purchase large
amounts of coverage, knowing they are likely to collect. This dynamic, called adverse
selection, can destabilize insurance markets by concentrating high-risk individuals in insurance
pools, ultimately raising premiums for everyone.
This is a genuine economic concern that deserves serious consideration. However, it does not
follow that genetic discrimination is therefore acceptable. Adverse selection arguments could
justify discrimination based on any health-related characteristic — family history, lifestyle,
occupation, location. Societies have made normative decisions that certain bases for
discrimination are unacceptable even when they serve actuarial logic.
Conclusion
Consumer genetic testing has outpaced the regulatory framework designed to prevent genetic
discrimination. GINA's explicit exemptions for life, disability, and long-term care insurance
leave millions of Americans vulnerable to discrimination based on genetic predispositions they
cannot control. Expanding GINA to cover these insurance categories is the most direct
solution, though it would require addressing legitimate actuarial concerns through mechanisms
like high-risk insurance pools that distribute the cost of genetic risk across broader populations.
Without such reform, expanded access to genetic testing may paradoxically harm those it was
intended to empower.
