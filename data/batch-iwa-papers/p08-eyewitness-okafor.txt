Introduction
Eyewitness misidentification is the leading cause of wrongful convictions in the United States.
The Innocence Project, which uses DNA evidence to exonerate wrongfully convicted
individuals, reports that eyewitness misidentification contributed to approximately 69% of the
more than 375 wrongful convictions overturned through DNA evidence since 1989 (Innocence
Project, 2023). These are not merely legal statistics: they represent innocent people who spent
years or decades in prison for crimes they did not commit, based in significant part on the
sincere but mistaken testimony of witnesses who genuinely believed what they reported.
The scientific understanding of memory has advanced dramatically since eyewitness testimony
was established as a cornerstone of criminal evidence. We now know, with the same
confidence we know anything in behavioral science, that human memory is not a recording
device — it is a reconstructive process that is systematically vulnerable to distortion,
suggestion, and post-event contamination. As Norberg (2021) argues about memory more
broadly, we mistake familiar reconstructions for accurate records, treating the stories we tell
ourselves about the past as transparent windows onto what actually happened. In the
courtroom, this cognitive error has consequences measured in decades of human freedom. This
paper argues that the scientific consensus on memory fallibility obligates the American
criminal justice system to implement fundamental reforms in how eyewitness identification

The Science of Memory Distortion
Elizabeth Loftus, a cognitive psychologist at the University of California Irvine and one of the
most cited researchers in psychology with over 600 publications, has demonstrated through
hundreds of experiments spanning four decades that human memory is profoundly malleable.
Her foundational experiments showed that the phrasing of questions asked after an event
systematically alters witnesses' memories of the event itself: asking "how fast were the cars
going when they smashed into each other?" produced higher speed estimates and more
frequent reports of broken glass than asking "how fast were the cars going when they hit each
other?" — even when no broken glass was present (Loftus and Palmer, 1974, p. 586). The
implication is profound: every interview of a witness, conducted by police, prosecutors, or
defense attorneys, is not merely a retrieval of stored information but a reconstruction that is
shaped by how questions are asked.
Garry and Gerrie (2005), memory researchers at Victoria University of Wellington who
published in Psychological Science in the Public Interest, reviewed the literature on the
misinformation effect — the tendency of post-event information to contaminate memories of
the original event — and found that it occurs reliably across thousands of experiments, affects
both children and adults, and becomes stronger when witnesses are repeatedly interviewed or
when significant time passes between the event and the testimony. Crucially, witnesses who
have been exposed to misinformation report their distorted memories with as much confidence
as witnesses who have accurate memories, making confidence an unreliable indicator of
accuracy.

The Current Legal Response Falls Short
Despite this scientific consensus, American courts continue to treat eyewitness testimony as
highly reliable evidence. In most jurisdictions, jurors receive no instruction explaining that
memory is reconstructive, that confidence does not predict accuracy, or that certain
identification procedures are more likely than others to produce false identifications. Expert
testimony on memory science is routinely excluded as unhelpful or prejudicial.
A 2014 National Academy of Sciences report, convened by some of the most distinguished
scientists in the field, concluded that eyewitness identifications are significantly less reliable
than generally assumed, that interview procedures profoundly affect accuracy, and that
substantial reforms in both police procedures and courtroom practices are warranted (National
Academy of Sciences, 2014). Yet reforms have been adopted unevenly, with some
jurisdictions implementing science-based identification procedures while others continue
practices known to increase false identifications.
The Counterargument: Practical Necessity
Critics of aggressive reform argue that eyewitness testimony, despite its imperfections, is often
the only available evidence in cases where no physical evidence exists. Eliminating or severely
restricting eyewitness testimony would make many serious crimes unprosecutable, allowing
dangerous offenders to escape accountability. This is a legitimate concern that a responsible
policy response must address.
However, the argument for reform is not that eyewitness testimony should be eliminated — it
is that it should be gathered using procedures known to minimize distortion, evaluated by
courts with full knowledge of its limitations, and corroborated wherever possible with physical
evidence. These reforms do not make prosecution impossible; they make prosecution more
reliable. A conviction based on properly gathered, appropriately evaluated eyewitness
testimony is more trustworthy, not less, than a conviction based on evidence gathered through
procedures known to induce false memories.

Conclusion
The scientific evidence that human memory is reconstructive, systematically vulnerable to
distortion, and corrupted by common investigative practices is overwhelming and consistent
across decades of research. Norberg's insight that we mistake familiar reconstructions for
accurate records is not merely a philosophical observation — it is the mechanism through
which innocent people are wrongfully convicted. Fundamental reform of eyewitness testimony
procedures, including mandatory adoption of blind lineup administration, recorded interviews,
and expert testimony about memory, is demanded not by abstract principle but by the same
scientific standard of evidence that makes prosecution possible in the first place.
