Introduction
In the 2020 presidential election, approximately 66.8% of eligible American voters cast ballots
— the highest turnout in over a century (United States Election Project, 2021). Yet even this
historic number means that roughly one in three eligible voters did not participate in choosing
the nation's leadership. In midterm elections, turnout typically falls to around 40-50%. Low
voter turnout raises fundamental questions about democratic legitimacy: can a government
truly claim to represent the people when the majority of those people did not vote?
Mandatory voting — a system in which eligible citizens are legally required to vote or face a
penalty — has been adopted in over 30 countries worldwide, including Australia, Belgium, and
Brazil. Supporters argue that mandatory voting would strengthen democracy by ensuring that
elected governments represent the preferences of the entire population rather than only the
most politically engaged citizens. Critics argue that forcing people to vote violates individual
freedom and might produce uninformed or random votes. This paper argues that mandatory
voting would strengthen democratic participation in the United States, though implementation
would require careful design.
Evidence for Mandatory Voting

Australia provides the most studied example of mandatory voting in an English-speaking
democracy. Since introducing mandatory voting in 1924, Australia has consistently achieved
turnout above 90% (Jackman, 2001). Australian researchers have found that mandatory voting
reduces the socioeconomic gap in political participation: without mandatory voting,
lower-income and less-educated citizens participate at lower rates than wealthier and more
educated citizens, meaning elected governments are more responsive to the interests of
advantaged groups. With mandatory voting, all socioeconomic groups participate equally,
producing governments more responsive to the full population.
Proponents also argue that mandatory voting reduces the influence of money in politics. When
campaigns must appeal to all voters rather than motivating their base to turn out, extreme
positions become less electorally advantageous. Research by Loewen and colleagues (2008),
published in Political Research Quarterly, found that mandatory voting in Canadian provincial
elections reduced partisan polarization by requiring candidates to appeal to moderates who
would not otherwise vote.
Counterarguments
Critics of mandatory voting raise legitimate concerns. The most fundamental is that freedom
includes the freedom not to participate — compelled voting could be seen as a violation of
First Amendment rights in the American context. The Supreme Court has never directly ruled
on this question, but the principle that government cannot compel political speech is well
established.
A second concern is vote quality. If people are forced to vote without genuine interest or
knowledge, they may vote randomly, for the candidate whose name they recognize, or for
whoever is listed first on the ballot. Some research suggests that uninformed voting can
produce random outcomes that do not reflect genuine public preferences any more than low
turnout does.
Conclusion
Mandatory voting would likely strengthen democratic participation by ensuring all citizens are
represented in electoral outcomes and reducing socioeconomic disparities in political
influence. The Australian experience demonstrates that compulsory voting systems can
function effectively in liberal democracies without producing significant authoritarian

overreach. However, implementation in the United States would require careful constitutional
analysis and design that protects individual freedoms while broadening participation. A system

the most promising path.
