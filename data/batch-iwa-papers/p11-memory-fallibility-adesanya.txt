Introduction
In 1984, Jennifer Thompson-Cannino, a rape survivor, identified Ronald Cotton from a police
lineup with complete certainty. She had studied her attacker's face during the assault,
determined to remember him so she could help convict him. She was absolutely sure. Ronald
Cotton was convicted and spent eleven years in prison before DNA evidence exonerated him
and identified the actual perpetrator — Bobby Poole, who had confessed to the crime while
Cotton was incarcerated. Thompson-Cannino and Cotton later co-wrote a book about their
experience. What makes this case paradigmatic is not that Jennifer Thompson-Cannino was
careless or dishonest — she was neither. What makes it paradigmatic is that the human
memory system, operating exactly as it was designed to operate, produced a sincere, confident,
and completely wrong identification.
Johan Norberg (2021), a senior fellow at the Cato Institute with over twenty publications in
economic and intellectual history, argues that human beings systematically mistake the
familiar for the accurate — we treat the stories our memories construct as transparent windows
onto what actually happened, when in fact they are reconstructions shaped by expectation,
post-event information, and the emotional needs of the present moment. What Norberg
identifies as a universal human cognitive tendency has specific, devastating consequences in

the courtroom: the justice system grants memory a credibility that the science of memory has
thoroughly dismantled. This paper argues that the scientific consensus on memory fallibility —
documented across fifty years of rigorous psychological research and confirmed by hundreds
of wrongful convictions — obligates the American criminal justice system to implement
fundamental reforms in how eyewitness identification evidence is gathered, presented, and
evaluated.
The Scientific Consensus: Memory Is Reconstructive, Not Reproductive
Elizabeth Loftus, a cognitive psychologist at the University of California Irvine and the most
cited researcher in psychology on memory, has through fifty years and hundreds of
experiments established beyond reasonable scientific doubt that memory is not a recording
device but a reconstructive process that is systematically vulnerable to post-event
contamination (Loftus, 1996, p. 72). Her foundational misinformation effect experiments
demonstrated that asking questions using different vocabulary — "smashed" versus "hit" in
descriptions of car accidents — reliably altered witnesses' subsequent memories of events,
including producing memories of objects that were never present. This is not a laboratory
curiosity: it is a description of what happens during every police interview, every prosecutorial
preparation session, and every courtroom examination.
Garry and Gerrie (2005), memory researchers at Victoria University of Wellington who
published in Psychological Science in the Public Interest, synthesized decades of
misinformation effect research and established three findings that are directly relevant to
criminal procedure: first, misinformation effects occur reliably and robustly across age groups
and laboratory conditions; second, witnesses exposed to misinformation report their distorted
memories with as much confidence as witnesses reporting accurate memories, making
confidence an invalid indicator of accuracy; and third, misinformation effects intensify over
time and with repeated interviews (p. 23). The implication is that standard police procedure —
interviewing witnesses multiple times, using suggestive questions, conducting lineups in which
suspects are known to investigators — is optimized to produce false memories, not accurate
ones.
Howe and Knott (2015), researchers at the University of Portsmouth who published in
Memory, extend this analysis to demonstrate that the reconstructive nature of memory is not a
pathology but a design feature: memory evolved to update our models of the world based on
new information, not to faithfully preserve an unchanging record of past events (p. 635). This

evolutionary function is adaptive in most circumstances but catastrophically maladaptive in
legal contexts that treat memory as if it were a video recording rather than a flexible,
updateable model. The legal system's assumption about memory is not merely wrong — it is
wrong in exactly the way that produces the maximum number of false identifications.
The Racial Dimension
The consequences of memory fallibility are not randomly distributed. Yang and colleagues
(2023), researchers who published in PNAS, conducted a comprehensive meta-analysis of
cross-race identification research and found that witnesses are significantly more likely to
correctly identify individuals of their own race than of other races, with Black individuals
identified by white witnesses showing a cross-race error rate approximately 1.56 times higher
than same-race identifications (p. 4). This cross-race effect interacts with the racial
demographics of American crime reporting and investigation: in a system where Black
individuals are disproportionately suspected and white individuals disproportionately serve as
witnesses, the mathematics of cross-race misidentification create systematic racial injustice
even in the absence of explicit bias. Memory science has identified a mechanism through
which racially neutral individuals produce racially biased outcomes.
The Counterargument: Necessity and Reform Cost
The most serious objection to fundamental eyewitness testimony reform is practical: in many
cases, eyewitness testimony is the only available evidence, and eliminating or severely
restricting its use would make prosecution of serious crimes impossible. This concern deserves
genuine engagement rather than dismissal.
However, the reform agenda advocated by memory scientists and the National Academy of
Sciences does not call for eliminating eyewitness testimony — it calls for gathering it through
procedures known to minimize distortion and presenting it to courts with full transparency
about its limitations (National Academy of Sciences, 2014). Blind lineup administration — in
which the officer conducting the lineup does not know which person is the suspect — has been
shown by Wells and Olson (2003), researchers at Iowa State University who published in
Psychology, Public Policy, and Law, to significantly reduce false identifications without
reducing accurate ones (p. 83). This is not a trade-off between justice and truth; it is a
procedure that produces both more accurate identifications and fewer false ones
simultaneously.

Conclusion
The scientific case for fundamental reform of eyewitness identification procedures is as strong
as any case in behavioral science. Norberg's framework for understanding memory as
reconstruction rather than reproduction is not merely a theoretical observation — it is the

cognitive mechanism through which Ronald Cotton spent eleven years in prison for a crime he
did not commit and through which hundreds of other innocent individuals have suffered
comparable fates. The convergence of Loftus's foundational research on the misinformation
effect, Garry and Gerrie's synthesis of its reliable and robust occurrence, Howe and Knott's
evolutionary framework for understanding why memory is reconstructive, Yang and
colleagues' documentation of the racial dimension, and the National Academy of Sciences'
comprehensive reform recommendations creates an overwhelming scientific consensus. The
justice system's continued reliance on identification procedures known to produce false
memories, conducted by officers who know who the suspect is, presented to jurors with no
information about memory's limitations, constitutes an ongoing institutional failure that is
costing innocent people their freedom. The obligation to reform is not discretionary — it is the
logical consequence of taking scientific evidence seriously in the domain where it matters
most.
