Introduction
The political slogan "Make America Great Again" is not a policy proposal — it is a memory
claim. It asserts that a better version of America existed in the past and that returning to that
version is both possible and desirable. As Johan Norberg (2021), a Swedish historian and
senior fellow at the Cato Institute with over twenty publications on economic history, argues:
human memory is systematically biased toward the past, leading us to believe that previous
eras were better than they actually were. When political actors deliberately exploit this
cognitive bias — amplifying nostalgic memories of a past that was, in Norberg's phrase, "false
nostalgia" — they distort the information environment in which democratic decisions are
made. This paper argues that the strategic manipulation of nostalgic memory poses a genuine
threat to democratic decision-making and that media literacy education represents the most
proportionate democratic response.
How Nostalgic Memory Distorts Political Judgment
Norberg's framework for understanding false nostalgia receives important empirical support
from the psychological literature on memory and political cognition. Wildschut and colleagues
(2010), researchers at the University of Southampton who published in the Journal of

Personality and Social Psychology, found that induced nostalgia reliably increases people's
expressed desire for political continuity and resistance to change, even when the change being
resisted is objectively beneficial (p. 1083). This finding is particularly significant because it
demonstrates that nostalgia functions as a political bias, not merely a private emotion — it
systematically shapes political preferences in predictable directions.
Boym (2017), a Harvard scholar of memory and collective identity, distinguishes between
what she calls "restorative nostalgia" — which seeks to literally reconstruct the past and treats
its own selective memory as historical truth — and "reflective nostalgia" — which engages the
past with awareness of its complexity and imperfection. Political movements that exploit
nostalgic memory typically mobilize restorative nostalgia: they present a simplified, idealized
version of the past as historical fact, making any challenge to this narrative feel like an attack
on identity rather than a correctable factual error.
The Mechanism of Political Exploitation
The mechanism through which nostalgic memory is politically exploited has been analyzed by
Mudde and Kaltwasser (2017), political scientists who published in their landmark work
Populism: A Very Short Introduction. They argue that populist political movements across the
ideological spectrum share a core rhetorical structure: identifying a virtuous "people" who
once enjoyed a better, simpler, more authentically national life, and blaming a corrupt "elite"
for stealing or corrupting that life (p. 47). This narrative structure is inherently nostalgic — it
requires a better past to be recovered — and it is inherently false in that the better past never
existed as described.
The tension between Norberg's cognitive explanation and Mudde and Kaltwasser's political
science framework reveals an important distinction: nostalgia-driven political distortion
operates simultaneously at the individual psychological level and the collective rhetorical
level. Addressing only one dimension — say, teaching individuals to be more critically aware
of their nostalgic biases — leaves the collective rhetorical exploitation intact. Effective
response requires interventions at both levels.

The Democratic Stakes
The threat to democratic decision-making is not that nostalgia exists — it is a universal human
experience — but that its strategic manipulation creates an asymmetric information
environment. Citizens who rely on nostalgic narratives rather than current evidence when
evaluating policy proposals are not exercising genuine democratic judgment; they are
responding to manufactured emotional cues. This undermines the deliberative ideal that
democratic legitimacy rests on the rational consent of informed citizens.
Conclusion
The strategic manipulation of nostalgic memory distorts democratic decision-making by
exploiting a universal cognitive bias to generate political loyalty based on fictional pasts rather
than accurate assessments of current conditions and future possibilities. The evidence from
psychology (Wildschut et al.), cultural theory (Boym), and political science (Mudde and
Kaltwasser) converges on this diagnosis, even as these frameworks offer different explanations
of the mechanism. Media literacy education that specifically addresses nostalgic reasoning —
helping citizens recognize when political appeals are exploiting rather than genuinely engaging
their memories — represents the most democratically appropriate response, as it preserves
political autonomy while reducing cognitive manipulation.
