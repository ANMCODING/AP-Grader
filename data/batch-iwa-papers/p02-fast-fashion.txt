FAST FASHION AND ENVIRONMENTAL IMPACT
2
Fast fashion is a term used to describe the rapid production of large quantities of clothing at
low cost. Companies like Zara, H&M, and Shein produce new clothing collections every few
weeks instead of the traditional two seasons per year. This has made fashionable clothing more
affordable for consumers but has raised serious environmental concerns. This paper examines
the environmental impact of fast fashion.
The Environmental Problem
Fast fashion creates enormous amounts of waste. According to the Environmental Protection
Agency, Americans throw away about 11.3 million tons of textile waste every year, which is
about 85 pounds per person. Most of this textile waste ends up in landfills where synthetic
fabrics like polyester can take hundreds of years to decompose. The fashion industry is also
responsible for about 10% of global carbon emissions, making it one of the most polluting
industries in the world.
Water pollution is another major problem caused by fast fashion. Textile dyeing is the second
largest polluter of clean water globally. Chemicals used to dye fabrics often end up in rivers
and lakes, harming aquatic life and contaminating drinking water. Countries like Bangladesh,
China, and India where most fast fashion is produced have experienced serious water quality
problems linked to textile manufacturing.

The Social Dimension
Beyond environmental problems, fast fashion also raises social justice concerns. Most fast
fashion is produced in developing countries where workers earn very low wages and work in
dangerous conditions. The Rana Plaza collapse in Bangladesh in 2013, which killed 1,134
garment workers, highlighted the dangerous conditions in which fast fashion is produced.
Consumer Responsibility
Some people argue that consumers are responsible for the problems caused by fast fashion.
When people buy cheap clothing they know will not last long, they are contributing to the
cycle of overproduction and waste. Buying fewer items of higher quality and shopping
secondhand are ways consumers can reduce their impact.
However, others argue that it is unfair to blame consumers when fashion companies
deliberately design products to fall apart quickly, use manipulative marketing tactics to
encourage overconsumption, and price sustainable alternatives out of reach for many people.
From this perspective, systemic change requiring companies to take responsibility for the full
lifecycle of their products is more effective than relying on individual consumer choices.
Conclusion
Fast fashion contributes significantly to environmental degradation through textile waste,
carbon emissions, and water pollution. While consumers can make more responsible choices,
systemic change requiring companies to adopt sustainable practices is necessary to address the
scale of the problem. Governments should consider regulations requiring fashion companies to
reduce waste and pollution.
