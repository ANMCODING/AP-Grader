Nuclear Energy and Climate Change: Examining Both Sides of a Controversial Debate

Climate change is one of the most important and serious challenges that the world is facing today. Scientists from many different countries have found through many different kinds of research and study that the Earth is getting warmer because of greenhouse gases that are released when people burn fossil fuels like coal, oil, and natural gas for energy. These fuels are used for many different purposes in modern life, including generating electricity, powering vehicles and transportation, heating homes and buildings, and running factories and industrial processes. The greenhouse gases from burning these fuels, especially carbon dioxide, build up in the atmosphere and trap heat, causing average temperatures to rise over time. This warming is causing many serious problems for people and nature around the world, including rising sea levels that threaten communities living near coasts, more frequent and intense storms and droughts, loss of habitats for wildlife, and disruptions to food production that could affect people's ability to get enough food. Most scientists and governments agree that addressing climate change will require moving away from fossil fuels and toward cleaner forms of energy.
Nuclear energy is one option that has come up in discussions about how to do this. Stewart
Brand, a scientist and environmentalist, has argued that nuclear power is an important low-carbon energy source that should be part of the solution to climate change, though many other people strongly disagree with this view. This paper will explore the arguments that different people make about nuclear energy and try to present both sides of the debate as fairly as possible so that readers can consider the different perspectives and think about this

important issue for themselves.

There are several important arguments that people who support nuclear energy make for why it should be considered as part of the response to climate change. The most frequently cited advantage of nuclear energy is that it produces very low amounts of greenhouse gas emissions compared to fossil fuels. When scientists calculate the total greenhouse gas emissions from nuclear power over its complete lifecycle—including mining the uranium fuel, building the power plant, generating electricity, and managing the waste at the end—they find that nuclear produces approximately 12 grams of carbon dioxide equivalent per kilowatt-hour of electricity generated, which is much lower than the approximately 820 grams produced by coal-fired power plants and the approximately 490 grams produced by natural gas plants
(World Nuclear Association, 2023). These numbers show that nuclear energy produces only a tiny fraction of the greenhouse gas emissions that fossil fuels produce for the same amount of electricity. A second important argument is that nuclear power plants can generate electricity reliably and continuously around the clock, regardless of the weather or the time of day. This is different from solar panels, which only work when the sun is shining, and wind turbines, which only work when the wind is blowing. Having power that works all the time is important for keeping the electricity grid stable and reliable. The country of France provides a real-world example of nuclear energy working at large scale: France generates approximately 70 percent of its electricity from nuclear power and has significantly lower per-person greenhouse gas emissions than most comparable industrialized countries (International Energy Agency, 2022).
Supporters also say that modern nuclear reactor designs are much safer than older ones, and that nuclear power has a good safety record compared to the ongoing health harms caused by air pollution from fossil fuels.

On the other side of the debate, critics of nuclear energy raise many serious concerns that they believe should prevent or limit its expansion. The most well-known and emotionally powerful concern for many people involves the risk of nuclear accidents. The disasters at
Chernobyl in Ukraine in 1986 and at Fukushima in Japan following a massive earthquake and tsunami in 2011 showed very dramatically that nuclear accidents can have catastrophic, wide-reaching, and long-lasting consequences. Chernobyl involved an explosion and fire that released enormous amounts of radioactive material and forced the evacuation of large areas that remain restricted to this day. Fukushima caused the evacuation of hundreds of thousands of people and the contamination of land and ocean water, creating ongoing problems that
Japan is still working to address. Although supporters of nuclear energy argue that modern reactor designs include many more safety features and that the risk of serious accidents is very low, critics say that the potential consequences of any accident are so catastrophic and so difficult to contain that even a small risk is unacceptable, especially since other clean energy alternatives that do not carry this type of risk are now increasingly available. Another major concern is the problem of radioactive waste. Nuclear power plants produce spent fuel rods and other radioactive materials that remain dangerously radioactive for thousands of years. Despite decades of scientific research and political discussion, no country has yet succeeded in constructing a permanent geological repository for high-level nuclear waste. This means radioactive waste currently sits in temporary storage at reactor sites around the world, creating a long-term problem that critics argue is unfair to impose on future generations who will bear the burden of decisions made today.

There are also important economic and practical concerns that critics of nuclear energy raise. Building new nuclear power plants has proven extremely expensive in recent decades, and most recent construction projects have gone significantly over budget and taken much longer to complete than originally planned. The expansion of the Vogtle nuclear facility in

Georgia is a prominent recent example: this project ultimately cost approximately $35 billion to complete, which was more than twice its original estimated cost, and took years longer than planned to finish and begin generating electricity (CNN, 2023). Critics of nuclear energy argue that this money could have been used much more effectively by investing in solar and wind energy, which have seen very dramatic reductions in cost over the past decade and can be built much more quickly than nuclear plants. The long time required to plan, get permission for, finance, and build a nuclear power plant is also an important concern, because most climate scientists agree that significant reductions in greenhouse gas emissions are needed relatively soon. A new nuclear plant started today might not be generating electricity for a decade or more, while wind and solar installations can be built and begin producing power in a matter of months to a few years. Some analysts argue that investing in nuclear energy is therefore a slow and expensive approach when faster and cheaper alternatives are available, and that resources spent on new nuclear plants would produce better outcomes for the climate if directed toward renewable energy and storage technology instead.

Different countries have made very different decisions about nuclear energy based on their own circumstances, histories, values, and experiences with nuclear technology. Germany, which had a significant nuclear energy sector, decided to phase out all nuclear power following the Fukushima accident in Japan, concluding that the risks to safety and public acceptance were too high to continue. France, as mentioned earlier, has strongly maintained and relied on its large nuclear energy sector for decades and produces most of its electricity from nuclear power. The United States has a mixed situation, with some older nuclear plants closing as they reach the end of their operational lives and some new capacity being built, while renewable energy is expanding rapidly in many parts of the country. Several countries in Asia, including
China and South Korea, are actively building new nuclear plants as part of their plans to expand electricity generation while reducing dependence on coal. These very different national

approaches show that there is no single answer that works for every country or every situation, and that governments, communities, and citizens can reach different conclusions about nuclear energy based on their specific circumstances and values. Looking at these different national experiences can also help us learn from what has worked and what has not worked in different contexts, which can be valuable information for other countries thinking about their own energy choices. The diversity of approaches also suggests that the debate about nuclear energy does not have one right answer that applies universally.

As this paper has explored, the debate about nuclear energy and its potential role in addressing climate change involves genuinely complicated tradeoffs between different types of risk, different values and priorities about what matters most, and different assessments of what is technically feasible and economically practical within the timeframes that are most critical for climate action. Supporters of nuclear energy emphasize its very low greenhouse gas emissions over its complete lifecycle, its ability to provide reliable, large-scale electricity around the clock regardless of weather conditions, and examples like France that show it can work effectively at a national scale. Critics of nuclear energy raise serious concerns about the potential for catastrophic accidents and the severe consequences when they occur, the unresolved problem of safely storing radioactive waste for thousands of years, the very high and often unpredictable costs of building new plants, the long development timelines, and the argument that renewable energy now represents a faster, cheaper, and safer path to decarbonizing electricity. Both sides raise legitimate and important points that deserve honest consideration. The appropriate role of nuclear energy in a clean energy transition will likely vary depending on a country's specific circumstances, including its existing energy infrastructure, the availability and cost of renewable alternatives in its region, public attitudes toward nuclear power and risk, and the regulatory environment. There does not appear to be a single correct answer that applies everywhere, and different communities and governments

may reasonably reach different conclusions.

In conclusion, nuclear energy is a complex and controversial topic that is directly relevant to the global challenge of addressing climate change. This paper has tried to present both sides of the debate as fairly and completely as possible, examining arguments from supporters who cite low carbon emissions, reliability, and examples of successful large-scale nuclear energy programs, and from critics who raise concerns about accident risk, radioactive waste, high construction costs, and long development timelines. Both perspectives make legitimate points, and looking at all of the evidence and arguments together, it seems clear that the question of what role nuclear energy should play in addressing climate change does not have a single simple answer that everyone agrees on. Different countries and communities will likely make different choices based on their specific situations and values. What seems most important is that these decisions be made through careful, transparent, and inclusive processes that take seriously all of the relevant considerations, are informed by the best available scientific and economic evidence about the true costs, benefits, and risks of different energy choices, and that include meaningful participation from all of the communities that will be affected by the outcomes. This paper has aimed to contribute to the public understanding of this important debate by presenting the key arguments on both sides in a clear and balanced way.

The question of how decisions about nuclear energy should be made in a democratic society is an important part of the debate that sometimes receives less attention than the technical and economic questions. Because nuclear energy involves risks that affect communities near reactors and waste storage sites, as well as broader environmental and security implications that extend beyond national borders, there are important questions about who should have a voice in decisions about whether to build nuclear plants and where to locate them. In many cases, the communities that end up hosting nuclear facilities or proposed waste repositories have not had a meaningful opportunity to participate in the decision-making process, and some critics argue that nuclear facilities have disproportionately been located near communities that have less political power and economic resources to resist their placement.
This raises important questions about environmental justice and the fair distribution of risks and benefits across different groups in society. Supporters of nuclear energy generally argue that these concerns about siting and community consent apply to many types of energy infrastructure, not just nuclear, and that improved processes for community engagement and democratic oversight can address these concerns without eliminating nuclear energy as an option for reducing greenhouse gas emissions. Critics respond that the unique and long-lasting nature of nuclear risks makes community consent especially important in this case, and that historical patterns of nuclear siting suggest that existing processes for community engagement have not been adequate. These questions about governance, democracy, and environmental justice add important dimensions to the nuclear energy debate that go beyond the technical and economic questions that receive most of the attention.

Looking at the debate as a whole, it seems clear that the question of nuclear energy's role in addressing climate change involves genuine complexity and real disagreement among thoughtful and knowledgeable people who have carefully considered the evidence. Some of the most respected scientists and environmentalists who have spent their careers working on

climate change have concluded that nuclear energy must be part of the solution, arguing that the scale and urgency of the problem requires using all available low-carbon energy sources.
Others who are equally committed to addressing climate change have concluded that the risks, costs, and other problems associated with nuclear energy make it a poor investment compared to renewable energy alternatives that are now rapidly improving and becoming cheaper. Both groups have looked at the same basic evidence and reached different conclusions, which suggests that the disagreement is not simply about the facts but also about how to weigh different kinds of risks and benefits against each other. For example, people who are most concerned about the urgency of climate change and the risks of doing too little to reduce emissions may be more willing to accept the risks associated with nuclear energy, while people who are most concerned about nuclear safety and waste may be less willing to do so even if they are also very concerned about climate change. These are genuine value judgments about risk and uncertainty that different people can reasonably make differently, which is part of why the debate about nuclear energy has continued for so long without reaching a clear consensus.
