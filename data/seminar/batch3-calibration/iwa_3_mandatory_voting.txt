The Democratic Case for Compulsory Voting: Civic Obligation and Political Equality

In the 2020 United States presidential election, often described as the most consequential in a generation, approximately 33 percent of eligible voters did not cast a ballot—meaning that the outcome of the election was determined by roughly 67 percent of the electorate rather than all of it. Non-voting is not randomly distributed across the population: it is concentrated among lower-income citizens, younger voters, and racial minorities, meaning that the unequal distribution of voting participation compounds existing political inequalities rather than merely reflecting them. Political philosopher Jason Brennan has argued that compulsory voting is one of the few institutional mechanisms with documented capacity to address participation inequality at scale, drawing on the experience of countries including
Australia, Belgium, and Brazil where mandatory voting laws have produced turnout rates consistently above 90 percent (Brennan & Hill, 2014). This paper argues that compulsory voting, implemented alongside a none-of-the-above option that preserves voter autonomy, represents a legitimate and necessary institutional response to the systematic exclusion of lower-income and minority citizens from effective electoral participation in the United States.
The case for compulsory voting is not that voting is the most important civic act but that the failure to vote, when it is systematically concentrated among the politically marginalized, distorts electoral outcomes and policy priorities in ways that democratic theory cannot accept.

The empirical evidence from countries with compulsory voting strongly supports the claim that mandatory participation laws increase turnout across income and demographic groups, reducing the participation gap between advantaged and disadvantaged citizens.
Australia adopted compulsory voting in 1924 and has maintained turnout rates above 90
percent in every federal election since, with surveys consistently showing that the participation gap between high- and low-income Australians is substantially smaller than in comparable voluntary-voting democracies (Brennan & Hill, 2014). Lijphart's comparative analysis of twenty-four democratic countries found that compulsory voting was the single institutional factor most consistently associated with reduced inequality in political participation, more powerful than electoral system design, campaign finance regulation, or registration procedures
(Lijphart, 1997). The policy implications of reduced participation inequality are significant:
Lijphart's study and subsequent research have found that governments in high-turnout democracies tend to produce more redistributive policy outcomes and greater responsiveness to lower-income citizens' preferences, consistent with the theoretical prediction that politicians respond to actual voters rather than the electorate as a whole. The mechanism is straightforward: when non-voters are disproportionately lower-income and minority, their policy preferences are systematically underweighted in electoral competition because politicians who focus campaign resources and policy attention on likely voters are responding rationally to the incentive structure that voluntary voting creates.

Critics of compulsory voting argue that forced participation violates individual liberty by compelling a political act, that uninformed or disengaged voters will make worse collective decisions, and that the legitimacy of democratic outcomes depends on the voluntary consent of participants rather than their coerced presence. Brennan's own earlier work on epistocracy raises versions of the competence objection: if voters who currently abstain do so because they feel insufficiently informed to vote responsibly, compelling them to participate may introduce

noise rather than signal into electoral outcomes (Brennan & Hill, 2014). This objection deserves serious engagement, but it proves less than it claims. The empirical evidence on what compulsory voting actually does to election outcomes in countries that practice it does not support the prediction that forced participation produces worse policy or more extreme electoral results—Australia's policy record does not exhibit the pathologies the competence objection predicts. The liberty objection is more philosophically fundamental but is adequately addressed by the inclusion of a formal none-of-the-above option, which preserves the right to withhold support from all candidates while requiring citizens to engage with the electoral process. Jury duty, taxation, and compulsory education similarly impose civic obligations without being regarded as fundamental violations of liberal democratic principles.

The implementation design matters enormously for whether compulsory voting achieves its egalitarian goals without generating the backlash and evasion that undermined compulsory voting regimes in some Latin American countries. The Australian model combines a modest fine for non-compliance (currently approximately $20 AUD) with a straightforward process for exemptions on grounds of illness, religious objection, or reasonable impediment, producing very high compliance without generating significant enforcement burden or popular resentment.

The research question this paper addressed—whether compulsory voting can be justified as a democratic institution in the United States—yields an affirmative answer grounded in both political theory and comparative empirical evidence. The systematic concentration of non-voting among lower-income and minority citizens is not a neutral fact about individual preferences but a structural feature of a voluntary voting system that operates within an environment of unequal resources, information, and access. Brennan and Hill's analysis establishes the participation inequality that compulsory voting addresses; Lijphart's comparative research establishes that it works; the Australian experience establishes that it is practically implementable and politically sustainable. The competence and liberty objections are serious but do not withstand scrutiny when the alternative is a voluntary system that systematically excludes the most disadvantaged citizens from effective electoral participation.
A democracy that produces outcomes reflecting the preferences of its most advantaged citizens more than those of its full population is failing its foundational promise, and institutional reform to address that failure is both legitimate and necessary.

The relationship between turnout inequality and policy inequality has been documented with increasing precision in comparative political science. Lijphart's original 1997 finding that compulsory voting is the institutional factor most associated with reduced participation inequality was based on aggregate data that could not definitively establish causal direction, but subsequent research using more sophisticated designs has strengthened the causal inference. Singh's analysis of survey data from countries with and without compulsory voting found that the participation gap between high- and low-education voters—a proxy for socioeconomic status—was significantly smaller in compulsory voting countries, even after controlling for other institutional and demographic factors (Singh, 2015). This finding suggests that compulsory voting does not merely increase aggregate turnout but specifically addresses the differential participation that creates political inequality, making it a targeted rather than merely a general intervention.

The design of a none-of-the-above option, which this paper identifies as essential for addressing the liberty objection to compulsory voting, requires careful consideration of how abstention is recorded and whether it produces meaningful electoral consequences. Australia's informal vote—ballots submitted blank or incorrectly marked—runs at approximately 5
percent in federal elections, providing evidence that a significant fraction of voters prefer not to support any candidate when given the choice to attend without voting. A formal none-of-the-above option that is counted separately and reported in official results would provide additional information about voter dissatisfaction with available candidates that informal votes currently obscure (Mackerras & McAllister, 1999). Some political scientists have argued for stronger consequences attached to high none-of-the-above results—such as triggering new candidate selection processes or requiring parties to explain why a significant fraction of their constituents refused to endorse any option—which would give this mechanism genuine democratic content rather than treating it as merely a safety valve for reluctant

participants.

The racial dimensions of voting participation inequality in the United States give the compulsory voting argument particular urgency in the American context, where the history of systematic disenfranchisement of Black and minority voters and the contemporary persistence of voter suppression through registration requirements, polling place closures, and ID
requirements makes voluntary voting a mechanism through which historical exclusion is perpetuated. Brennan and Hill's argument for compulsory voting as an egalitarian institution draws explicitly on this history, noting that the same communities that were denied voting rights through legally sanctioned disenfranchisement for generations now face lower voter registration rates, higher barriers to participation, and disproportionate representation among non-voters (Brennan & Hill, 2014). Compulsory voting with robust voter registration and identification access provisions—automatic registration, same-day registration, accessible polling locations—would address both the demand side (motivation and obligation) and the supply side (access and ease) of participation, creating a more genuinely universal system than the current voluntary model provides.

The objection that compulsory voting produces an electorate of low-information voters who make worse collective decisions than the self-selected turnout of voluntary systems deserves engagement but ultimately fails on both empirical and normative grounds.
Empirically, there is no evidence that countries with compulsory voting produce systematically worse policy outcomes or more extreme electoral results than comparable voluntary-voting democracies—Australia, Belgium, and Luxembourg are not notable for the pathologies the competence objection predicts. The normative version of the objection—that uninformed votes should not count because they reduce decision quality—runs into the fundamental democratic commitment to political equality: the legitimacy of democratic decision-making derives from

the inclusion of all members of the political community, not from the epistemic quality of individual votes. A system that produces outcomes reflecting the preferences of the full electorate, even imperfectly, is more democratically legitimate than one that produces outcomes reflecting the preferences of a systematically biased subset, however well-informed that subset may be. The goal of democratic reform should be to improve the quality of political information and deliberation available to all citizens, not to restrict participation to those who have already benefited from better access to political information.
