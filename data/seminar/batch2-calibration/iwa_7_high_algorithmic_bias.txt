Bias by Design: How Algorithmic Risk Assessment Tools Perpetuate Racial Inequity in
American Criminal Justice

In 2016, a ProPublica investigation into the COMPAS (Correctional Offender
Management Profiling for Alternative Sanctions) risk assessment algorithm revealed that the
tool incorrectly flagged Black defendants as future criminals at nearly twice the rate of white
defendants, even when controlling for age, criminal history, and other legally recognized
factors (Angwin et al., 2016). The investigation analyzed risk scores assigned to more than
7,000 people arrested in Broward County, Florida, finding that Black defendants were 77
percent more likely than white defendants to be incorrectly classified as high-risk when they
did not subsequently reoffend. This paper argues that the deployment of machine learning risk
assessment tools in criminal sentencing and pretrial detention represents a systematic
laundering of historical racial discrimination through the institutional authority of algorithmic
objectivity—and that the mathematical properties of such systems make it impossible to
eliminate discriminatory outcomes by any purely technical means. As Angwin and colleagues'
investigation established, the problem is not that COMPAS's designers intended to produce
racially biased outcomes; it is that training any predictive system on data generated by a
racially unequal enforcement apparatus will reproduce that inequality in probabilistic form.
The criminal justice system's embrace of algorithmic decision-making reflects an institutional
desire for the appearance of neutral, evidence-based judgment that conceals the fundamentally
value-laden choices embedded in every stage of how these systems are designed, trained, and

deployed against the most vulnerable populations in American society.

The empirical foundation of this argument rests on documented statistical properties of
recidivism prediction that academic research has confirmed with methodological rigor
extending well beyond the ProPublica investigation. COMPAS assigns risk scores on a scale
of one to ten; ProPublica's analysis found that Black defendants received high-risk scores at
substantially elevated rates and—critically—that the false positive rate for Black defendants
was approximately twice that for white defendants, meaning Black individuals who did not
subsequently reoffend were far more likely to have been incorrectly labeled as dangerous
(Angwin et al., 2016). Figure 1 documents these disparate false positive and false negative
rates across racial categories as calculated from the Broward County dataset, illustrating the
asymmetry that ProPublica's investigation first surfaced. Dressel and Farid (2018) extended
this analysis through a controlled experiment comparing COMPAS's predictive accuracy
against untrained human volunteers given only seven data points—age, sex, and five prior
conviction counts. COMPAS performed no better than these minimally informed human raters
who had spent approximately twenty minutes learning the task. This finding is significant
beyond its immediate methodological implications: it reveals that algorithmic tools in criminal
justice derive their institutional authority not from demonstrably superior accuracy but from
the social prestige that computational methods carry in settings where they are assumed to be
categorically more objective than human judgment. That assumption, Dressel and Farid's
research establishes, is empirically unfounded.

The Northpointe-ProPublica controversy generated substantial academic attention that
ultimately illuminated a deeper structural problem. Chouldechova (2017) demonstrated
formally that for any binary classifier applied to groups with different base rates of the
predicted outcome, it is mathematically impossible to simultaneously achieve calibration
parity (equal probability of the outcome given the same score across groups), equal false
positive rates, and equal false negative rates. This result—the calibration-fairness impossibility
theorem—explains why Northpointe's defense of COMPAS and ProPublica's critique were
both technically correct: the tool was well-calibrated by the standard Northpointe measured,
while simultaneously producing the unequal false positive rates ProPublica documented. Both
findings were consistent with each other because they assessed different properties of the same
system. The impossibility theorem means there is no technical fix for algorithmic bias in
criminal justice risk assessment when group recidivism base rates differ—as they do in the
United States, primarily as a consequence of racially differential enforcement and prosecution
practices that have nothing to do with actual propensity to reoffend. The choice of which
fairness criterion to optimize is a value judgment, not a technical determination, and that
judgment is currently being made implicitly by algorithm designers operating without
democratic accountability for the consequences.

The most substantive defense of algorithmic risk assessment comes from Dieterich and
colleagues at Northpointe, who argued that ProPublica applied the wrong fairness metric to
evaluate a predictive instrument and that calibration—the standard measure in actuarial
assessment across medical and behavioral sciences—demonstrates COMPAS to be both
accurate and equitable (Dieterich et al., 2016). This argument has genuine methodological
support: calibration is the appropriate standard for evaluating whether a predictive instrument
measures what it claims to measure, and there are principled decision-theoretic reasons to
prioritize it over demographic parity in error rates. Even accepting Northpointe's
methodological argument fully, however, this defense does not engage the problem this paper
identifies. As Figure 2 illustrates, calibration parity and disparate false positive rates can
coexist within the same system whenever underlying base rates differ—which means that a
technically accurate and well-calibrated algorithm can still systematically detain more innocent
members of one racial group than another. Barocas and Hardt's foundational treatment of
fairness in machine learning establishes that technical bias cannot be resolved at the level of
algorithm design when its causes are located in the social and institutional conditions that
generated the training data (Barocas & Hardt, 2017). A system optimizing against data that
encodes the effects of racially unequal policing will reproduce those effects in probabilistic
form regardless of the sophistication of its calibration procedures.

The institutional implications of this analysis extend beyond COMPAS to the broader
project of algorithmic governance in criminal justice. The Arnold Foundation's Public Safety
Assessment, deployed across multiple American jurisdictions for pretrial detention decisions,
represents an attempt to improve on COMPAS through greater transparency and a more
limited predictor set (Arnold et al., 2018). Yet even this more carefully designed tool operates
within the fundamental constraint that Chouldechova's theorem identifies: when group base
rates differ, algorithmic prediction cannot be simultaneously fair by all measures. The
constitutional requirement of individualized assessment in criminal proceedings—rooted in
due process protections against punishment based on group membership—sits in direct tension
with the statistical logic of risk prediction, which necessarily treats individuals as
representatives of groups whose historical patterns inform the prediction. What algorithmic
tools provide that purely human judgment does not is not greater accuracy or reduced bias but
rather a form of documentation that shifts accountability away from individual
decision-makers and toward systems that are more difficult to challenge or scrutinize. The
effect is to insulate discriminatory outcomes from legal challenge precisely by rendering them
invisible inside computational processes that courts lack the expertise to evaluate.

The research question this paper addressed—whether machine learning risk assessment
tools like COMPAS perpetuate racial discrimination in American criminal justice—admits a
clear and empirically grounded answer: they do, and the mechanism by which they do so is not
primarily technical error but the mathematical impossibility of eliminating disparate impact
when group base rates differ and those differences themselves reflect historical discrimination.
Angwin and colleagues' investigation documented the disparate impact; Dressel and Farid's
research undermined the rationale for privileging algorithmic over human judgment in this
context; and Chouldechova's impossibility theorem explains why no technical refinement can

resolve what is fundamentally a political and ethical question about what fairness requires in a
context of unequal historical treatment. The appropriate response is not to abandon
evidence-based approaches to criminal justice decision-making but to recognize that
algorithmic tools do not provide the neutral, objective evidence base their institutional
deployment implies. Any deployment of predictive risk assessment in criminal justice should
be required to specify explicitly which fairness criterion it optimizes for, the tradeoffs that
choice entails across demographic groups, and the democratic process through which those
tradeoffs were authorized—requirements that no current deployment of these systems in the
United States meets.

The implementation gap between the academic literature on algorithmic fairness and
actual practice in American courts reflects an accountability structure that insulates algorithmic
decision-making from meaningful challenge. When a human judge exercises discretion in a
bail or sentencing decision, that discretion can in principle be reviewed for consistency and
bias through appellate processes and through the accumulating record of an individual judge's
decisions over time. Algorithmic systems, by contrast, are typically proprietary—Northpointe
has successfully resisted disclosure of the specific weights and formulas used in COMPAS on
intellectual property grounds—making independent audit of their decision logic practically
impossible for defendants or their counsel. The Supreme Court's 2017 decision in Loomis v.
Wisconsin, in which the court upheld a sentence informed by a COMPAS score over the
defendant's objection that he had been denied the right to challenge the algorithm's
methodology, established a legal precedent that effectively immunizes proprietary algorithmic
tools from the constitutional scrutiny that their use in liberty-deprivation decisions would seem
to require. The combination of proprietary opacity and judicial deference has created a
regulatory vacuum in which algorithmic tools that encode historical discrimination are
deployed at scale in consequential legal proceedings with no meaningful mechanism for
affected individuals to challenge their accuracy or fairness.

The broader literature on algorithmic governance in public institutions converges on
the observation that automated decision systems consistently reproduce and often amplify the
biases of the human systems they replace, while creating institutional structures that make
those biases harder rather than easier to identify and challenge. Virginia Eubanks's analysis of
algorithmic systems in welfare administration, child protective services, and criminal justice
establishes a consistent pattern: digital tools promise efficiency and objectivity, but in practice
they encode the assumptions and priorities of their designers and the datasets that trained them,
producing automated disadvantage for populations that were already disadvantaged by the

manual systems the algorithms replaced (Eubanks, 2018). The value of this critical perspective
for the COMPAS analysis is that it reframes the question from whether COMPAS is
technically biased—which Chouldechova's work establishes is in some sense an incoherent
question—to whether algorithmic risk assessment as an institutional practice serves or
undermines the values of equal justice that criminal proceedings are constitutionally required
to embody. That reframing suggests that the appropriate evaluation of COMPAS is not
whether it meets any particular technical fairness criterion but whether its deployment
advances or obstructs the constitutional commitment to treating individuals as individuals
rather than as representatives of statistically constructed group profiles.
