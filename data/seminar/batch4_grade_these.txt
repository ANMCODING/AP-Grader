Foreclosing on Justice: How Predictive Policing
Encodes Racial Bias Into Law Enforcement Infrastructure

Kenji Nakamura

Kenji Nakamura
Dr. Okafor
AP Seminar, Period 1

Foreclosing on Justice: How Predictive Policing Encodes Racial Bias Into Law
Enforcement Infrastructure

In 2013, the RAND Corporation published a comprehensive evaluation of predictive policing systems—tools that analyze historical crime data to generate probability maps forecasting where crimes are likely to occur, which police departments then use to direct patrol resources—and found that while the tools demonstrated modest capacity to predict crime locations, they also systematically reproduced the geographic patterns of previous police activity rather than patterns of underlying criminal behavior (Perry et al., 2013). The distinction Perry and colleagues drew is fundamental: training a predictive system on arrest data rather than crime occurrence data means the algorithm learns where police have been, not where

crime

happens,

and

directs

additional

resources

to

those

same

neighborhoods—producing a feedback loop in which racially concentrated enforcement generates data that justifies further racially concentrated enforcement. This paper argues that the deployment of predictive policing tools in American law enforcement represents not a technology-neutral reform of police resource allocation but the algorithmic institutionalization of historical patterns of racially discriminatory enforcement—and that Perry and colleagues'
own assessment of the technology's limitations establishes the empirical foundation for this critique. The enthusiasm for predictive policing among municipal administrators seeking cost-effective crime reduction reflects an institutional preference for the appearance of scientific neutrality that functions to insulate discriminatory policing practices from political

accountability and legal challenge.

The feedback loop that Perry and colleagues identified operates through a mechanism that subsequent research has documented with increasing precision. Lum and Isaac's simulation study demonstrated formally that when a predictive system is trained on biased enforcement data—data reflecting racially differential stop, frisk, and arrest practices—it will produce predictions that send police back to the same communities, generating additional arrests in those communities, which further reinforces the algorithm's predictions in a self-sustaining cycle (Lum & Isaac, 2016). Their analysis showed that even with very low initial racial disparities in the training data, the feedback loop amplifies bias over successive prediction-patrol-enforcement cycles. Figure 1 illustrates this dynamic: the spatial distribution of predictive policing alerts across a simulated city narrows progressively with each patrol cycle, concentrating enforcement resources in neighborhoods that were already overpoliced in the initial data. This mathematical formalism gives rigorous expression to what the Perry
RAND study identified qualitatively: that predictive systems built on arrest data are not predicting future criminal activity but forecasting the future behavior of the police force that generated the training data. The constitutional significance of this distinction cannot be overstated. The Equal Protection Clause prohibits law enforcement practices that are motivated by racial animus or that have a racially discriminatory effect without adequate justification; a system whose outputs are driven by prior discriminatory enforcement meets both criteria.

Figure 1. Simulation of predictive policing feedback loop: spatial concentration of patrol alerts across five successive enforcement cycles, beginning from a baseline with modest racial disparity in training data. Concentration accelerates in neighborhoods with initially elevated arrest rates (Lum & Isaac, 2016).

The empirical record from cities that have deployed predictive policing tools confirms the feedback loop dynamics Perry and colleagues and Lum and Isaac identified theoretically.
Santa Cruz, California became the first city in the United States to ban predictive policing systems in 2020, following an extensive review by its city council that concluded the tools had concentrated enforcement in the city's predominantly Latino neighborhoods without evidence of corresponding crime reduction (Brayne, 2020). Chicago's Strategic Subject List—a predictive scoring system that assigned individuals a risk score based on their social network ties, prior contacts with police, and other factors—was found by a series of investigative analyses to produce scores strongly correlated with race and neighborhood rather than with subsequent criminal behavior, and was discontinued after significant civil liberties challenges.
The RAND Corporation's own guidance document, Perry and colleagues' 2013 report, recommended that departments using predictive tools conduct regular audits for racial disparities in deployment patterns and outcomes—a recommendation that the vast majority of departments adopting these tools have never implemented. The gap between the conditions under which Perry and colleagues endorsed the technology's potential and the conditions under which it has actually been deployed is itself evidence of the institutional dynamic this paper identifies: administrators adopt the appearance of scientific rigor while ignoring the safeguards that rigor requires.

The most substantive defense of predictive policing comes from policing administrators and criminologists who argue that the tools, properly deployed with appropriate community oversight, can reduce both crime and the need for aggressive patrol tactics—replacing discretionary stop-and-frisk style enforcement with data-driven deployment

that allows officers to be present in high-risk locations without requiring investigative contact with individuals (Ratcliffe, 2016). This argument has genuine practical grounding: if predictive systems direct officers to locations rather than to individuals, they operate closer to traditional patrol distribution than to individual-level risk assessment, and the deterrence benefit of visible patrol in high-crime areas is reasonably well-documented. Even accepting Ratcliffe's location-based deterrence framework on its own terms, however, this defense does not engage the structural problem that Perry and colleagues and Lum and Isaac identify. A system trained on historically biased enforcement data will direct officers to historically over-enforced locations regardless of whether those locations have elevated criminal activity, and the presence of additional officers in those locations will produce additional enforcement contacts that reinforce the data bias—independent of whether individual-level targeting is involved.
The location-based defense of predictive policing does not resolve the feedback loop; it merely relocates the mechanism from individual to geographic discrimination.

The accountability dimensions of predictive policing extend beyond the feedback loop to encompass the opacity of the systems and the mechanisms available for challenging their outputs. Brayne's ethnographic study of predictive policing implementation in the Los Angeles
Police Department found that officers who questioned algorithmically-generated deployment recommendations were discouraged from doing so by institutional culture that framed the

algorithm's outputs as objective and therefore beyond the appropriate scope of discretionary modification (Brayne, 2020). This dynamic reproduces the institutional accountability problem that Angwin and colleagues documented in algorithmic risk assessment—the algorithm's apparent objectivity insulates its discriminatory outputs from challenge precisely by framing them as mathematical rather than political. Residents of neighborhoods targeted by predictive systems have no mechanism for challenging the algorithm's designation of their neighborhood as high-risk, no access to the training data that produced that designation, and no forum in which to contest whether the algorithm's predictions reflect criminal activity or prior enforcement patterns. The civil liberties organizations that have challenged predictive policing in court have faced the additional obstacle that proprietary systems—like the PredPol algorithm deployed in dozens of American cities—are shielded from disclosure on intellectual property grounds, making independent audit of their methodology effectively impossible.

The research question this paper addressed—whether predictive policing constitutes a racially neutral technology or an algorithmic institutionalization of discriminatory enforcement—admits a clear answer grounded in the systems' own design. This paper has argued that the distinction Perry and colleagues drew in 2013—between predicting crime and predicting police behavior—is not a technical limitation that better algorithms will resolve but a structural feature of any enforcement-data-trained prediction system in a society with a history of racially discriminatory policing. Lum and Isaac's simulation establishes that even modest initial bias amplifies through the feedback loop; Brayne's fieldwork documents the institutional dynamics that prevent correction; and the record from Santa Cruz to Chicago demonstrates that the theoretical predictions are playing out in deployed systems. The Ratcliffe location-based defense of predictive policing deserves serious engagement but cannot survive scrutiny of the feedback mechanism that Perry and colleagues themselves identified and that
Lum and Isaac formalized. The appropriate response is not better algorithms but the abolition

of enforcement-data training, mandatory bias audits as a precondition for any deployment, and civil society access to algorithmic methodology sufficient to enable independent accountability—conditions that no current deployment of predictive policing in the United
States satisfies.

The legal framework governing predictive policing is underdeveloped relative to the scale and speed of deployment. Richardson, Schultz, and Crawford's analysis of what they term 'dirty data'—the criminal justice records that feed predictive systems—found that in numerous American cities, the training data for predictive tools was generated during periods of documented unconstitutional policing: mass stops and frisks later found to violate the
Fourth Amendment, drug enforcement operations concentrated in minority neighborhoods without corresponding enforcement in white neighborhoods, and arrest practices challenged in civil rights litigation (Richardson et al., 2019). Data generated by unconstitutional enforcement practices retains its unconstitutional taint when used as training data for future enforcement decisions—but this principle has not been operationalized in either judicial decisions or legislative guidance on predictive policing. Courts evaluating Fourth Amendment challenges to predictive policing deployment have generally deferred to law enforcement expertise on the algorithmic methodology, precisely because the opacity that Perry and colleagues and Brayne document prevents meaningful judicial scrutiny. The result is a system in which the constitutional accountability mechanisms designed to prevent racially discriminatory enforcement have been rendered effectively inoperative by the technical complexity of the tools they are supposed to constrain.

Shapiro's analysis in Nature of the path toward responsible predictive policing deployment articulates the minimal conditions for what ethical use of these tools would require: algorithmic transparency sufficient for independent audit, mandatory bias testing before deployment and at regular intervals afterward, community oversight boards with genuine authority to halt or modify deployments that show racial disparities, and prohibition on using arrest data as training data without correction for known enforcement biases (Shapiro,
2017). These conditions are demanding, and Shapiro acknowledges that they may effectively prohibit current-generation predictive policing tools because none are deployed under

conditions that meet them. The argument of this paper is that this conclusion is the appropriate one: Perry and colleagues' identification of the feedback loop mechanism, Lum and Isaac's formalization of its dynamics, and Brayne's documentation of its institutional embedding establish that predictive policing as currently practiced is not a tool that can be reformed into constitutional compliance through modest adjustments—it requires a structural redesign that begins from a commitment to non-discriminatory enforcement rather than from a commitment to algorithmic efficiency.

The conditions for ethically defensible predictive policing—algorithmic transparency, mandatory bias auditing, community oversight with genuine authority, and prohibition on dirty-data training—are not minor administrative reforms. They are structural requirements that, if taken seriously, preclude every currently deployed system in the United States. Perry and colleagues' research established the theoretical possibility of a predictive policing that serves public safety without encoding discrimination; the empirical record of actual deployment has not yet produced a system that realizes that possibility. Until it does, the appropriate institutional response is suspension, not reform.

Gaps, and the Case for Universal Basic Healthcare

Abena Osei

AP Seminar, Period 3

The Cost of Fragmentation: Administrative Waste, Coverage Gaps, and the Case for
Universal Basic Healthcare

The United States spends approximately twice as much per capita on healthcare as comparable high-income countries while achieving worse outcomes on most population health metrics—lower life expectancy, higher infant mortality, and greater rates of preventable death than Canada, Germany, Japan, and the United Kingdom (Papanicolas et al., 2018). Health economist David Himmelstein, whose research on administrative costs in the American healthcare system has documented these inefficiencies in detail across three decades of study, estimates that administrative overhead consumes approximately 34 percent of total healthcare spending in the United States—compared to 12 percent in Canada, where a single-payer system eliminates much of the billing, pre-authorization, and claims-processing infrastructure that American providers must maintain (Himmelstein et al., 2014). This paper argues that the fragmented multi-payer structure of American healthcare financing is not merely an inefficient arrangement that produces high administrative costs but the primary structural mechanism through which coverage gaps, healthcare inequality, and cost-driven medical debt are systematically produced—and that Himmelstein and colleagues' documentation of administrative waste provides a compelling empirical foundation for the transition to a universal coverage model financed through a simplified payment structure. The administrative inefficiency is not incidental to the system's design; it is produced by a financing architecture that requires multiple payers with different coverage rules, reimbursement rates, and

administrative requirements to be separately managed by every provider in the country.

Himmelstein and colleagues' comparative analysis established that a single-payer transition in the United States would generate savings of approximately $500 billion annually in administrative costs alone—sufficient to finance universal coverage without net increases in total healthcare spending (Himmelstein et al., 2014). The mechanism is straightforward:
American hospitals and physician practices must employ large administrative staffs to manage billing relationships with hundreds of distinct payers, each with its own formularies, pre-authorization requirements, billing codes, and denial appeals processes. A single-payer system eliminates this complexity, allowing providers to focus resources on clinical care rather than administrative compliance. Papanicolas and colleagues' systematic international comparison of healthcare spending confirmed that the United States spends more on healthcare than any other country in the sample by a substantial margin, and that the excess is disproportionately concentrated in administrative costs and in prices for the same goods and services rather than in greater utilization of care (Papanicolas et al., 2018). Figure 1 illustrates the administrative cost differential between the United States and comparable healthcare systems, demonstrating that the gap is not explained by differences in healthcare complexity, population size, or medical technology. Himmelstein's research further documents the cost to individual providers: physician practices in the United States spend four times as many hours on insurance-related administrative tasks as practices in Canada, with the time cost translating directly into reduced capacity for patient care.

Figure 1. Administrative costs as a percentage of total healthcare expenditure across eleven high-income countries, 2019. United States at 34.2% compared to single-payer system range of 11.6–14.8% (Himmelstein et al., 2014; Papanicolas et al., 2018).

Critics of universal coverage proposals argue that administrative costs in the American system reflect genuine complexity and heterogeneity—a large, geographically diverse population with varying healthcare needs and preferences that a uniform payment system would inadequately serve—and that international comparisons are misleading because they do not account for the innovation premium that market-based financing provides through higher prices and profits that fund pharmaceutical and medical device research (Papanicolas et al.,
2018). The innovation argument is the most substantive version of this critique: if American healthcare's high costs partly reflect returns to innovation that benefit global health, the case for eliminating the market structures generating those returns is more complicated than administrative waste comparisons suggest. Even accepting that some portion of the American cost premium reflects innovation investment, however, this does not address Himmelstein's core finding: the administrative cost differential between the United States and comparable systems is far larger than any plausible innovation premium can explain. A system that spends
34 percent of its healthcare budget on administration while comparable systems spend 12
percent is not primarily paying an innovation premium—it is paying for the operational complexity of fragmenting healthcare financing across hundreds of competing payers.
Reducing administrative waste through a simplified payment structure does not preclude maintaining a pharmaceutical innovation ecosystem; it frees resources currently consumed by billing complexity for clinical care, prevention, and research.

The coverage dimensions of American healthcare fragmentation reinforce the administrative efficiency argument with a direct equity analysis. Approximately 26 million
Americans remained uninsured as of 2022, and millions more were underinsured—covered in name but exposed to cost-sharing requirements that effectively denied them access to needed care (Tolbert et al., 2022). The geographic distribution of coverage gaps closely tracks racial and economic inequality: states that declined to expand Medicaid under the Affordable Care
Act have substantially higher uninsurance rates concentrated among their lower-income and minority populations. The moral premise of universal coverage is not efficiency but equal access: a system that conditions healthcare on employment status, income level, and state of residence produces health disparities that cannot be addressed by market competition or administrative reform within the existing fragmented structure. Himmelstein and colleagues'
administrative cost analysis and the coverage gap data converge on the same structural diagnosis: the fragmented multi-payer system produces both waste and inequality as structural features, not incidental malfunctions.

The international evidence on universal coverage outcomes addresses the efficiency argument from the demand side as well as the supply side. Countries with universal coverage systems consistently outperform the United States on the preventable mortality measures that reflect whether people receive timely appropriate care—conditions like diabetes complications, hypertension-related stroke, and infection-related mortality that are highly responsive to primary care access (Papanicolas et al., 2018). These outcomes differences suggest that the coverage gaps in the American system impose not merely equity costs but efficiency costs: uncompensated emergency care for uninsured patients with preventable complications is more expensive than covered primary care that prevents those complications, and the costs are distributed to insured patients, hospitals, and the public treasury rather than to

the multi-payer financing structure that created the gap. Himmelstein's analysis of medical debt in the United States finds that approximately 60 percent of personal bankruptcy cases in the country cite medical expenses as a contributing factor—a phenomenon that does not occur at comparable rates in any universal-coverage country.

The research question this paper addressed—whether the fragmented multi-payer structure of American healthcare financing is the primary driver of both its cost disadvantage and its coverage inequalities—receives an affirmative answer from the comparative evidence.
Himmelstein and colleagues' documentation of administrative waste, Papanicolas and colleagues' international spending comparison, and the coverage gap data together establish that the system's inefficiencies and inequities are structural rather than incidental. The innovation objection to universal coverage deserves engagement but cannot account for the administrative cost differential that Himmelstein's research has documented across multiple decades and methodologies. The path toward a healthcare system that achieves the outcomes of comparable countries at comparable cost requires addressing the structural source of the divergence—the financing fragmentation that generates both the waste and the gaps.

The political economy of healthcare reform in the United States has historically made the administrative efficiency argument difficult to translate into policy action despite the strength of the empirical evidence Himmelstein and colleagues have assembled. The private insurance industry, pharmaceutical companies, and hospital systems that benefit financially from the current fragmented structure have consistently mobilized substantial political resources against reform proposals that would rationalize payment and reduce the administrative complexity that generates their revenue. The $500 billion in annual administrative savings that Himmelstein estimates would accrue from a single-payer transition represents real income for the billing, coding, prior authorization, and claims-processing industries—income that would largely disappear under a simplified payment system.
Understanding healthcare reform as a political economy question rather than merely a policy design question explains why the administrative efficiency evidence has not translated into legislative action despite decades of documentation: the beneficiaries of the current inefficiency are concentrated and well-organized, while the beneficiaries of reform are diffuse and—because the administrative cost burden is embedded invisibly in premiums and out-of-pocket costs—largely unaware of what they are paying.

International evidence on single-payer or regulated multi-payer systems provides natural experiments in what healthcare financing reform can achieve. Canada's transition to a provincial single-payer system in the 1970s was followed by a sustained reduction in administrative costs relative to the United States; Woolhandler and colleagues' comparison of administrative costs in the two systems documented that American hospitals spent 34 percent of their budgets on administration compared to 13 percent in Canadian hospitals by the early
2000s—a gap that has persisted and widened in subsequent research (Woolhandler et al.,
2003). Germany's regulated multi-payer system, which limits the number of competing non-profit sickness funds and standardizes benefit packages, achieves substantially lower

administrative costs than the American system while maintaining comparable access and outcomes to single-payer systems. The German model is relevant to the American context because it demonstrates that the administrative efficiency gains do not require the elimination of competition entirely—they require standardization of benefits and payment processes sufficient to eliminate the administrative arbitrage that multi-payer systems currently create.

The coverage expansion argument for healthcare reform connects directly to the administrative efficiency argument through the financial mechanism of cross-subsidization.
Hospitals that treat uninsured patients for uncompensated emergency care must offset those costs by charging insured patients higher prices—creating a hidden subsidy from insured to uninsured that flows through hospital billing rather than through an explicit public financing mechanism. Dieleman and colleagues' analysis of healthcare spending growth factors found that price increases for healthcare services—particularly for hospital care—account for the largest share of American healthcare cost growth over the past two decades, and that these price increases are partly driven by the cost-shifting dynamics that coverage gaps create
(Dieleman et al., 2017). Eliminating coverage gaps through universal coverage would reduce the financial pressure that drives this cost shifting, potentially moderating the price growth that has been the primary driver of healthcare cost increases. This connection between coverage and cost demonstrates that Himmelstein's administrative efficiency argument and the equity case for universal coverage are not separate arguments but complementary components of a single structural diagnosis.

The transition to universal coverage in the United States faces genuine political and implementation challenges that the administrative efficiency evidence does not resolve. A
single-payer transition would displace approximately 900,000 jobs in the health insurance industry—workers who would need employment transition support. It would require negotiating payment rates with providers who currently benefit from the price variation that the fragmented system allows. It would need to design a financing mechanism—most likely through payroll and income taxes replacing premiums—that is progressive and maintains the total revenue necessary to sustain coverage. These implementation challenges are not arguments against universal coverage; they are design problems that the policy process must address. The fact that every other high-income democracy has solved analogous problems demonstrates they are solvable. What the Himmelstein administrative cost evidence, the
Papanicolas international comparison, and the coverage gap data together establish is that the status quo has its own severe costs—measured in administrative waste, preventable deaths, and medical bankruptcy—that make inaction as costly as action, only less visibly.

Mr. Adeyemi

Lily Chen

3 March 2025

The Remote Work Debate: Productivity, Equity, and the Future of the Office

The COVID-19 pandemic forced an unprecedented natural experiment in remote work, transitioning approximately 60 percent of American workers to work-from-home arrangements and generating a body of empirical evidence on productivity effects that economists and management researchers are still processing. Nicholas Bloom, Stanford economist and leading researcher on remote work outcomes, found in his studies of large-scale remote work implementations that fully remote work reduces individual productivity by approximately
10-20 percent compared to in-office work, primarily because of communication frictions, reduced collaboration, and difficulty maintaining mentorship relationships—but that hybrid arrangements (two to three days remote per week) produce productivity outcomes comparable to full in-office work while significantly increasing worker satisfaction and reducing turnover
(Bloom et al., 2015). This paper argues that the debate about remote work policy should not be framed as a binary choice between full remote and full in-office work but should instead focus on designing hybrid arrangements that capture the productivity and mentorship benefits of in-office collaboration for the portions of work that require them while preserving the flexibility and autonomy benefits of remote work for individual-focus tasks. The evidence from Bloom's natural experiments and the pandemic period suggests that blanket return-to-office mandates are as poorly calibrated to actual productivity needs as fully remote arrangements—both sacrifice productivity on one dimension to optimize on another.

Bloom's research on remote work has tracked this question across multiple contexts and designs, from his landmark Chinese call center experiment in 2015 to his analysis of pandemic-period productivity data. The 2015 call center study—which randomly assigned employees to work from home or in the office—found a 13 percent productivity improvement for remote workers, driven by a quieter working environment and fewer breaks, alongside reduced attrition and lower facility costs for the employer (Bloom et al., 2015). This finding appeared to contradict Bloom's later pandemic-era research showing productivity losses from remote work—but Bloom's own analysis of the apparent contradiction reveals the key variable:
the call center workers were experienced, individually-focused, and did not require intensive collaboration or mentorship, while pandemic-era remote work included large populations of new employees, managers with coordination responsibilities, and workers in roles requiring frequent team communication. The productivity effect of remote work depends entirely on the nature of the work being performed, which is why blanket policies in either direction misallocate the arrangement.

Critics of remote and hybrid work arrangements argue that the productivity and innovation losses from reduced in-person collaboration are more severe than large-sample studies capture, and that the data showing comparable hybrid productivity undercount the damage to junior employee development, spontaneous innovation, and organizational culture.
Management researchers including Martine Haas have documented that distributed teams show measurably lower rates of the informal knowledge transfer and serendipitous connection that drive innovation in creative and technical fields (Haas & Mortensen, 2016). This concern deserves serious attention because it addresses a real asymmetry in the productivity research:
individual output metrics (calls handled, code committed, documents drafted) capture one dimension of work productivity while missing the relationship-building and collaborative innovation that may have disproportionate long-term value. Bloom's research, however,

addresses this concern directly: his recommendations for hybrid arrangements specify that in-office days should be coordinated across teams (rather than individually chosen) precisely to preserve the collaborative benefit while maintaining flexibility for individual-focus work.
The question is not whether collaboration matters—it does—but whether the appropriate policy response is requiring five days of office attendance or designing two to three days of coordinated team presence.

The equity dimensions of remote work policy add a further argument that productivity analysis alone cannot resolve. Remote work disproportionately benefits workers in higher-income, knowledge-intensive occupations who can perform their work from home, while being unavailable to the service, healthcare, manufacturing, and logistics workers who cannot. Within the population of remote-eligible workers, the productivity and wellbeing benefits of flexible arrangements flow disproportionately to parents of young children, people with disabilities, and workers who live far from expensive urban centers—populations that have historically faced higher barriers to participation in high-wage knowledge work. A
return-to-office policy that eliminates flexible arrangements for knowledge workers without addressing the structural barriers to remote-eligible work for other populations preserves only the appearance of equal treatment while maintaining differential access to the documented wellbeing and retention benefits of schedule flexibility. Bloom's recommendations for hybrid work acknowledge this equity dimension but are limited to the remote-eligible workforce—a limitation that any comprehensive policy analysis must acknowledge.

The evidence from Bloom's research program and the management literature supports a nuanced conclusion: hybrid work arrangements, properly designed with coordinated in-office days and task-appropriate allocation, can capture the collaboration and mentorship benefits of office work while preserving the flexibility and autonomy benefits of remote work. Neither full

remote nor full in-office arrangements optimize across all the dimensions that matter—individual productivity, innovation, mentorship, worker wellbeing, and organizational culture. The debate about remote work policy should focus not on which arrangement is categorically superior but on which design choices in hybrid arrangements best serve the specific needs of particular teams and roles. Bloom's research provides the empirical foundation for that more targeted analysis while the equity concerns raised by remote work's unequal availability demand that any hybrid policy be evaluated not only for its productivity effects but for its distributional consequences across the workforce.

The management literature on distributed teams provides important context for interpreting Bloom's productivity findings. Haas and Mortensen's research on what distinguishes high-performing from low-performing distributed teams identified structured communication

protocols,

clearly

defined

roles,

and

deliberate

investment

in

relationship-building as the factors that most strongly predicted distributed team success—suggesting that the productivity costs of remote work are not fixed but are mediated by management practices that many organizations deploying hybrid arrangements have not systematically developed (Haas & Mortensen, 2016). The implication for hybrid policy design is that the research supports not merely a hybrid schedule but a hybrid management architecture: investment in asynchronous communication tools, documented decision-making processes, intentional in-office calendar coordination, and explicit mentorship programs for junior employees who cannot benefit from osmotic learning in the way in-person employees can. Organizations that implement hybrid schedules without the corresponding management infrastructure are likely to experience the productivity losses that critics of remote work predict, while organizations that invest in distributed team management will more closely approximate the optimistic outcomes that Bloom's research describes.

The Gibbs, Mengel, and Siemroth analysis of individual productivity data from information technology workers during the pandemic period provides granular evidence on the specific mechanisms through which remote work affects different types of work (Gibbs et al.,
2023). Their analysis found that hours worked increased significantly under remote arrangements—consistent with the elimination of commuting time and the blurring of work-home boundaries—while the share of time spent in meetings increased and the share of time in deep-focus individual work decreased. Output as measured by lines of code and task completions per hour declined despite the increase in total hours, suggesting that the increase in meeting time and communication overhead was not fully compensated by the additional

total hours. This finding is consistent with Bloom's recommendation for hybrid arrangements:
remote days should be protected for deep-focus individual work while in-office days are reserved for collaborative and communication-intensive activities, with explicit calendar management to prevent in-office days from being consumed by individual-focus work that could be done remotely.

The geographic dimensions of remote work availability create equity considerations that extend beyond the workforce of remote-eligible workers to urban housing and transportation systems. If remote work reduces the necessity of proximity to employment centers, it enables some workers to move to lower-cost areas—potentially exacerbating housing costs in destination communities while reducing demand in high-cost urban centers.
Barrero, Bloom, and Davis's analysis of post-pandemic work patterns found that this geographic dispersal was already occurring, with remote workers relocating at higher rates than in-office workers and often moving farther from employment centers (Barrero et al.,
2021). The macroeconomic implications of this shift are contested: optimists argue that distributed economic activity spreads growth more broadly and reduces the concentration of housing demand that has made urban areas unaffordable; pessimists argue that the agglomeration effects that make urban clustering productive—the knowledge spillovers, talent density, and spontaneous innovation that proximity generates—will be diminished if remote work allows geographic dispersal of the workers who produce them. The hybrid arrangement that Bloom recommends, by preserving some in-office collaboration while allowing geographic flexibility, may represent a partial resolution of this tension: preserving agglomeration benefits for the activities that require them while enabling geographic flexibility for the work that does not.

The question of whether remote and hybrid work arrangements will persist beyond the pandemic period as a stable feature of American labor markets or represent a temporary disruption is itself a research question that Barrero, Bloom, and Davis address directly. Their analysis of survey data from workers and employers found that both groups anticipated maintaining substantially more remote work than existed before the pandemic—not because of lingering pandemic concerns but because the forced experiment of 2020-2021 demonstrated to both parties that remote and hybrid arrangements were operationally viable in ways that pre-pandemic assumptions had not credited (Barrero et al., 2021). Workers who experienced remote work developed preferences for schedule flexibility that they treated as a compensating wage differential—meaning they were willing to accept somewhat lower wages in exchange for schedule flexibility, making hybrid work a form of compensation that employers could offer at lower financial cost than equivalent wage increases. This market dynamic suggests that hybrid arrangements will persist as a structural feature of knowledge-work labor markets regardless of individual employer preferences—because employers who require full in-office work will face a talent disadvantage relative to employers who offer comparable compensation with schedule flexibility.

The long-term implications of hybrid work normalization for urban commercial real estate, public transit systems, and downtown economies are significant and still unfolding.
Cities that built their transit and commercial infrastructure around five-day-per-week commuting patterns are experiencing substantially reduced utilization as hybrid workers commute two or three days rather than five. The fiscal consequences extend from transit authorities facing reduced fare revenue to downtown commercial landlords facing elevated vacancy rates to municipalities experiencing reduced tax revenue from restaurants, retail, and service businesses that depend on weekday lunch and post-work traffic. Bloom's research optimistically projects that hybrid work will stabilize at approximately two days remote per week for knowledge workers, which would maintain substantial commuting demand relative to fully remote arrangements (Barrero et al., 2021). But even this stabilization scenario requires infrastructure adaptation: transit systems designed for uniform daily ridership need to be reconceived for peak-Tuesday, peak-Wednesday patterns, and commercial districts need to develop service offerings appropriate for a reduced but concentrated in-office population. The hybrid work transition is not merely a labor management question but a structural economic adjustment whose costs and benefits are distributed across workers, employers, landlords, transit agencies, municipalities, and urban service workers in ways that policy has not yet fully addressed.

Selling Unhealthy Habits: The Ethics and Effects of Junk Food Marketing Directed at Children

Diego Martinez

AP Seminar, Period 5

Mrs. Park

Riverside High School

Diego Martinez
Mrs. Park
AP Seminar, Period 5
5 March 2025

Selling Unhealthy Habits: The Ethics and Effects of Junk Food Marketing Directed at
Children

Childhood obesity rates in the United States have more than tripled since the 1970s, with approximately 19.3 percent of children aged 2-19 now classified as obese according to the
Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. Research has consistently found that children who consume high levels of fast food, sugary beverages, and ultra-processed snack foods are at significantly elevated risk for obesity, type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and other chronic conditions. The food and beverage industry spends approximately $14 billion annually on marketing directed at children and adolescents, including advertising on television, social media platforms, in schools, and through product placement and branded entertainment (Cairns et al., 2013). The products most heavily marketed to children—fast food, breakfast cereals, candy, soft drinks, and salty snacks—are overwhelmingly high in sugar, saturated fat, and sodium. This paper argues that the scale and sophistication of junk food marketing directed at children represents an ethical violation that warrants regulatory restriction, because children's developmental limitations in understanding advertising intent mean they cannot provide meaningful informed consent to the persuasive communications directed at them—and the documented health consequences of the dietary patterns these communications promote fall disproportionately on children who lack the cognitive resources to resist them.

Researchers studying children's responses to food advertising have documented the specific cognitive mechanisms through which marketing directed at children differs from advertising directed at adults. Children under approximately eight years old lack the cognitive development to identify advertising as a persuasive communication distinct from informational content, meaning they process advertising claims as factual statements rather than as advocacy.
Cairns and colleagues' systematic review of the global evidence on food marketing and children's diet found consistent evidence that food advertising exposure shapes children's food preferences, their requests to parents, and their actual consumption patterns—with effects that persist even when parents attempt to counteract advertising messages through education about healthy eating (Cairns et al., 2013). The review found that the effects were stronger for younger children and for children from lower-income households, suggesting that the marketing's impact is greatest precisely where parental countervailing resources are most limited. The industry's response to these findings has been a series of voluntary pledges to limit advertising of the most nutritionally poor products to children under twelve, which independent evaluations have consistently found to have limited effectiveness in practice because the pledges are self-monitored, contain definitional exceptions that cover most products, and do not apply to social media and digital marketing where children's exposure is most concentrated.

The food and beverage industry argues that marketing to children is protected commercial speech, that parents bear primary responsibility for children's dietary choices, and that the correlation between advertising exposure and childhood obesity does not establish causation because children who watch more television also have less physical activity and more family food purchasing, making it difficult to isolate advertising's specific contribution.
These are reasonable arguments that deserve consideration. The causation concern is methodologically valid—randomized controlled trials on advertising exposure and obesity are

not ethically feasible for children, and observational studies face confounding challenges.
However, the evidence from countries that have implemented advertising restrictions provides a form of natural experiment: Chile's comprehensive restrictions on marketing of high-sugar, high-fat products to children, combined with mandatory front-of-package warning labels, produced measurable reductions in purchase of the targeted products in the two years following implementation. This evidence addresses the causation concern without requiring a randomized trial and demonstrates that the relationship between marketing and consumption is causal in the policy-relevant sense.

There are also important distributional dimensions to junk food marketing that make it an equity issue as well as a public health concern. Marketing of the most nutritionally poor products is concentrated in media environments—local broadcast television, urban billboards, convenience store advertising—that are disproportionately accessed by lower-income and minority children. The food and beverage industry has acknowledged this targeting in its own marketing research, using it to justify investment in these channels on audience reach and demographic effectiveness grounds. The result is that children who already face higher rates of food insecurity, fewer grocery options, and less access to safe outdoor physical activity are also subjected to more intense marketing of products associated with the chronic diseases that disproportionately affect their communities. Addressing junk food marketing restrictions purely as a consumer protection question without acknowledging this distributional dimension misses an important argument for why restrictions are justified: they would reduce a form of commercial exploitation that is concentrated among the most vulnerable children.

In conclusion, junk food marketing directed at children represents a documented harm that voluntary industry pledges have failed to address and that regulatory restriction can mitigate. The Cairns systematic review establishes the causal pathway from marketing

exposure to dietary preference and consumption change; the Chile natural experiment provides evidence that restrictions produce measurable consumption reductions; and the developmental psychology evidence establishes that children lack the cognitive resources to resist advertising in the way adult consumers can. The industry's arguments about parental responsibility and commercial speech protection deserve serious consideration but do not resolve the fundamental problem: marketing sophisticated persuasion to audiences who cannot recognize it as persuasion exploits a developmental vulnerability that regulatory frameworks exist precisely to protect.

The regulatory landscape for food marketing to children in the United States is fragmented and weak compared to the frameworks that several other high-income countries have implemented. The Federal Trade Commission has authority to regulate deceptive advertising but has historically been reluctant to restrict advertising that does not make factually false claims—meaning that emotionally manipulative advertising using cartoons, celebrities, and gamification to attract children to nutritionally poor products is permissible provided it does not include false factual claims about the product. The Children's Advertising
Review Unit (CARU), the industry self-regulatory body, sets guidelines for advertising directed at children under twelve but these are voluntary and apply only to CARU members.
Several states have considered legislation restricting junk food marketing in schools and on school grounds, and some districts have removed vending machines and fast food sponsorships from school facilities—but these local measures address a fraction of the marketing exposure that children experience across all media environments.

The digital marketing dimension of junk food advertising to children represents a significant expansion of marketing's reach that existing regulatory frameworks were not designed to address. Social media platforms, online games, and YouTube create environments where sponsored content, influencer partnerships, and gamified brand interactions blur the line between entertainment and advertising in ways that are even less transparent to children than traditional television advertising. Cairns and colleagues noted that digital marketing's interactive and apparently organic nature makes it particularly effective at building brand affinity without triggering the advertising-awareness defenses that older children develop for traditional media (Cairns et al., 2013). An eight-year-old who knows that a television commercial is trying to sell them something may still not recognize that a popular YouTuber's enthusiastic review of a fast food item was paid for by the brand—because the FTC's sponsored content disclosure requirements are difficult to enforce for child-directed influencer

content and are frequently not applied by creators who produce content primarily for young audiences.

The nutritional quality of the products marketed most heavily to children provides direct evidence that the marketing is not merely a neutral promotion of available products but a deliberate strategy to build preferences for products that are profitable for manufacturers regardless of their health effects on consumers. Harris and colleagues' analysis of food and beverage products marketed primarily to children found that they were overwhelmingly in the highest-sugar, highest-saturated-fat, and highest-sodium categories—and that the products specifically targeted at children (branded with characters, offered in child-sized portions, or packaged with toys) were on average nutritionally worse than the corresponding adult versions of the same product category (Harris et al., 2009). This finding is significant because it demonstrates that child-directed marketing is not merely promoting existing products to a new demographic but is promoting specifically reformulated products optimized for child preference and profitability rather than for child health. The design of child-specific products is a marketing strategy, not a response to children's nutritional needs.

The regulatory response that this paper advocates—restriction of marketing of nutritionally poor products in media primarily accessed by children under twelve, modeled on
Chile's approach and the UK's earlier advertising restrictions—would address the market failure that the current voluntary framework has failed to correct. The industry's argument that such restrictions would violate First Amendment commercial speech protections is undermined by the Supreme Court's consistent recognition that commercial speech directed at audiences who are legally incapable of contracting—including children—receives reduced constitutional protection. The precedent for restricting advertising of legal products to children exists in tobacco and alcohol regulations; applying the same principle to food products that carry

well-documented health risks for the child population seems straightforwardly consistent with established constitutional framework. The burden of justification rests with the industry to explain why a form of commercial communication directed at audiences who cannot legally consent to commercial transactions should receive the same constitutional protection as advertising directed at adults with full commercial legal capacity.

The school food environment provides an additional arena where marketing restrictions would have significant impact. Despite federal nutrition standards for school meals and competitive foods introduced under the Healthy Hunger-Free Kids Act, many schools maintain sponsorship relationships with fast food companies, display branded marketing materials in cafeterias and hallways, and operate vending machines stocked with products that would not meet the nutritional standards required of school meals served at lunch. These arrangements generate revenue for cash-strapped schools but expose students to commercial messaging in an environment that carries implicit institutional endorsement—a particularly problematic combination given that children are less likely to apply critical evaluation to brand messages in settings they associate with educational authority. Requiring schools to be advertisement-free zones for nutritionally poor products—as several states and many districts have done—is a low-cost regulatory intervention with direct access to the population most at risk from junk food marketing. The Chile front-of-package labeling initiative and advertising restrictions that
Taillie and colleagues evaluated demonstrate that regulatory environments significantly affect consumer behavior even for deeply established product preferences: two years after implementation, purchases of the labeled high-sugar and high-fat products declined significantly even among consumers who reported familiarity with the products before the policy change (Taillie et al., 2020). The United States has the regulatory infrastructure to implement comparable policies; what has been lacking is the political will to deploy it against the opposition of a concentrated and politically influential food and beverage industry.

Measuring Merit or Measuring Privilege?
An Examination of the Debate Over Standardized Testing

Jordan Williams

AP Seminar, Period 4

Ms. Thompson

Lincoln High School

Jordan Williams
Ms. Thompson
AP Seminar, Period 4
7 March 2025

Measuring Merit or Measuring Privilege? An Examination of the Debate Over
Standardized Testing

Standardized tests like the SAT and ACT have been used in college admissions for decades and are among the most debated tools in American education. Supporters argue that standardized tests provide an objective measure of academic ability that helps colleges identify talented students from diverse backgrounds who might not have strong grades due to differences in school quality. Critics argue that the tests are deeply influenced by socioeconomic background, that students from wealthier families have access to expensive test preparation resources that disadvantaged students do not, and that the tests measure test-taking skill as much as genuine academic potential. The COVID-19 pandemic led many colleges and universities to adopt test-optional admissions policies, generating new data about what happens to admissions outcomes when standardized tests are not required. Research on these policies has produced mixed findings that have given ammunition to both sides of the debate.
This paper will examine the arguments for and against standardized testing in college admissions, considering what different researchers and education experts have found and what these findings mean for the future of testing. While there are some arguments that suggest standardized tests may provide useful information in certain contexts, the picture is complicated enough that drawing strong conclusions about the right policy seems premature given the current state of the evidence.

Proponents of standardized testing argue that the tests serve an important equalization function by providing a common yardstick that allows colleges to compare applicants across different schools and educational contexts. Researchers like Jonathan Rothstein have studied how different admissions criteria relate to college outcomes and have found that standardized test scores predict first-year college grades better than high school GPA alone, particularly when grade inflation and variation in grading standards across schools is taken into account.
The argument is that without a common standardized measure, colleges will rely more heavily on subjective criteria like essays, interviews, and extracurricular activities that may favor students with more resources to develop and present these credentials. Some research has found that test scores can identify high-potential students from disadvantaged backgrounds whose grades might not fully reflect their abilities. Supporters of testing also point out that many of the most selective colleges have actually increased socioeconomic and racial diversity in their entering classes during periods when they used test scores, suggesting that the tests are not necessarily barriers to access for underrepresented students.

Critics of standardized testing raise a number of concerns about whether the tests are fair and whether they measure what they claim to measure. The most frequently cited concern is the strong correlation between SAT scores and family income: students from higher-income families consistently score higher on average than students from lower-income families, even when controlling for academic performance in school. Critics argue this reflects the advantages wealthier students have in test preparation, access to high-quality schools and tutoring, and familiarity with the format and conventions of standardized tests. Research by Claude Steele and others has also documented stereotype threat effects in standardized testing contexts—the phenomenon where members of groups that are stereotyped as performing poorly on tests actually perform worse when that stereotype is made salient, suggesting that test scores may underestimate the abilities of some students from underrepresented groups (Steele & Aronson,

1995). These findings raise questions about whether the tests are measuring academic potential or are measuring the effects of social inequality and psychological pressure.

The test-optional movement has generated new evidence about what happens when colleges stop requiring standardized tests, though this evidence is still being analyzed and the findings are complex. Some studies have found that test-optional policies have increased the racial and socioeconomic diversity of applicant pools without significantly reducing academic quality in the entering class, suggesting that the tests may have been unnecessary barriers for some qualified applicants. Other analyses have found that when test scores are not available, admissions officers rely more heavily on other factors that may also be correlated with socioeconomic advantage, like the quality of the applicant's high school, their extracurricular record, and the sophistication of their essays. This has led some researchers to question whether test-optional policies actually reduce inequality in admissions or simply shift the inequality to different parts of the application. The evidence on long-term outcomes—whether students admitted under test-optional policies succeed at similar rates to students admitted under test-required policies—is still emerging and does not yet provide clear guidance.

The debate about standardized testing in college admissions involves genuinely difficult tradeoffs between competing values and goals—academic excellence, equal access, institutional diversity, and efficient use of admissions resources—and the empirical evidence does not clearly favor one approach over the others. Different stakeholders weight these values differently and will therefore reach different conclusions about the right policy even from the same evidence. What does seem clear is that neither blanket defense of standardized testing as an objective merit measure nor blanket condemnation of it as a proxy for privilege fully captures what the research shows. The question of how colleges should use standardized tests, if at all, is likely to continue to be actively debated as more data from test-optional policies

accumulates and as researchers develop better methods for understanding what different assessment tools actually measure and predict. Reasonable people can and do disagree about the right approach, and any strong policy conclusions should be held with appropriate caution given the complexity of the evidence and the difficulty of the underlying tradeoffs.

The historical context of standardized testing in American education adds complexity to the contemporary debate. The SAT was developed in part to identify talented students from non-elite backgrounds who might not otherwise gain access to selective colleges—a mission of democratization that its early proponents emphasized. Whether the test has served this democratizing function in practice is disputed: some research suggests that standardized tests do help identify high-ability students from less advantaged backgrounds who would otherwise be overlooked; other research emphasizes that the wealthy have become increasingly skilled at optimizing test scores through preparation resources, private tutoring, and multiple retakes.
The history of the test's use—including periods in which it was explicitly deployed as a gatekeeping mechanism against specific immigrant and minority groups, and periods in which it was used to expand access—means that the contemporary debate cannot be understood without accounting for this legacy, even if it does not determine the right contemporary policy.

The global context of standardized testing offers some comparative evidence, though international comparisons in education are complicated by the many other factors that differ between national systems. Some high-performing education systems—including those in
Finland, which consistently scores near the top of international assessments—do not use high-stakes standardized tests for university admissions in the way the United States does, relying instead on course grades, teacher recommendations, and subject-specific examinations.
Other high-performing systems—including South Korea and Japan—use highly consequential national standardized examinations for university admission. The diversity of approaches among high-performing systems does not resolve the American debate but suggests that neither the presence nor absence of standardized testing is a necessary condition for educational success, which implies that the choice of assessment approach involves tradeoffs that cannot be resolved simply by pointing to international examples.

Looking at both sides of this debate, it seems that the question of how to use standardized tests in college admissions is genuinely complex and does not admit an easy answer. The correlation between test scores and family income is real and concerning. At the same time, simply eliminating standardized tests may shift the advantages to other application components that are also correlated with socioeconomic status. The evidence from test-optional policies is still too new to draw definitive conclusions. Different colleges with different missions serving different student populations may legitimately reach different conclusions about how to balance these considerations in their own admissions processes.
What seems most important is that admissions policies be evaluated honestly for their actual effects on access and outcomes, not just their stated intentions—and that the debate continue to be informed by careful research rather than by ideological commitment to either the continuation or elimination of standardized testing.

One dimension of the standardized testing debate that deserves more attention is the question of what these tests are actually measuring and whether whatever they measure is the right thing to be assessing for college readiness. Critics like Hiss and Franks argue that the
SAT and ACT are better understood as proxies for socioeconomic advantage than as independent measures of academic potential, and that their widespread use reflects institutional inertia and ease of administration more than evidence that they add unique value to the admissions process (Hiss & Franks, 2014). Research on which factors best predict college success has produced mixed findings about how much incremental predictive validity test scores add beyond high school grades. Some studies find that the SAT does add predictive validity for college GPA; others find that high school grades are the stronger predictor and that test scores add little once grades are accounted for. The predictive validity debate matters because if test scores do not add substantially to what high school grades already tell colleges about applicants, the costs of testing—financial, psychological, and in terms of access—may outweigh the benefits.

The experience of the COVID-19 pandemic, which forced virtually all selective colleges to adopt test-optional policies almost simultaneously, has provided an unprecedented natural experiment in whether the absence of test scores changes the quality or diversity of entering classes. Early analyses of this period suggest that test-optional policies did increase the diversity of applicant pools at many institutions and did not obviously reduce academic quality in entering classes, though the conclusions are complicated by other factors that also changed during the pandemic period. Several highly selective institutions have announced plans to make test-optional policies permanent while others have returned to test requirements, reflecting genuine disagreement among admission professionals about what the evidence shows. The fact that selective institutions are reaching different conclusions from the same evidence base suggests that the right answer may genuinely vary across institutional

contexts—that a test requirement makes sense for some colleges and not for others depending on their applicant pools, their missions, and what they are trying to predict. This variability is itself an argument against treating the question as having one right answer that should apply uniformly across all institutions.

These considerations together suggest that thoughtful, context-sensitive approaches to standardized testing—rather than blanket adoption or rejection—are most likely to serve students and institutions well as the evidence base continues to develop. The ongoing natural experiment created by different institutions' varying policies will eventually provide clearer answers than are currently available, and that evidence should guide future policy rather than ideological commitments in either direction.

Training the Attentive Mind: Mindfulness-Based Interventions and Working Memory
Capacity in Adults

Working memory—the cognitive system responsible for temporarily storing and manipulating information during active mental tasks—is among the strongest predictors of academic achievement, professional performance, and general cognitive function across the lifespan, yet its capacity is both limited and subject to deterioration under conditions of chronic stress and attentional overload (Baddeley, 2003). Approximately 37 percent of American adults report experiencing high levels of stress in a given month, and chronic stress has been shown to impair working memory function through cortisol-mediated effects on prefrontal cortex activity—a mechanism that links psychological stress to cognitive performance at the neurobiological level. Mindfulness-based interventions (MBIs), which involve structured training in non-judgmental present-moment awareness through practices like focused attention meditation and open monitoring, have attracted growing research attention as potential cognitive training tools. This investigation asks: to what extent do mindfulness-based interventions improve working memory capacity in adults, and through what attentional and neurobiological mechanisms does this effect operate? The available evidence from controlled trials and neuroimaging studies establishes a robust positive effect on working memory-relevant attention regulation, with a plausible mechanistic account linking mindfulness practice to prefrontal cortex function, while significant methodological heterogeneity across studies limits confidence in precise effect size estimates.

The neurobiological mechanisms through which mindfulness training may enhance working memory capacity are increasingly well-characterized. Zeidan and colleagues' early controlled studies using attention regulation tasks demonstrated that even brief mindfulness training (four days of 20-minute sessions) produced significant improvements in sustained attention and working memory performance compared to listening to audiobooks, with neuroimaging data consistent with increased prefrontal regulatory activity (Zeidan et al.,
2010). Jha and colleagues extended this research to high-stress real-world populations, finding that military cohorts who received MBI training before deployment showed maintenance of working memory capacity over an eight-week high-stress period, while untrained cohorts showed significant working memory decline (Jha et al., 2010). This maintenance-under-stress finding is particularly important because it addresses the mechanism: if mindfulness training preserves working memory under conditions that normally impair it, the effect is likely mediated through stress regulation rather than through direct capacity expansion—a distinction with important implications for intervention design. Figure 1 illustrates the neuroimaging findings from Zeidan and colleagues: mindfulness-trained participants show greater prefrontal activation during working memory tasks relative to both pre-training baseline and active control groups, consistent with enhanced top-down attentional regulation.

The perspectives of Zeidan, Jha, and Creswell on mindfulness and working memory represent a productive convergence with important methodological differences. Creswell's active-control designs represent the most conservative methodological position, accepting smaller effect sizes in exchange for greater confidence in

causal attribution. The disagreement between these perspectives reflects genuine uncertainty about whether MBIs are capacity-expanding interventions, stress-buffering interventions, or attentional training interventions—and the answer has practical consequences for how and for whom they should be prescribed. Together these perspectives establish a mechanistic account without resolving the question of which mechanism is primary—a productive uncertainty that points toward the next generation of research questions.

This investigation established that mindfulness-based interventions produce robust improvements in working memory-relevant attention regulation in adults, with neurobiological evidence linking the effect to prefrontal cortex function and default mode network regulation.
The research question asked to what extent MBIs improve working memory capacity and through what mechanisms: the honest answer is that improvements in working memory performance are well-documented across multiple well-designed studies, with the Zeidan, Jha, and Creswell research programs providing converging evidence from different populations and designs. The mechanism question is partially resolved—attentional regulation and stress buffering are both supported—but the relative contribution of each pathway remains an open research question. The practical implication is that brief MBIs are well-supported as low-cost cognitive performance and stress management tools for high-demand populations, while more intensive programs show greater and more durable effects for specific working memory capacity enhancement.

The clinical application of mindfulness training for working memory enhancement raises practical questions about dose, format, and population that the research base does not yet fully resolve. Klingberg's review of working memory training research more broadly found that cognitive training effects tend to be most robust on tasks closely related to the training activity and attenuate as transfer to more distal tasks is assessed—suggesting that mindfulness training's benefits for working memory may be partially limited to the attentional regulation components of working memory rather than generalizing to all working memory tasks
(Klingberg, 2010). The evidence from Zeidan's brief four-day protocols shows faster onset but smaller and less durable effects than Jha's longer programs; Creswell's RCT provides the most rigorous causal evidence but for an intensive format that may not be scalable. The practical question for clinical or educational implementation is which format achieves the best outcome-to-resource ratio for a given population and purpose—a question that current evidence can partially address for specific populations (military cohorts, clinical samples) but that requires additional research for broader deployment. The convergence of Zeidan, Jha, and
Creswell on positive effects across diverse populations and designs provides a solid foundation for this next generation of dose-optimization and dissemination research.

Early Investments: Universal Pre-Kindergarten Programs and Kindergarten Readiness
Outcomes

Access to high-quality preschool education varies dramatically across the United States by income, geography, and state policy, with approximately 41 percent of four-year-olds not enrolled in any preschool program and lower-income children—who would benefit most from high-quality early education—disproportionately unserved. Nobel Prize-winning economist
James Heckman has argued extensively that early childhood education represents the highest-return social investment available to policymakers, with returns estimated at 7-13
percent annually through effects on adult earnings, health, and reduced costs from crime and welfare use (Heckman et al., 2013). Several states have implemented universal pre-kindergarten programs—offering publicly funded pre-K to all four-year-olds regardless of income—providing natural experiments for evaluating these programs' effects. This investigation asks: to what extent do universal pre-kindergarten programs improve school readiness outcomes in kindergarten among children who otherwise lack access to high-quality early education? The evidence from the most rigorously evaluated programs in Oklahoma and
Georgia establishes substantial readiness gains, particularly for lower-income and minority children, though the persistence of these gains through elementary school remains contested.

The most rigorous evaluations of universal pre-K programs in the United States have focused on Oklahoma's Tulsa program, which Gormley and colleagues evaluated using a

regression discontinuity design that compared children who just qualified for the program by age cutoff to children who just missed the cutoff—an approach that substantially reduces selection bias compared to simple comparisons of enrollees and non-enrollees (Gormley et al.,
2005). Gormley's evaluation found significant positive effects on language, literacy, and cognitive skills at kindergarten entry, with the largest effects concentrated among Hispanic and lower-income children—the populations for whom the program provided the greatest increment over their counterfactual childcare and home environments. The regression discontinuity design's power is that it compares children who are essentially identical except for one characteristic: which side of the age cutoff they fell on. This near-random variation allows reasonably strong causal inference without a formal randomized controlled trial.
Heckman's analysis of the longer-term evidence from high-quality programs like Perry
Preschool and Abecedarian found that the readiness gains translate into lasting advantages in adult outcomes, though critics have noted that both programs were intensive, expensive interventions that may not generalize to the more variable quality of large-scale universal programs.

The question of whether universal pre-K's readiness gains persist through elementary school—or whether they 'fade out' by second or third grade as children in the comparison group catch up—is one of the most contested in early education research. Heckman argues that apparent fade-out in test scores reflects the limitations of standardized cognitive measures rather than genuine loss of benefit, because the social-emotional and non-cognitive skills developed in high-quality early education continue to compound even when they no longer show up in standardized test comparisons (Heckman et al., 2013). Gormley's follow-up analyses of Tulsa children through elementary school found persistent advantages in grade retention and special education placement rates even after the test score gap narrowed, consistent with Heckman's non-cognitive skills hypothesis. critics of universal versus targeted pre-K investment, holds that the evidence for universal programs is less compelling than for targeted high-quality programs serving the children with the greatest need—and that the additional cost of universality, which includes providing pre-K
to middle- and upper-income children who already have access to quality early education, reduces the program's return on investment relative to a well-targeted alternative.

The targeted versus universal design question is the most policy-relevant unresolved issue: the evidence supports investment in quality early education for underserved children, but whether universality adds value beyond targeting depends on cost, quality maintenance at scale, and political economy considerations that the current evidence base cannot resolve.

The implementation quality question is central to interpreting the evidence on universal pre-K and deserves explicit attention. The Perry Preschool and Abecedarian programs that
Heckman analyzes for long-term returns were intensive, high-quality programs with well-trained teachers, low child-to-teacher ratios, and individualized developmental support—characteristics that are not reliably present in large-scale universal programs.
Gormley's Tulsa evaluations are more directly relevant to universal programs because Tulsa's pre-K operates within the public school system with certified teachers at public school pay scales—a model that is more replicable than the laboratory-like conditions of Perry and

The central policy question is whether the teacher certification, curriculum alignment, and class size standards that produced Tulsa's positive outcomes can be maintained as programs expand to serve larger and more geographically distributed populations, or whether expansion pressure will drive cost reductions that erode the quality characteristics on which the evidence for effectiveness is based.

The fiscal dimensions of universal versus targeted pre-K frame the policy choice in terms of opportunity costs. Heckman's return-on-investment estimates assume that the quality of the programs generating the returns is maintained at scale—an assumption that Heckman himself has qualified by emphasizing the importance of program quality as a precondition for the estimated returns. The NIEER state preschool yearbook data shows considerable variation in quality benchmarks met by state pre-K programs, with several large universal programs falling short of the teacher qualification, curriculum, and assessment standards associated with the highest-quality programs (NIEER, 2022). A targeted program that concentrates resources on children with the greatest need—ensuring high quality by limiting scale—may achieve better outcomes per dollar than a universal program that dilutes quality across a larger population. The question for policymakers is whether the political and administrative benefits of universality—broader constituency, simpler eligibility, reduced stigma—justify the

potential quality tradeoff, or whether targeting with quality assurance is the more defensible approach given the evidence base.

The most defensible near-term policy implication of the available evidence is that investments in quality early education for children without access to high-quality alternatives are well-justified by the research, while the case for universal expansion beyond this targeted core rests more on equity and political economy grounds than on evidence of incremental educational return for children who already have access to quality early care. Both Heckman and Gormley would agree that improving program quality for underserved children is the highest-priority use of early education resources.

Moving Through the Dark: Physical Exercise as an Intervention for Major Depressive
Disorder

Major depressive disorder affects approximately 21 million American adults annually, making it one of the most prevalent mental health conditions in the United States and a significant driver of disability and healthcare costs. While pharmaceutical antidepressants and psychotherapy remain the first-line treatments, a substantial body of research has examined whether structured physical exercise can serve as an effective adjunct or alternative treatment for depression, particularly for patients who do not respond adequately to medication or who prefer non-pharmacological approaches. The biological rationale for exercise as a depression intervention is well-established: aerobic exercise increases the availability of monoamine neurotransmitters (serotonin, dopamine, norepinephrine) that are associated with mood regulation, stimulates the release of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) that supports neuroplasticity in areas relevant to depression, and reduces the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis hyperactivation associated with chronic stress. This investigation asks: how effectively does structured aerobic exercise reduce symptoms of major depressive disorder in adults, and does exercise produce clinically significant improvements comparable to standard pharmacological treatment?

The most influential study of exercise as depression treatment is the SMILE trial
(Standard Medical Intervention and Long-term Exercise) by Blumenthal and colleagues, which

randomized 202 adults with major depression to aerobic exercise, antidepressant medication
(sertraline), or combination exercise plus medication for sixteen weeks (Blumenthal et al.,
2007). At sixteen weeks, all three groups showed significant improvement in depression symptoms, with no statistically significant difference between exercise and medication in rates of remission—approximately 40-45 percent for exercise and medication groups versus 30
percent for placebo. The ten-month follow-up showed that patients who had been in the exercise group were significantly less likely to have relapsed than those in the medication group, suggesting that exercise may produce more durable benefits by addressing underlying neuroplasticity rather than managing symptoms acutely. Blumenthal's mechanistic hypothesis focuses on the BDNF pathway: exercise-induced BDNF upregulation supports hippocampal neurogenesis and prefrontal cortex plasticity that are impaired in chronic depression, potentially addressing a root biological vulnerability rather than just compensating for neurotransmitter depletion.

Cochrane meta-analyses of exercise trials for depression have generally confirmed that exercise produces moderate to large effects on depressive symptoms compared to no treatment, but have noted significant methodological heterogeneity across studies. Schuch and colleagues'
updated meta-analysis found that the effect of exercise on depression was robust even in studies with low risk of bias, with an effect size in the moderate range—meaningful but somewhat smaller than the largest individual studies suggested (Schuch et al., 2016). Some researchers have argued that the apparent efficacy of exercise in trials may be partly attributable to the non-specific benefits of structured activity —social interaction, improved sleep, sense of mastery—rather than to the specific neurobiological mechanisms proposed. The comparator matters: exercise significantly outperforms no treatment and wait-list controls, but studies comparing exercise to active controls like yoga, stretching, or social activities show smaller effects, suggesting that some of exercise's benefit may not be specific to aerobic

exercise per se.

This investigation found that structured aerobic exercise is an effective intervention for major depressive disorder with effects comparable to antidepressant medication in well-designed trials. The SMILE trial's head-to-head comparison with sertraline provides the strongest evidence for this equivalence, and the follow-up data suggesting better long-term outcomes for exercise is a notable finding that warrants further replication. The mechanistic account linking exercise to BDNF and hippocampal neuroplasticity provides a plausible biological explanation for these effects. The evidence base supports exercise as a first-line or adjunct

treatment

option

for

depression,

particularly

for

patients

who

prefer

non-pharmacological approaches or who have not responded adequately to medication. The practical challenge is adherence: maintaining an exercise regimen requires motivation and resources that are difficult to sustain for individuals experiencing major depression, and clinical implementation requires structured support rather than simple prescription.

The adherence challenge in exercise research deserves particular attention because it affects both the internal validity of trials and the external validity for clinical implementation.
In the Blumenthal SMILE trial, participants were supervised during exercise sessions, providing levels of support and accountability that are not available in standard clinical settings. When exercise is prescribed rather than supervised—as it typically is in outpatient mental health settings—adherence rates are substantially lower and the benefits documented in supervised trials may not fully materialize. Schuch and colleagues' meta-analysis found heterogeneity in effects that correlated with supervision intensity, with supervised programs producing larger and more consistent effects than unsupervised or partially supervised programs (Schuch et al., 2016). This finding has practical implications for how exercise should be implemented as a clinical treatment: it may require more structured support than a simple prescription, potentially through group exercise programs, structured fitness programs, or integrated behavioral activation components that address the motivational deficits that are central to the depression experience.

The question of exercise as a standalone treatment versus an adjunct to standard pharmacological or psychotherapy treatment is important for clinical recommendations. The
SMILE trial's finding that exercise produced comparable remission rates to sertraline at sixteen weeks supports standalone use, but the trial was conducted in a population of adults with mild-to-moderate depression rather than severe depression—the population for whom medication is most strongly indicated. For patients with severe depression, exercise should likely be recommended as an adjunct to rather than a replacement for established treatments, with the evidence supporting its use as a way to enhance outcomes, improve the durability of response, and provide patients with an additional tool for maintaining wellbeing over the long term. The NIMH's treatment guidelines for major depression recommend exercise as a complementary approach alongside evidence-based treatments, which reflects the current

evidence base appropriately (NIMH, 2023).

The growing body of evidence supporting exercise as an effective depression intervention has practical implications for how mental health care is delivered in settings where pharmacological treatment is not available, affordable, or acceptable to patients. In lowand middle-income countries where psychiatric medication is often inaccessible and where the mental health treatment gap is largest, structured exercise programs represent a potentially scalable and cost-effective intervention that can be delivered without specialized clinical infrastructure. Even in high-income countries, the evidence supports integrating exercise recommendations more systematically into primary care pathways for mild-to-moderate depression—not as a replacement for evidence-based treatments but as a complementary approach that increases the range of options available to patients and that may improve long-term outcomes by building self-management capacity alongside symptom relief.

Supports Student Learning and Development

The Case for the Arts: How Arts Education Supports Student Learning and
Development

Arts education—including music, visual art, theater, and dance programs in schools—has faced increasing pressure in recent decades as schools have redirected resources toward tested subjects in reading and mathematics. A significant body of research has examined whether arts education contributes to student learning and development in ways that justify its inclusion in school curricula, and this research has generated considerable interest from educators and policymakers who believe arts programs provide unique educational value that cannot be replaced by other subjects. Some studies have found that students who participate in arts programs perform better academically, have higher graduation rates, and show stronger social-emotional development than students who do not participate. However, the research in this area faces significant methodological challenges because students who choose to participate in arts programs may differ from those who do not in ways that independently predict better outcomes. This investigation asks what the research shows about how arts education affects student academic and developmental outcomes. The available research provides some positive evidence for arts education's benefits, though the methodological limitations of many studies make it difficult to draw firm conclusions about causation.

A study by Catterall and colleagues examining arts participation among a large national sample of students found that students who were highly engaged in arts activities over their school careers showed substantially higher rates of academic achievement, community service, and civic engagement than students with low arts engagement (Catterall et al., 1999). Students in the lowest socioeconomic quartile who were highly engaged in arts showed particularly strong advantages compared to their low-arts peers, suggesting that arts participation may matter especially for disadvantaged students who may have fewer other enrichment opportunities. However, as Catterall and colleagues acknowledged, these findings are correlational—students who choose to participate heavily in arts may be more motivated, have more supportive parents, or attend better schools, and the study cannot determine whether arts participation caused the academic advantages or whether both were produced by these underlying characteristics. More recent research by Bowen and colleagues attempted to address this selection bias by using lottery-based admissions to arts-focused schools as a natural experiment, finding some evidence of positive effects on academic outcomes for students who attended arts-integrated schools compared to lottery losers who enrolled elsewhere.

Some researchers and educators argue that the focus on measuring arts education's effects on academic outcomes like test scores and graduation rates fundamentally misunderstands what arts education is for. From this perspective, arts education should be valued for its intrinsic contributions to creative development, aesthetic experience, and cultural participation—goals that are not captured by academic outcome measures. This argument has intuitive appeal and is consistent with a broader educational philosophy that values the development of the whole person rather than optimizing academic performance metrics. The policy challenge is that in resource-constrained school environments, advocating for arts education on intrinsic grounds alone is often insufficient to protect programs from

cuts—which is why researchers have focused on demonstrating academic benefits. The tension between these perspectives reflects a genuine disagreement about what schools are for and how educational success should be measured.

In conclusion, the research on arts education and student outcomes provides some evidence of positive associations between arts participation and academic achievement, particularly for disadvantaged students, but the methodological limitations of much of this research make it difficult to establish clear causal effects. The research question asked what the evidence shows about how arts education affects student outcomes, and the honest answer is that the correlational evidence is promising but causation is not firmly established. More rigorous research using lottery-based and other quasi-experimental designs would help clarify the extent to which arts education itself, rather than characteristics of students who choose arts, produces the positive outcomes observed. In the meantime, advocates for arts education have compelling intrinsic and correlational arguments for preservation of these programs, even if the causal evidence for academic benefits remains incomplete.

The relationship between arts education and academic outcomes may be mediated by effects on social-emotional learning that are not captured by standardized test scores but that influence academic engagement and persistence. Arts participation has been associated in some research with improvements in self-regulation, perseverance, collaborative skills, and intrinsic motivation—characteristics that are sometimes called non-cognitive skills and that predict long-term outcomes independently of cognitive test performance. Winner, Goldstein, and Vincent-Lancrin's review of the evidence for arts education's effects on thinking skills found that the evidence was strongest for the specific thinking skills that arts subjects directly train—musical training's effects on auditory discrimination and drawing's effects on spatial visualization—rather than for broad academic benefits (Winner et al., 2013). This finding supports a more modest claim for arts education: that it develops specific skills and dispositions that have value in their own right, rather than that it broadly improves academic performance across subjects.

Advocates for arts education face a difficult rhetorical situation: if they argue for arts on the basis of broad academic benefits that the evidence does not robustly support, they are vulnerable to being discredited when the evidence is examined critically; if they argue for arts on intrinsic grounds alone, they may lose the political battles that determine school resource allocation. The most defensible position may be one that honestly acknowledges the methodological limitations of the academic outcomes literature while making the strongest possible case for arts education's unique developmental contributions—creativity, aesthetic development, cultural participation, and the specific thinking skills that Winner and colleagues identify—that are not substitutable by other parts of the curriculum. This argument requires educating policymakers and the public about what education is for, which is a more challenging task than providing a simple causal claim about test scores, but may be the only position that is simultaneously honest and persuasive in the long run.

Ultimately, the arts education debate reflects a broader tension in educational policy between outcome-based justifications that can be measured and compared and value-based justifications that resist quantification. The research reviewed here supports modest claims for arts education's academic contributions while suggesting that the strongest arguments for arts programs rest on what they uniquely contribute to human development in ways that are not captured by academic performance metrics—creative expression, cultural engagement, and the development of aesthetic judgment and craft. These are legitimate educational goals that deserve institutional support on their own terms, without requiring proof that they improve mathematics scores.

Social Media and Body Image: Understanding the Connection

Social media has become a major part of life for teenagers and young adults around the world, with platforms like Instagram, TikTok, and Snapchat being used by billions of people to share photos and videos of themselves and others. One topic that has received a lot of attention from researchers, parents, and mental health professionals is the relationship between social media use and body image—how people feel about their own bodies and appearance.
According to Wikipedia, body image refers to a person's perceptions, feelings, and thoughts about their own body and is an important component of self-esteem and mental health
(Wikipedia, 2024). Many people have expressed concern that social media exposes young people, especially girls and young women, to unrealistic beauty standards and edited images that make them feel bad about their own bodies. This is an interesting and important topic to investigate because body image issues can affect mental health and wellbeing in significant ways. This investigation asks what social media does to teenage body image and whether social media use is associated with worse body image outcomes among teenagers. There is some research on this topic that provides useful insights, though the research does not all point in the same direction.

Some research has found that spending more time on social media is associated with worse body image among teenagers, particularly girls. The idea is that social media platforms show a lot of highly edited and filtered photos of attractive people with idealized bodies, and

when young people constantly compare themselves to these images they may feel worse about their own appearance. This is sometimes called social comparison, and it is something that researchers have studied in various contexts. Fardouly and colleagues have done research suggesting that exposure to images of thin or toned bodies on social media can increase body dissatisfaction among women who engage in appearance comparisons (Fardouly et al., 2015).
This research found that looking at Facebook was associated with increased body dissatisfaction and more negative mood in a laboratory experiment where participants were randomly assigned to browse Facebook or a website about travel. The experiment suggests a causal relationship, though the short-term laboratory setting may not perfectly reflect how social media affects body image in real life over longer periods of time.

Not all research has found that social media is harmful to body image, and some researchers have suggested that the relationship is more complicated than it might seem. Some studies have found that body-positive content on social media, which shows diverse body types and promotes self-acceptance rather than comparison, can actually improve body satisfaction among users who are exposed to it. This suggests that what kind of content people see on social media might matter as much or more than how much time they spend on social media. Some researchers have also pointed out that social media can provide communities of support for people who struggle with body image issues, where they can find others who share their experiences and receive encouragement. The picture seems to be that social media can be either harmful or helpful for body image depending on the content that people are exposed to and how they engage with that content.

In conclusion, this investigation found that social media use is associated with body image concerns among teenagers in some research, particularly when use involves exposure to idealized images and upward social comparisons. The research question asked what social

media does to teenage body image, and the answer seems to be that it depends a lot on what kind of content is being viewed and how the person is engaging with it. Negative social comparison with idealized images appears to be one pathway through which social media can worsen body image, but body-positive content and community support can have positive effects. More research is needed to better understand these relationships across different platforms and populations, and to determine what kinds of interventions might be most effective in helping young people have healthier relationships with social media and their own body image.

Researchers have also looked at how social media platforms might be designed or used differently to reduce harm to body image. Some studies have suggested that Instagram's introduction of features that hide public like counts reduced some aspects of social comparison behavior, though the evidence on whether this actually improved body image outcomes is limited. Others have proposed that media literacy education—teaching young people how images on social media are edited, filtered, and curated to present idealized versions of appearance—might help reduce the impact of exposure to unrealistic images. There is some research suggesting that this kind of education can help people feel less affected by social comparison after seeing idealized images, though the long-term effects and the effectiveness in real-world conditions are not well established. The challenge is that media literacy education requires sustained intervention and cannot eliminate the fundamental dynamic of exposure to a large volume of appearance-focused content that characterizes many young people's social media use.

The research on social media and body image continues to develop as platforms change and as researchers develop better methods for studying media effects in real-world conditions rather than just in laboratory experiments. The basic finding from the available research—that social comparison with idealized images is associated with worse body image—seems fairly consistent, though the magnitude of these effects and their relationship to serious mental health outcomes like eating disorders is less clear. Parents, educators, and young people themselves would benefit from more research on which specific patterns of social media use are most harmful and most protective for body image, and on what kinds of interventions are most effective in different contexts. The conversation about social media and body image is likely to remain an important one as these platforms continue to play a central role in how young people communicate, form social connections, and understand expectations about appearance and identity.

Given what the research shows, parents and educators can encourage young people to be more thoughtful about how they use social media and what kinds of accounts they follow.
Choosing to follow accounts that show diverse body types and promote self-acceptance rather than idealized or heavily edited images may help reduce the negative comparison effects that research has identified. These are practical steps that individuals can take while the larger conversations about platform design and regulation continue.
