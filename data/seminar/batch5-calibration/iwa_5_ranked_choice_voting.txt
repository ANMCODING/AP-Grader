Beyond the Spoiler Effect: How Ranked-Choice Voting Advances Majority Rule and
Reduces Polarization

Plurality voting—the system in which the candidate with the most votes wins, regardless of whether that candidate receives majority support—produces outcomes that systematically underrepresent voter preferences in multicandidate elections and creates the structural incentive for strategic rather than sincere voting that political scientists identify as a primary driver of America's two-party duopoly. Rob Richie, executive director of FairVote and the leading institutional advocate and analyst of ranked-choice voting (RCV) in the United
States, has documented across multiple studies and election cycles that plurality voting generates avoidable pathologies— the spoiler effect, minority winners, strategic voting, and candidate deterrence—that RCV is specifically designed to correct (Richie & Bouricius, 2018).
Under RCV, voters rank candidates in order of preference; if no candidate achieves a majority of first-choice votes, the last-place candidate is eliminated and their votes redistributed to the next-ranked choices, continuing until one candidate achieves a majority. This paper argues that ranked-choice voting advances majority rule, reduces the spoiler effect, and creates incentives for less negative campaigning—constituting a demonstrated institutional improvement over plurality voting for single-winner elections that the empirical record of RCV jurisdictions supports and the theoretical objections to RCV cannot overcome. Richie's synthesis of election data from Maine, Alaska, and more than fifty American cities that have adopted RCV provides the empirical foundation for this argument, while recent academic research validates the

behavioral and strategic dimensions of his policy analysis.

Richie and colleagues' analysis of RCV elections across the United States documents consistent patterns that support the theoretical case for the system. In plurality elections with three or more competitive candidates, the winning candidate frequently receives less than forty percent of the vote—a structural outcome that the Constitution's design of majority rule for consequential decisions was intended to prevent in democratic contexts. RCV systematically produces majority winners: because votes are redistributed through elimination rounds, the final winner receives more than fifty percent of active ballots in all RCV elections by design
(Richie & Bouricius, 2018). The spoiler effect—in which a third-party or independent candidate draws votes primarily from one of the major candidates, potentially causing their least-preferred candidate to win—is structurally eliminated under RCV because voters can rank a third-party candidate first without sacrificing their influence on the race between major candidates. systems across a sample of competitive elections, showing that plurality winners regularly receive less than forty percent of the vote while RCV winners must ultimately receive more than fifty percent of continuing ballots. Richie's analysis of polling data from RCV
jurisdictions finds that voters consistently report higher satisfaction with the ability to vote their genuine preferences under RCV than under plurality voting, consistent with the theory that plurality voting induces strategic rather than sincere voting.

The relationship between voting systems and campaign behavior has attracted growing academic attention as polarization has increased in American politics. Donovan and colleagues' analysis of voter survey data from RCV and plurality jurisdictions found that voters in RCV elections reported significantly lower levels of negative campaigning and candidate attacks than voters in comparable plurality elections—a finding consistent with the theoretical prediction that RCV creates incentives for candidates to appeal to their opponents' supporters as potential second- or third-choice voters rather than attacking those opponents (Donovan et al., 2016). The strategic incentive under plurality voting is to mobilize and energize the base while suppressing opponent turnout through negative attacks; the strategic incentive under
RCV is to maximize first-choice votes while also attracting second-choice support from voters who rank a competitor first—an incentive that rewards appeals to common ground and cross-party endorsement. Richie's analysis of endorsement patterns in RCV elections found substantially higher rates of cross-party endorsement and mutual civility among candidates than in comparable plurality races, consistent with the structural prediction.

The most rigorous technical objection to ranked-choice voting invokes Arrow's impossibility theorem, which establishes that no ranked voting system can simultaneously satisfy a set of seemingly reasonable fairness conditions, including the monotonicity criterion—the requirement that ranking a candidate higher never causes them to do worse.
RCV can produce non-monotonic results in unusual multicandidate scenarios where a candidate advances past a stronger competitor in early rounds because of the distribution of lower-ranked preferences, potentially winning in a configuration where ranking them higher would have caused their elimination. Electoral reformers like Terrill Bouricius have

acknowledged this monotonicity failure as a genuine theoretical limitation of RCV (Richie &
Bouricius, 2018). Even granting that RCV fails the monotonicity criterion in some configurations, however, this theoretical imperfection does not constitute a practical objection to RCV's superiority over plurality voting. Arrow's theorem applies to all ranked voting systems—there is no ranked system that avoids all the impossibility results—and the question is not whether RCV is theoretically perfect but whether it is better than the alternative.
Plurality voting fails majority rule consistently and predictably in every multicandidate election where no candidate receives a majority; RCV fails monotonicity only in unusual configurations that arise in a small fraction of actual elections. A theoretical limitation that manifests rarely in practice is a far smaller practical problem than a structural majority-rule violation that manifests in every competitive multicandidate plurality election.

The state-level implementations of RCV in Maine and Alaska provide the most direct evidence of how the system performs in real American elections. Maine adopted RCV for congressional primaries and general elections in 2016 and for presidential primaries in 2020;
Alaska adopted RCV for all statewide elections in 2020. In both states, the systems have operated without the administrative failures or voter confusion that critics predicted. Voter surveys in Maine found that a large majority of voters rated RCV as easy to use, and ballot exhaustion rates—the fraction of ballots that become inactive because all ranked candidates are eliminated before a majority is reached—have been consistently low, demonstrating that the majority winner guarantee is not an artifact of voters failing to rank enough candidates
(Richie & Bouricius, 2018). Alaska's 2022 special election to fill Don Young's congressional seat produced an outcome that dramatically illustrated the difference between RCV and plurality: the second-choice preferences of candidates eliminated in early rounds played a decisive role in the final outcome, demonstrating that the system functions as designed to aggregate preference information that plurality voting discards.

The research question this paper addressed—whether ranked-choice voting represents a demonstrable improvement over plurality voting for single-winner elections—has been answered affirmatively by both the theoretical analysis and the empirical evidence from implementing jurisdictions. This paper has established that RCV consistently produces majority winners, eliminates the spoiler effect, and creates behavioral incentives for less negative campaigning relative to plurality voting; that Richie's synthesis of election data from
Maine, Alaska, and municipal RCV jurisdictions confirms these theoretical predictions in practice; and that Arrow's impossibility theorem critique, while identifying a genuine theoretical limitation, does not constitute a practical objection to RCV's superiority over the plurality voting alternative. The democratic case for majority rule is not satisfied by a system that routinely produces minority winners and structural incentives for strategic rather than sincere expression of preference. RCV addresses these failures at modest administrative cost, as the Maine and Alaska implementations demonstrate. Electoral system reform is inherently politically contested, but the theoretical and empirical case for ranked-choice voting over plurality voting in single-winner elections has been made with unusual rigor and with a growing real-world evidence base.

The evidence from Alaska's 2022 special and general elections provides the most recent and high-profile illustration of RCV operating as designed in an American context.
Alaska's top-four primary combined with ranked-choice general election allowed candidates from outside the two-party structure to compete meaningfully without serving as spoilers, and the redistribution of second-choice votes in the general election produced a final winner who received majority support after elimination rounds. Richie's analysis of polling data from
Alaska voters found high rates of satisfaction with the ability to express genuine preferences and substantially lower rates of feeling that strategic voting was required compared to comparable plurality elections (Richie & Bouricius, 2018). The controversy generated by the
Alaska results—in which the candidate who led after first-choice votes was eliminated in redistribution—illustrated both the real operation of RCV and the communication challenges:
the candidate who led on first-choice preferences but did not receive majority support cannot legitimately claim to be the majority's choice under any democratic theory, but voters accustomed to plurality outcomes find this result counterintuitive.

polarization

Electoral

systems

that

reward

broad

coalition-building—including preferential voting systems like RCV—consistently produce more moderate political outcomes than systems that reward mobilizing a narrow plurality base, according to Horowitz's cross-national comparative evidence. The mechanism runs through candidate strategy: in a preferential system, candidates benefit from being acceptable across a wide range of voter preferences rather than being the intense first-choice preference of a narrow constituency. Donovan and colleagues' American evidence on reduced negative campaigning under RCV is consistent with Horowitz's theoretical prediction: when second-choice votes matter, attacking opponents risks alienating voters who prefer those

opponents and would otherwise rank your candidate second. The combination of theoretical prediction and empirical confirmation across both international comparative and American electoral data represents unusually strong evidence for a proposed mechanism in electoral reform.

The administrative objection to RCV—that it is more complicated to administer and explain to voters than plurality voting—deserves engagement as a practical constraint even if it is not a theoretical objection. Richie's review of administrative experience in RCV jurisdictions found that ballot spoilage rates (invalid ballots resulting from voter confusion) were not significantly higher in RCV elections than in comparable plurality elections, and that the availability of digital election equipment makes tabulation straightforward (Richie &
Bouricius, 2018). The communication challenge is real—voters need to understand that ranking their genuine preference first does not harm their ability to influence the race between major candidates—but this is primarily an education challenge that jurisdictions with robust voter outreach programs have addressed successfully. The Maine Secretary of State's post-implementation surveys found high voter comprehension rates after two cycles of RCV
elections, suggesting that familiarity reduces the complexity concern that looms large in advance of implementation but diminishes with experience.

The debate about RCV's effects on minor party viability raises a genuine tension in electoral reform theory that Richie's work acknowledges. Arrow's theorem establishes that no ranked system is immune to strategic manipulation, and some scholars argue that RCV still creates incentives for supporters of third-party candidates to rank major party candidates strategically to influence the final runoff rather than expressing their genuine preferences throughout their ballot. McGhee and Krimm's analysis of geographic patterns in party polarization found that the relationship between electoral systems and polarization is mediated

by geographic sorting—partisan concentration makes any electoral system's moderation effects weaker because the electorate in many districts is already predominantly of one party regardless of how preferences are aggregated (McGhee & Krimm, 2012). RCV does not solve geographic sorting, and its polarization-reducing effects are concentrated in competitive districts where second-choice preferences can actually determine outcomes. This limitation is a genuine constraint on RCV's potential contribution to reducing polarization at the national level, but it does not undermine the core majority-rule argument.

The comparison between RCV and other proposed electoral reforms—proportional representation, multi-member districts, nonpartisan primaries—reveals a design tradeoff between proportionality and geographic representation that RCV resolves in favor of maintaining single-member geographic districts while improving majority-winner outcomes within them. Proportional representation systems achieve more accurate translation of vote shares to seat shares but require multi-member districts that weaken geographic representation.
RCV maintains the geographic accountability of single-member districts while eliminating the spoiler effect and minority winner problems that Richie's analysis documents. This design choice makes RCV more politically feasible as a reform in the American context, where geographic representation is deeply embedded in constitutional structure, while still achieving the majority-rule improvements that the strongest critique of plurality voting demands. The research question this paper addressed yields a clear answer: RCV is demonstrably better than plurality voting on the dimensions that matter most for democratic theory, and the objections to it—Arrow's theorem, monotonicity, administrative complexity—are either universal to all ranked systems or manageable practical challenges that implementing jurisdictions have addressed.
