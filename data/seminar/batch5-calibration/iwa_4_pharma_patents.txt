The Evergreening Trap: How Pharmaceutical Patent Manipulation Delays Generic
Competition and Harms Patients

In 2021, the ten best-selling drugs in the United States collectively generated $97
billion in revenue; the brand-name manufacturers of those drugs held an average of 118 active patents covering each product—not because the drugs have 118 distinct inventive contributions that merit patent protection, but because the manufacturers have systematically pursued

patent

strategies

that

Aaron

Kesselheim,

Harvard

Medical

School

pharmacoepidemiologist, has documented as 'evergreening': the accumulation of secondary patents on formulation changes, delivery mechanisms, dosing intervals, and other minor modifications to extend market exclusivity beyond the expiration of the original compound patent (Kesselheim et al., 2016). Kesselheim's analysis of the top twelve selling drugs in the
United States found that secondary patents routinely extend effective market exclusivity by seven to twelve years beyond the original compound patent's expiration, with the median drug in his sample protected by thirty-eight patents—the vast majority of which were secondary patents filed after the compound patent issued. This paper argues that pharmaceutical patent evergreening represents a systematic manipulation of the patent system that delays generic competition, suppresses price reductions that would otherwise benefit patients and payers, and redirects pharmaceutical R&D investment toward reformulation and litigation rather than genuinely novel drug development—and that Congress should reform the patent linkage and listing provisions of the Hatch-Waxman Act to prevent secondary patents from delaying

generic competition when they do not represent genuine therapeutic advances.

Kesselheim and colleagues' analysis of the patent landscape surrounding top-selling drugs established that the relationship between patent protection and drug innovation is substantially more complex than the pharmaceutical industry's standard narrative acknowledges. The patent system rewards invention by providing time-limited market exclusivity that allows inventors to recoup R&D costs; the implicit bargain is that robust competition follows when patents expire, reducing prices toward competitive levels.
Kesselheim's research documents that evergreening strategies systematically delay this competition by listing secondary patents in the FDA's Orange Book—the database that controls when generic manufacturers can enter the market—even when those secondary patents cover modifications that do not improve therapeutic outcomes for patients (Kesselheim et al., 2016). Figure 1 maps the patent lifecycle for a representative top-selling drug in
Kesselheim's sample, showing the original compound patent expiration, the dates of secondary patent filing, the dates of generic application, the litigation periods triggered by
Hatch-Waxman patent challenges, and the actual date of generic entry—demonstrating that effective market exclusivity extended nine years beyond the compound patent's expiration through secondary patents and litigation. Kesselheim's policy analysis concludes that
Hatch-Waxman reforms that limit Orange Book listing to patents that genuinely claim the approved drug's therapeutic mechanism would substantially reduce evergreening without affecting incentives for genuine pharmaceutical innovation.

The patient access and pricing consequences of delayed generic competition are substantial and well-documented. Generic drugs entering the market after brand-name patent expiration typically achieve prices 70-90 percent below the brand-name price within the first two years of competition, with price reductions accelerating as the number of generic manufacturers increases. Hemphill and Sampat's analysis of the relationship between patent breadth and generic delay found that each additional year of delayed generic entry costs patients and payers billions of dollars in excess drug expenditures—costs that fall disproportionately on uninsured and underinsured patients who cannot afford brand-name prices (Hemphill & Sampat, 2012). The top twelve drugs in Kesselheim's sample collectively generated over $700 billion in excess expenditures compared to what competitive generic pricing would have produced, over the period of their secondary-patent-extended exclusivity.
Drugs

treating

chronic

conditions—diabetes,

hypertension,

high

cholesterol,

HIV—disproportionately appear in the evergreening analysis because their large patient populations make extended exclusivity especially valuable and the R&D cost of reformulation is modest relative to the revenue from delayed competition. The insulin market provides the clearest illustration: insulin analogues have been protected by overlapping secondary patents for decades, producing prices in the United States five to ten times higher than in comparable countries with different patent linkage policies.

The pharmaceutical industry's defense of secondary patents argues that reformulation and incremental innovation serve genuine patient needs—extended-release formulations that improve adherence, delivery mechanisms that reduce injection requirements, and combination products that simplify regimens can provide real therapeutic benefits even when they do not represent novel molecular entities. The industry's research associations contend that restricting
Orange Book listing to original compound patents would undermine incentives for these incremental improvements and that Kesselheim's characterization of secondary patents as uniformly unimportant misrepresents the genuine therapeutic value of some formulation improvements. Even granting that some secondary patents cover genuine therapeutic advances—and Kesselheim acknowledges this in his own analysis—the current system provides no mechanism for distinguishing patents that represent therapeutic improvements from those that represent evergreening, because Orange Book listing is self-reported and unchecked (Kesselheim et al., 2016). The reform that Kesselheim proposes addresses exactly this problem: require that Orange Book listing document the specific therapeutic advantage of the secondary patent's claimed modification, subject to FDA review of whether the modification is therapeutically meaningful. This reform would protect genuinely valuable incremental innovations while preventing the listing of patents that cover modifications with no demonstrated therapeutic advantage—which Kesselheim's analysis shows constitute the majority of secondary patents currently listed.

The international comparative evidence supports the conclusion that patent linkage reform would reduce evergreening without destroying pharmaceutical innovation. Countries with more stringent patent linkage policies—Canada, Australia, Japan—have not experienced the collapse in pharmaceutical innovation that the industry predicts would follow from reform in the United States, while simultaneously achieving substantially lower drug prices through faster generic entry. Hemphill and Sampat's analysis found that the United States generates proportionally more evergreening litigation than comparable countries precisely because its patent linkage system creates stronger financial incentives for secondary patent filing and litigation through the thirty-month stay on generic entry that Hatch-Waxman automatically grants upon patent challenge (Hemphill & Sampat, 2012). Reforming the automatic stay to apply only to patents that the FDA has determined cover genuine therapeutic advances would immediately reduce the litigation leverage that makes evergreening financially attractive, without affecting the protection afforded to genuine pharmaceutical innovation. The argument this paper has developed rests on Kesselheim's empirical analysis of what secondary patents actually cover, Hemphill and Sampat's analysis of the access costs they impose, and the international evidence that alternative patent linkage policies achieve better price outcomes without impairing innovation. This paper has established that pharmaceutical patent evergreening is not an unintended side effect of a well-functioning patent system—it is a predictable product of specific Hatch-Waxman provisions that can be reformed without sacrificing the genuine innovation incentives that patent protection is designed to provide.

The insulin market provides the clearest and most consequential illustration of pharmaceutical patent evergreening's human costs. Insulin—a century-old drug whose original patents expired decades ago—is among the most heavily evergreened pharmaceutical products in the United States. Kang and Bai's analysis found that the three primary insulin analogues used by American patients are protected by an average of sixty secondary patents each, covering delivery devices, formulations, and dosing systems that have been incrementally modified to maintain exclusivity far beyond any genuine innovation timeline (Kang & Bai,
2021). The result is that American patients pay five to ten times as much for insulin as patients in comparable countries with different patent linkage frameworks, with one in four insulin-dependent diabetics in the United States reporting rationing their insulin due to cost—a phenomenon associated with diabetic emergencies, hospitalizations, and preventable deaths.
The human cost of evergreening is not abstract: Kesselheim and colleagues' framework for understanding pharmaceutical patent manipulation was developed partly in response to the insulin crisis as the most visibly harmful instance of a systematic practice (Kesselheim et al.,
2016).

Feldman's analysis of pharmaceutical product hopping—a closely related evergreening strategy in which manufacturers discontinue the original drug formulation and shift patients to a slightly modified but identically-patented successor, undermining generic substitution even after the original patent expires—documents another mechanism through which secondary patents harm patients (Feldman, 2018). When a manufacturer discontinues the original formulation, pharmacists cannot automatically substitute the now-available generic because the manufacturer's patients have been switched to the reformulated product for which no generic equivalent exists. The product-hopping strategy is specifically designed to capture patients in a proprietary product pipeline at each patent expiration point, and Feldman's analysis establishes that it has been used by manufacturers in drugs treating attention deficit

disorder, multiple sclerosis, and other conditions with large patient populations. Kesselheim's policy analysis of these practices converges with Feldman's: the legal framework that enables both evergreening and product hopping is the Orange Book listing system that allows manufacturers to self-report secondary patents without demonstrating their therapeutic relevance to the FDA.

The international dimension of pharmaceutical patent policy provides evidence that the
United States is an outlier in allowing the extent of evergreening that Kesselheim documents.
Beall, Nickerson, Kaplan, and Attaran's comparative analysis of originator drug patents across multiple countries found that the United States generates substantially more secondary patents per approved drug than Canada, Australia, or European countries, and that the gap is explained primarily by differences in patent linkage policy rather than by differences in the underlying drug portfolio (Beall et al., 2016). Canada's system requires that Orange Book-equivalent listings be supported by demonstrated therapeutic relevance; Australia's system limits the automatic litigation stay that the American Hatch-Waxman system provides for secondary patents. The practical result is that generic competition arrives years earlier in Canada and
Australia than in the United States for the same drugs, producing substantially lower prices without impairing pharmaceutical innovation rates in those countries. The United States pharmaceutical industry generates a disproportionate share of global pharmaceutical R&D
despite its patent linkage anomalies—suggesting that international market access and high US
prices drive innovation incentives independent of the secondary patent system's contribution.

Sarpatwari, Avorn, and Kesselheim's proposal for a drug-specific FDA approval pathway represents a more targeted reform than general patent linkage revision, suggesting that the FDA require manufacturers seeking approval for a new formulation of an existing drug to demonstrate clinical superiority over the existing formulation as a condition of Orange Book

listing (Sarpatwari et al., 2015). Under this reform, a drug that is therapeutically identical to its predecessor—same active compound, same efficacy, same safety profile—would not receive the patent linkage protections that delay generic competition, while a drug that genuinely improves on the existing formulation through better efficacy, reduced side effects, or meaningful delivery improvements would continue to receive full patent protection. This design preserves incentives for genuine pharmaceutical innovation while eliminating the
Orange Book listing mechanism that allows purely commercial reformulation to delay generic entry. Kesselheim's endorsement of this approach reflects his empirical finding that the majority of secondary patents in his sample cover modifications with no demonstrated clinical advantage—modifications that should not qualify for the competitive protection that patent linkage provides.

The broader argument that pharmaceutical patent reform will destroy innovation incentives conflates two distinct questions: whether genuine pharmaceutical innovation should receive patent protection (yes, unambiguously) and whether incremental reformulation without therapeutic improvement should receive the same competitive protection as genuine innovation (no, on both efficiency and equity grounds). Kesselheim's research has established that these are different things and that the current Orange Book system does not distinguish between them. The appropriate reform follows directly: require the FDA to make the distinction that market forces and patent law cannot make on their own. This paper has established that pharmaceutical patent evergreening causes concrete and quantifiable harm to patients, that it imposes excess costs on the healthcare system without corresponding innovation benefits, and that targeted legal reforms can address the harm without disrupting incentives for the genuine pharmaceutical innovation that justifies the patent system's existence. The human cost documented in the insulin market is not an aberration but the predictable outcome of a system that Kesselheim's research has shown treats all secondary

patents as therapeutically equivalent regardless of their actual clinical value.
