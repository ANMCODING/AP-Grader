Protecting Young Minds: Why Social Media Platforms Must Implement Age Verification
for Users Under 18

When I was fourteen, I spent an average of six hours a day on Instagram and TikTok. I
didn't think much of it at the time, but looking back, I can see how much it affected the way I
saw myself and the world around me. I'm not alone — social psychologist Jonathan Haidt,
whose research on adolescent mental health has tracked the dramatic rise in teen anxiety and
depression that began around 2012, argues that the arrival of smartphone-based social media is
the primary driver of what he calls a 'great rewiring of childhood,' a transformation that has left
a generation of adolescents more anxious, more depressed, and less capable of real-world
social interaction (Haidt, 2023). This paper argues that social media platforms have both a
legal and moral obligation to implement robust age verification systems that prevent children
under thirteen from accessing their platforms and limit features available to users under
eighteen — and that the industry's decade-long resistance to meaningful age verification
reflects a deliberate choice to prioritize growth over the documented wellbeing of adolescent
users. Haidt's research provides the empirical foundation: the correlation between social media
adoption and adolescent mental health decline is not a coincidence but a causal relationship
that the platforms' own internal research confirmed even as their executives publicly denied it.

Haidt and his colleague Jean Twenge have documented the mental health crisis among
American adolescents with unusual precision, using multiple data sources to establish that

something fundamentally changed around 2012–2013 — exactly when smartphone ownership
and social media use became near-universal among American teens. Rates of depression,
anxiety, self-harm, and suicide among adolescent girls rose sharply and have not recovered.
The effect is strongest for girls and for the youngest teens, consistent with Haidt's hypothesis
that social media harms through social comparison, cyberbullying, sleep disruption, and
replacement of in-person socializing with passive consumption of curated content (Haidt,
2023). The leaked Facebook research that Frances Haugen brought to Congress in 2021
confirmed that Meta's own researchers had found Instagram was associated with body image
problems and depression in teenage girls — and that executives decided not to act because
doing so would reduce engagement (Haugen, 2021). This is not ignorance of harm; it is a
deliberate choice of profit over safety.

The age verification technology needed to implement meaningful protections already
exists. Online alcohol retailers, gambling platforms, and adult content websites all use age
verification systems that substantially reduce underage access. The UK's Age Appropriate
Design Code, which went into effect in 2021, required platforms to implement age-appropriate
safety defaults for users likely to be minors, and platforms complied — demonstrating that
when regulatory requirements have real teeth, the technical obstacles the industry claimed
were insurmountable turn out to be solvable. Haidt's policy recommendations include requiring
age verification, restricting algorithmic recommendation for users under sixteen, and imposing
a duty of care on platforms for their youngest users, analogous to the duty of care that other
industries providing products to children already face (Haidt, 2023).

Those who oppose mandatory age verification argue that it creates privacy risks by
requiring platforms to collect sensitive identity documents, and that teenagers will simply use
their parents' accounts or find workarounds. These concerns deserve genuine engagement.

Even granting that age verification is imperfect and that some teenagers will circumvent it, this
argument does not justify doing nothing. A system that successfully prevents even half of
underage users from accessing adult-designed features is better than the current system, which
prevents none. The privacy concerns are manageable through thoughtful design — age
verification can be done through trusted third-party services that confirm age without
transmitting identity documents to the platform itself. The choice is not between perfect age
verification and none at all; it is between making a good-faith effort to protect children and
hiding behind technical imperfection as an excuse for inaction.

Between 2010 and 2020, the rate of major depressive episodes among girls aged 12 to
17 increased by more than 50 percent. Emergency room visits for self-harm among adolescent
girls doubled. These are not minor fluctuations — they represent a genuine crisis in the mental
health of a generation. The timing, demographics, and cross-national pattern all point in the
same direction: rapid adoption of social media designed to maximize engagement among
adolescents who were not ready for it. Social media platforms are the only consumer product
widely used by children that face essentially no safety requirements specific to their use by
minors. The reason is not that social media is safe for children — the platforms' own leaked
internal studies show otherwise. The reason is that the platforms' lobbying power has so far
prevented the regulation that other child-facing industries routinely face.

I want to acknowledge something that might seem like a complication: not every
teenager has a bad experience on social media. Many teens use platforms to maintain
friendships, find communities of people who share their interests, and access support they may
not find elsewhere. These benefits are real. But when a product has substantial benefits for
some users and substantial documented harms for others, the appropriate response is targeted
regulation — not a choice between total prohibition and complete laissez-faire.

Thompson 5

Age-appropriate design and meaningful age verification can preserve the benefits of social
media for teenagers who can use it safely while reducing exposure for younger adolescents and
more vulnerable users for whom the harms consistently outweigh the benefits. The connection
between social media and adolescent mental health is the most important public health crisis
that is not being treated as one. This paper has argued that Haidt's documentation of the crisis,
the leaked internal research confirming that platforms knew about the harm, and the successful
implementation of age-appropriate design in the UK together establish that the obstacle to
protecting children is not technical — it's political and financial. Even granting the privacy
concerns raised by age verification's opponents, the argument for doing nothing is not
available to any platform that has seen its own internal research showing harm to the children
using its products.

Thompson 1

The research question behind this paper — whether social media platforms have an
obligation to implement age verification — has a clear answer in the evidence. The platforms
have known about the harm their products cause to adolescents, they have chosen not to act,
and the external pressure of regulation has proven effective when it has been applied. Haidt's
framework gives us both the diagnosis and the prescription: the great rewiring of childhood is
a structural problem that requires structural solutions. Age verification, later accounts for users
under sixteen, and algorithmic protections for younger users are not radical proposals. They
are the minimum that a company with genuine concern for the children using its products
would already have implemented voluntarily. The fact that these companies have not done so
voluntarily tells us everything we need to know about what will be required to make them do
it. Congress has passed COPPA, the Children's Online Privacy Protection Act, requiring
parental consent for data collection from children under thirteen — but it has not been updated
since 1998 and does not address the ways social media harms adolescents between thirteen and
eighteen. The evidence demands an updated legal framework that treats adolescent mental
health with the same seriousness we bring to adolescent physical safety.

Thompson 1

It is worth being clear about what age verification actually means in practice, because
critics sometimes characterize it as impossible or as requiring a government surveillance
infrastructure. What serious proponents of age verification actually advocate for is much more
modest: require that platforms use third-party age verification services that confirm whether a
user is above or below a threshold age, without transmitting identity documents to the platform
itself. This is how age verification works in the UK under the Online Safety Act and how
several other countries have implemented it. The platform does not need to know who you are
— only whether you are old enough to use the service. The privacy concerns that civil
libertarians raise are legitimate and should inform how age verification systems are designed,
but they do not constitute an argument against age verification in principle. They constitute an
argument for careful design of age verification systems that protect privacy while
accomplishing the protective goal. Haidt makes this distinction clearly: the goal is not to
surveil teenagers but to make it harder for platforms to claim ignorance about who is using
their products and what those products are doing to them.

Thompson 1

This paper has argued that the evidence assembled by Haidt, Twenge, and Haugen
establishes clearly that social media platforms have a documented pattern of prioritizing
engagement over the safety of adolescent users, and that the regulatory tools needed to change
this behavior exist and have been proven effective in other jurisdictions. The argument is not
complicated: products that harm children should be subject to safety requirements, and social
media platforms that knowingly expose children to content and engagement mechanisms that
cause measurable psychological harm should be no exception. Even granting that age
verification is imperfect and that teenagers are resourceful in circumventing restrictions, a
serious regulatory effort would substantially reduce harm compared to the current environment
of zero requirements. The mental health of a generation cannot be held hostage to the demand
for a perfect solution when a good-enough solution is available and other countries are already
implementing it. Congress must act.

Thompson 1

The strongest argument against any protective regulation of social media is the slippery
slope — that once we allow the government to regulate content platforms in the name of child
safety, there is no principled stopping point before censorship. This concern is legitimate but
misapplied here. Age verification and algorithmic protections for minors do not restrict the
content adults can access or create. They are narrowly targeted at the specific population —
children and adolescents — for whom the documented harms are greatest and for whom we
already accept that some restrictions on adult-oriented content are appropriate. The First
Amendment does not require that every product available to adults be equally available to
children. We already regulate children's access to alcohol, cigarettes, gambling, and explicit
sexual content without this being treated as a threat to adult freedom. Social media designed
for adult engagement patterns should be no different.
