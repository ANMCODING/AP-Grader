Filtered Reality: The Impact of Social Media on Body Image Among Teenage Girls

Social media platforms like Instagram and TikTok are filled with images of models,
influencers, and celebrities with seemingly perfect bodies, and teenage girls are their primary
audience. Research has found strong associations between social media use and body
dissatisfaction among adolescent girls, but the exact nature of this relationship — how it
works, who is most affected, and what can be done about it — is still being studied. This
investigation asks: to what extent does exposure to idealized body images on social media
contribute to body dissatisfaction and disordered eating attitudes among teenage girls aged 13
to 18, and what individual factors moderate this relationship? The research suggests that social
media exposure does contribute to body image problems for many teenage girls, especially
those who engage in active social comparison, but that the relationship is more complicated
than a simple cause-and-effect.

Renee Engeln, a psychology professor at Northwestern University who has studied
what she calls 'beauty sickness,' argues that social media intensifies the already-powerful
cultural messages about female appearance by making image comparison constant and
unavoidable (Engeln, 2017). Her research found that young women who spent more time
looking at appearance-related social media content reported higher levels of body
dissatisfaction and spent more time thinking about their own appearance compared to women
who used social media for other purposes. A study by Jasmine Fardouly and colleagues used

an experimental design to test the causal relationship more directly: women randomly assigned
to browse Facebook for a period of time reported more negative mood and higher body
dissatisfaction than women who browsed a travel website, suggesting that the effect is not just
a correlation but actually caused by the social media exposure itself (Fardouly et al., 2015).

Not all researchers agree on how strong or universal this effect is. Some studies have
found that the relationship between social media use and body dissatisfaction depends heavily
on what kind of content users see and how they engage with it. Girls who follow body-positive
accounts or use social media primarily for connecting with friends show fewer negative effects
than girls who actively compare themselves to celebrities and influencers. Researchers have
also noted that girls who already have lower self-esteem or existing body image concerns may
be more susceptible to negative effects from social media, while those with stronger
self-esteem may be more resilient. This suggests that social media is not equally harmful to
everyone and that individual psychological factors play an important role in determining how
much a given girl is affected.

Overall, the research on social media and body image suggests that exposure to
idealized images does contribute to body dissatisfaction for many teenage girls, particularly
those who engage in social comparison. The experimental evidence from Fardouly and others
provides stronger evidence for causation than many studies in this area. At the same time, the
relationship is moderated by individual factors, type of content, and how social media is used.
The research question this investigation asked is answered by the available evidence, but with
the caveat that the effect varies significantly across individuals and contexts. More research is
needed on what interventions are most effective in helping teenage girls develop healthier
relationships with social media and more resilient body image.

One emerging area of research looks at the potential for social media to also be used
positively for body image. Body-positive communities on Instagram and TikTok, which
celebrate diverse body types and challenge narrow beauty standards, have grown significantly
in recent years. Some researchers have found that exposure to body-positive content can
actually improve body satisfaction for girls who encounter it, suggesting that the problem is
not social media itself but the dominant type of content that algorithms tend to promote —
which skews heavily toward thin, conventionally attractive influencers. This points to a
potential intervention: platform changes to algorithm design that would reduce the proportion
of appearance-focused content served to teenage users. Instagram's own research, which was
leaked by whistleblower Frances Haugen, showed that the platform's algorithms were actively
amplifying content related to body image and weight loss for vulnerable teenage users even
when those users showed signs of distress. This is a different kind of finding than the
correlational studies — it suggests that the harm is not just a byproduct of social media but a
product of specific algorithmic choices that platforms could change if they chose to. The
research on body image and social media ultimately points toward the need for both

It is also worth noting that most research on this topic has focused on girls and young
women, while boys and young men are also affected by social media body image pressures —
just in different ways, with greater focus on muscularity and fitness rather than thinness.
Research on male body image and social media is less developed but suggests that boys who
use fitness and bodybuilding content on platforms like Instagram and TikTok show similar
patterns of social comparison and body dissatisfaction as girls who follow beauty and fashion
influencers. A more complete picture of social media's effects on body image would need to
account for these gender differences and the different content environments that boys and girls
tend to encounter on social platforms.

Media literacy education may be the most promising intervention for addressing social
media's impact on body image. Teaching teenage girls to recognize that social media images
are heavily filtered, curated, and edited — and that even the influencers posting them often
don't look like their photos in real life — may help reduce the power of social comparison by
making the unrealistic nature of idealized images more visible. Some research has found that
brief media literacy interventions can reduce body dissatisfaction after exposure to
appearance-focused content, though the long-term effects of such interventions are less clear.
This research question remains an important area for future investigation.

Future research should also examine how emerging platforms like TikTok, where short
video format may create different patterns of social comparison than static image platforms
like Instagram, affect body image outcomes. The research base on social media and body
image developed primarily in the era of Facebook and early Instagram, and the rapid evolution
of social platforms means that findings from even five years ago may not fully capture the
current landscape of adolescent social media use and its effects.
