An Unfair Advantage: The Case Against Legacy Preferences in College Admissions

Every year, thousands of students are rejected from elite colleges while less qualified
applicants are admitted because their parents also attended that school. This practice, known as
legacy admissions preference, gives a significant boost to applicants whose family members
are alumni, creating what many researchers have called an affirmative action program for the
wealthy and well-connected. A study by economists at Harvard found that legacy applicants at
elite universities are admitted at rates four to five times higher than non-legacy applicants with
comparable academic credentials (Arcidiacono et al., 2022). This paper argues that legacy
admissions preferences should be abolished at American colleges and universities because they
perpetuate racial and economic inequality, undermine the principle that admissions should be
based on merit and potential, and provide no educational benefit that cannot be achieved
through other means. The practice survives primarily because it benefits the alumni donors
who fund universities, not because it serves the educational mission that justifies universities'
existence.

The data on who benefits from legacy admissions makes the equity problem impossible
to ignore. Because the alumni of elite universities are disproportionately white and wealthy —
a product of the explicit racial exclusion that characterized higher education for most of
American history — legacy preferences systematically advantage applicants from the most
privileged backgrounds. A study of thirty elite universities found that legacy students were

significantly more likely to be white, from high-income families, and from private secondary
schools than non-legacy admits (Espenshade and Radford, 2009). At the same time, these are
institutions that claim to be committed to diversity and equal opportunity. The contradiction
between those stated values and the actual practice of giving preferences to the children of
privileged alumni is not subtle. When Johns Hopkins University eliminated legacy preferences
in 2020, it reported no decline in alumni giving in the subsequent years — directly refuting the
most common argument universities use for maintaining the practice.

Some university administrators argue that legacy admissions are necessary to maintain
the alumni relationships and donations that fund financial aid, scholarships, and research that
benefit all students. This argument is worth taking seriously because universities do depend on
alumni support, and that support does fund programs that help less advantaged students.
However, the evidence from universities that have abolished legacy preferences does not
support the claim that giving up legacy admissions means giving up alumni donations. MIT,
which does not consider legacy status, has one of the most generous alumni giving rates in the
country. The assumption that alumni will stop donating if their children can no longer receive
preferential treatment may reflect a cynical view of alumni loyalty that is not actually borne
out by the data. Most alumni give because they value their institution, not because they expect
a quid pro quo in the admissions process.

The argument for abolishing legacy preferences ultimately rests on a simple principle:
college admissions should evaluate what applicants can contribute to and benefit from a
college education, not who their parents are. This is not a radical position — it is the stated
principle of every selective university in the country. The gap between that stated principle and
the actual practice of legacy preferences is a form of institutional hypocrisy that students and
the public are increasingly unwilling to tolerate. The Supreme Court's 2023 decision

eliminating race-conscious admissions actually makes the case against legacy preferences
stronger, not weaker: if universities cannot consider race as a factor in admissions, they should
also not be able to consider whether an applicant's grandfather attended the same school.
Consistency requires that the move toward purely merit-based admissions that the Court
demanded apply to all preferences, not just the ones that help historically excluded groups.

In conclusion, legacy admissions preferences are an unjustifiable holdover from a time
when elite universities openly operated as finishing schools for the children of the white
Protestant establishment. The research shows they provide a substantial boost to
already-advantaged applicants, the equity harm is well-documented, and the financial
argument for keeping them doesn't hold up to scrutiny. Colleges that are serious about being
the engines of social mobility they claim to be should follow the example of Johns Hopkins
and MIT and eliminate legacy preferences entirely. The students who would take those spots
deserve them on merit.

Okafor 1

The history of legacy admissions is inseparable from the history of racial exclusion in
American higher education. For most of the twentieth century, elite universities explicitly
excluded Black, Jewish, Catholic, and other non-Protestant applicants while giving preferential
treatment to the sons — and eventually daughters — of Protestant white alumni. Legacy
preferences were not created as a neutral alumni relations tool; they were created as a
mechanism for maintaining the demographic composition of elite institutions against the
perceived threat of academically qualified applicants from excluded groups. The fact that the
most selective universities in the country still give preferences to the descendants of alumni
who attended during periods of explicit racial exclusion is not a coincidence or a historical
footnote. It is a structural inheritance of that exclusionary past that continues to shape who gets
the opportunities that elite college attendance provides.

The Supreme Court's 2023 decision in SFFA v. Harvard and UNC eliminated
race-conscious admissions programs, holding that universities could not consider race as a
factor in holistic admissions review. In that same litigation, the data revealed that Harvard's
admissions office gave significant preferences to legacy applicants and children of donors —
preferences that disproportionately benefited white applicants. The Court did not address these
preferences, but the logic of its decision points directly at them. If considering race as one
factor among many is unconstitutional because the Constitution requires colorblind evaluation
of applicants, then considering which applicant's grandfather attended the same school is also
inconsistent with the principle of individual merit the Court invoked. Several members of
Congress have introduced legislation that would eliminate legacy preferences at universities
receiving federal funds, and the Department of Education opened a civil rights investigation
into Harvard's legacy admissions policies in 2023. The political and legal momentum is
shifting against legacy preferences even if the institutions themselves have been slow to
respond.

Okafor 2

What would be lost if elite universities eliminated legacy preferences entirely?
Probably much less than administrators claim. The universities that have eliminated them —
MIT, Johns Hopkins, Amherst, and others — have not experienced the alumni relations
catastrophe that legacy defenders predict. Alumni continue to give, sometimes at higher rates,
because their connection to their university is based on their own experience there, not on the
expectation of special treatment for their children. The real constituency for legacy preferences
is not rank-and-file alumni but the very wealthy donors who make large gifts partly in
expectation of admissions preferences for their children. Losing that constituency would
require universities to fundraise more broadly — from the larger population of alumni who
believe in what their institution stands for — and that is probably a healthier long-term model
for institutional fundraising than dependence on the transactional giving of a small number of
very wealthy families.

Okafor 1

It is worth being honest about what abolishing legacy preferences would and would not
accomplish. Getting rid of legacy preferences would not by itself make elite universities
accessible to low-income or first-generation students. The barriers to elite college access go far
deeper than legacy preferences — they include the vast disparities in K-12 educational quality,
access to test preparation, extracurricular opportunities, and college counseling support that
mean wealthy students are far better prepared to compete for elite admissions regardless of
whether legacy preferences exist. What ending legacy preferences would do is remove one
specific mechanism by which already-privileged students gain an additional advantage they
have not earned. That matters both instrumentally — every legacy spot admitted on the basis
of family connection is a spot not available to a student admitted purely on merit — and
symbolically, because institutions that claim to select on merit while practicing legacy
preferences are not being honest about what they actually value. Ending legacy preferences
won't fix higher education. But it would make elite universities slightly more honest about
what they are and slightly more accessible to students who have earned their place rather than
inherited it.

Okafor 1

The students who are displaced by legacy admits are not hypothetical. They are real
applicants — disproportionately first-generation college students, students from lower-income
families, and students of color — who compete without any inherited advantage in a system
that is supposed to reward what they have accomplished rather than who their parents are.
Every year that legacy preferences continue is another year that a small number of
well-connected families receive an unearned advantage at institutions that claim to be
meritocratic. The students who lose those spots deserve better from institutions that benefit
enormously from public trust, public funding, and the social prestige that comes from being
associated with the aspiration of equal opportunity.

Okafor 1

The fact that elite universities have continued to defend legacy preferences even as the
evidence against them has mounted reflects something important about institutional inertia.
These are institutions that pride themselves on evidence-based decision-making in every other
domain — they publish research on education policy, they debate the best ways to structure
financial aid and student support, they survey their graduates to measure long-term outcomes.
But on legacy preferences, the evidence that the policy undermines their stated values of merit
and opportunity has simply been set aside because changing it would require confronting a
small but powerful constituency of wealthy alumni donors. That is a choice, not an
inevitability, and it is a choice that gets harder to defend as public awareness of the practice
grows and as the legal landscape shifts after the Supreme Court's 2023 admissions decision.

Okafor 1

Legacy preferences represent a concrete, fixable problem in college admissions. Unlike
the broader structural inequalities in K-12 education that are genuinely difficult to address
quickly, ending legacy preferences requires only a policy decision by university
administrators. That they have not made that decision, despite the evidence and despite
growing public and political pressure, tells us something about who these institutions
ultimately serve. The students who are denied admission because legacy applicants took their
spots deserve better. The public trust that makes elite universities valuable depends on their
being at least approximately what they claim to be. Both considerations point in the same
direction: it is time to end legacy preferences.

The evidence is clear and the path forward is straightforward: end legacy preferences
now.
