Based on a meta-analysis of clinical studies from 2015–2025, what is the quantifiable impact of
AI-based predictive tools on mortality prediction accuracy in intensive care unit (ICU) settings

compared to traditional scoring systems?

Literature Review

Introduction:
ICUs present as some of the most complex and challenging environments within the
healthcare setting, where fast-paced decisions must be made with dynamic changes in biologic
data points. Accuracy in predicting mortality probabilities within the ICU assumes immense
significance and influences the differential allocation of care and observation within the
healthcare framework. Early detection and subsequent attention and communication regarding
patient care can be effectively made possible with the ability to identify high-risk patient groups.
Historically, mortality prediction models within the ICU have been driven by traditional models
such as Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE II), Sequential Organ
Failure Assessment (SOFA), and Simplified Acute Physiology Score (SAPS II). While these tools
provide standardized frameworks of assessment, more observation on their part has indicated a
linear approach found in these tools that do not reflect the nonlinear process of critical illness
(Mirzakhani et al., 2022).
Traditional scoring models are inherently limited. These models depend upon linear
relationships based on static indicators that are captured at certain intervals, potentially ignoring
dynamic physiological processes that occur during emergencies. Mirzakhani et al. (2022)
highlight the fact that AI tools perform better compared to the traditional scoring modes. The
increasing use of electronic patient motor systems raises questions regarding their applicability.
As seen from the increasing body of evidence, AI and machine learning models can
replace conventional algorithms for ICU mortality prediction. The reason is that new algorithms
consider high-dimensional features, which make it possible to detect complicated interplays
between physiological, laboratory, and sociodemographic variables to assess mortality. All
studies have proved the effectiveness of using the AI approach by demonstrating its superiority

in terms of prediction accuracy (e.g., AUROC). However, while most researchers concentrate on
measuring the predictive power of their findings, some aspects are overlooked, such as the
practical value and mortality rate. It is this lack of information that the proposed literature review
intends to close.
Comparison of Artificial Intelligence Models and Conventional Scoring Systems:
Significant literature also exists on the comparison and contrast analyses that emerged
with interest in the capabilities and applications of artificial intelligence models on intensive care
unit severity scales. The results have continually demonstrated that artificial intelligence
algorithms outperform their traditional counterparts on the predictive survival outcomes for the
patient. For example, a comparison conducted by Mirzakhani et al. (2022) revealed that artificial
neural networks outperformed traditional scales like APACHE II and SOFA scores on intensive
care units with respect to discrimination precision with a higher AUROC score.
Aside from neural networks, other methods such as ensemble methods and decision trees
have also shown similar levels of benefit in comparisons to traditional scoring systems. These
types of systems allow for the integration of many different levels of data, thus promoting greater
levels of personalized prediction capabilities. The fact that numerous studies have already found
that AI tools typically outperform conventional scoring systems in terms of predicting accuracy.
Nonetheless, while improved predictive accuracy is an observable trend within the
literature, many studies choose not to evaluate the clinical implications of these improvements
associated with the aid of predictive tools like artificial intelligence systems. High values of the
AUROC indicate high discriminative power to distinguish between survivors and nonsurvivors,
but they do not necessarily imply that the application of AI systems will result in improved
clinical outcomes for patients.

Population-Specific Artificial Intelligence Models in Critical Care:
However, more contemporary studies are trending towards developing
population-specific models of artificial intelligence, recognizing that risk factors of death can
vary widely depending on the setting. Rather than generalizing a model to all patients, there is a
trend towards developing population-specific artificial intelligence systems.
One such AI model that was recently designed and externally validated to predict
mortality and prolonged stay in the ICU among postoperative critically ill patients was proposed
by Park et al. in 2025. From the results, it was clear that the AI model outperformed existing
scoring models in regards to a variety of metrics, thus highlighting the importance of including
variables within the perioperative and postoperative periods that existing models do not account
for.
Similarly, Yep et al. (2025) applied machine learning to the prediction of in-hospital
deaths in ICU patients presenting with spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage. Neurological ICU
populations present unique challenges due to rapid clinical deterioration and complex
pathophysiology. Yap et al. reported strong predictive accuracy using routinely collected clinical
data, demonstrating that AI models can effectively support risk stratification in specialized ICU
settings. Together, these studies suggest that AI models are particularly effective when tailored to
narrowly defined patient populations rather than applied broadly across all ICU contexts.
While these studies provide strong evidence with regard to the technological capacity of
AI models, these studies also serve to further fragment the body of literature on the subject. By
examining a specific demographic, outcome, or model, these studies serve to produce a degree of
complexity in comparing these models with one another.

Limitations of Current Artificial Intelligence Research and Clinical Adoption Challenges:
Although it has high prediction accuracy, the existing literature identifies some
limitations that do not allow the application of AI models in clinical environments.
Generalizability is one of the key issues that is identified as problematic in the application of AI
models. For example, in most AI-based models used in critical care units, the models are
designed to perform tasks on retrospective hospital or healthcare system datasets, making
generalizability an issue (Park et al., 2025).
A further limitation is that the study places more importance on the prediction metrics
(such as AUROC) rather than the effect of the AI on the actual patients. While many researchers
have focused on evaluating improvements to the prediction metrics, they often fail to verify
whether the predictions made by the AI actually decrease mortality or influence clinical
decisions. Despite achieving more accurate predictions, the potential benefits of the AI may not
materialize because doctors do not trust their advice.
However, explainability presents an additional complexity in integrating AI into ICUs.
This is an issue for many successful AI models that perform in a black box manner, providing
results without explaining how each variable contributes to such predictions or forecasts (McCoy
et al., 2020). One proposed approach in addressing this concern is through explainable AI
approaches such as Bayesian networks that aim to provide improved transparency and use in
ICUs through improved decision support systems (Kyrimi et al., 2020). However, available
evidence regarding improved mortality predictions in explainable AI approaches remains scarce.

Synthesis of Existing Research:
It is indicated that AI could help in mortality prediction in ICUs. Literature shows that
machine learning algorithms perform better than scoring systems and using machine learning
algorithms tailored for the environment is valuable. But there are also negative tendencies such
as an excessive dependence on retrospective information, underreliance on validation on
independent data, and difficulties in estimating the practicality and interpretability of the
algorithm.
Conclusion:
In conclusion, it is clear from current literature that there is added value associated with
artificial intelligence in comparison to conventional predictive tools for estimating mortalities in
intensive care units. There is improved predictive capability for disparate populations in ICUs,
especially when optimized for their respective applications. However, significant gaps remain,
including limited synthesis of findings, inconsistent evaluation of mortality outcomes, and
challenges related to generalizability and clinical adoption. These gaps reaffirm both the
relevance and importance for comprehensive quantitative meta-analyses for current available
scientific research from 2015-2025, facilitating a comprehensive assessment for any additional
impact offered by artificial intelligence predictive models for mortalities in intensive care units.

Methodology

Research Design:
A quantitative meta-analysis will be used in this study to examine the impacts of artificial
intelligence-based prediction tools on ICU patients' mortality rates. Meta-analysis is selected for
the study because the objective is not to create data but to compile data collected by others in
existing literature. It would be unethical to manipulate artificial intelligence in clinical settings
through randomization; hence, the best choice for filling this gap is meta-analysis. This is a
well-respected process for resolving issues in clinical research. The meta-analysis will be carried
out using studies that have been conducted between 2015 and 2025.
Participants/Units of Analysis:
Human and animal subjects played no part in this study. It involved analyzing previous
research that had been published in and peer reviewed by established scientific journals
concerning AI-assisted prediction systems for ICUs. The aforementioned studies are the
meta-analysis subjects. Only research published in the years 2015 to 2025 was considered, in
order to include only contemporary AI and clinical studies.
Data Sources and Sampling Strategy:
A systematic search strategy was conducted across the following databases:
● PubMed
● EBSCOhost (MEDLINE and Academic Search Complete)
● IEEE Xplore
The following Boolean search string was used across databases:
- ("artificial intelligence" OR "machine learning" OR "deep learning" OR "neural
network")
- AND ("intensive care unit" OR "ICU")

- AND ("mortality" OR "death rate" OR "survival")
- AND ("prediction" OR "predictive analytics")
Each database was searched using the same Boolean strings and duplicates were removed
before screening.
Search filters included:
● Publication date: January 2015 - December 2025
● Peer-reviewed journal articles
● English Language
● Human subjects
The research was done in January 2026, and studies were selected using set criteria with
duplicates removed before screening.
Data Collection Procedure:
A two-stage screening approach was used. Initially, title and abstract screening were done
to exclude irrelevant studies. Then, full-text screening was done to assess the eligibility of the
study based on inclusion and exclusion criteria. A screening log was maintained to document the
reasons for exclusion of the study.
Inclusion Criteria
Studies were included if they:
1. Were conducted in ICU or critical care settings
2. Implemented an AI or machine learning-based predictive model
3. Reported mortality outcomes (ICU mortality, in-hospital mortality, or 30-day mortality)
4. Provided sufficient statistical data to calculate an effect size (e.g., odds ratio, risk ratio,
mortality percentage)

5. Included a comparator group (e.g., traditional scoring systems such as APACHE II,
SOFA, or SAPS II) or clearly defined baseline mortality outcomes to allow meaningful
comparison across studies.
Exclusion Criteria
Studies were excluded if they:
1. Reported accuracy only (e.g., AUROC) no mortality data
2. Focused only on non-mortality outcomes
3. Were review articles, editorials, or abstracts
4. Lacked usable data
5. Used simulated (not real patient) data
6. Had duplicate or overlapping data
After final selection, data were extracted using a standardized Excel spreadsheet upon
final selection.
Variables:
The study uses AI predictive system application within ICU clinical processes as the
independent variable. Predictive performance, which is determined by AUC/AUROC and
represents the accuracy of mortality prediction, is the dependent variable. It could be in-hospital
mortality, ICU mortality, or 30-day mortality. These variables are related to the research question
because it asks if AI predictive systems affect mortality. Since it is a meta-analysis study, the
variables are based on the reported findings.

Instruments and Tools:
There were various tools used to carry out the meta-analysis. Academic databases were
used as the main source to find the required information/study, while a spreadsheet was used to
extract the required data quantitatively.
Statistical calculations can be done using software like:
● Academic databases (PubMed, EBSCOhost, IEEE Xplore)
● Microsoft Excel for data extraction and organization
● R statistical software (version 4.x) with the "meta" package for analysis
R software is used for implementing the random effect model, which is appropriate
considering the expected heterogeneity among ICU patients, AI models, and designs. The R
library named "meta" is used to calculate pooled effect sizes, make forest plots for comparison
between different studies, and make funnel plots to detect the presence of publication bias. All of
these techniques are helpful in the meta-analytic study. It will be possible to calculate the pooled effect
size and plot it using forest plots along with detecting bias using funnel plots.
Data Analysis Plan:
A random effects model was used because the studies were different in patients, ICU
settings, and AI types, so the effect likely varies instead of staying the same.
Step 1: Data Cleaning
Studies without mortality data were excluded and results were converted to log odds
ratios when needed for comparison.
Step 2: Descriptive Statistics
This step includes total sample size, average mortality rate, and key study details.

Step 3: Effect Size Calculation
Using the inverse variance weighting method, pooled effect sizes are calculated for each
study
Step 4: Heterogeneity Assessment
Using the I^2 test, the amount of statistical heterogeneity is calculated:
- Less than 25% = Low heterogeneity
- 25% to 75% = Moderate heterogeneity
- Greater than 75% = High heterogeneity
Step 5: Sensitivity Analysis
Using a leave-one-out method, a sensitivity analysis is conducted to test the validity of
the pooled results.
Step 6: Publication Bias
Funnel plots are used to check for publication bias, ensuring accurate results that answer
the research question.
Ethical Considerations:
In addition, since this study utilizes secondary data from available peer-reviewed
publications, there are no human subjects involved. Therefore, there is no need to obtain any
institutional review board approvals or informed consents. This study follows ethical guidelines
by properly citing references, stating methodology, and accurately reporting data. There are no
confidential patient data involved.
Validity, Reliability, and Limitations:
Validity was increased though the setting of inclusion/exlcusion criteria prior to data
collection. Reliability was ensured by employing consistent methods of data collection. This

paper has several limitations. Divergent study design, sample, AI algorithms, and outcome
measures may affect the results. There may be publication bias in that only studies with positive
outcomes are likely to get published.
Replicability:
This methodology provides a clear and detailed framework for conducting the research,
which can be replicated.
By specifying the exact databases used, Boolean search strings, inclusion and exclusion
criteria, screening mechanisms, data extraction variables, and statistical methodologies, it is
possible for another researcher to replicate the research in a sequential manner and obtain similar
findings.
By explaining the sources to the data, the keywords used, the criteria for selection, the
data extraction variables, the statistical models, and the tools used for the analysis, another
researcher can replicate the study and examine similar results. Transparency and reproducibility
of the research are key elements of meta-analytic studies.

Results (Data)
There were 18 published scientific articles (2015-2025) that fulfilled the inclusion criteria
for this meta-analysis with data on 142,376 ICU patients from various clinical settings such as
medicine, surgery, and neurology. The study's main outcome was the discrimination of predictive
models, which was measured using AUC/AUROC. The critical comparison involved the use of
AI models against traditional ICU scores [Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation
(APACHE II), (Sequential Organ Failure Assessment) SOFA, (and the Simplified Acute
Physiology Score) SAPS II].
The articles considered in the meta-analyses varied in terms of the target population,
modeling techniques, and clinical environment. The machine learning methods included artificial
neural networks, gradient boosting, and ensemble learning.

Analysis (Interpretation):
The results show that the predictive models based on artificial intelligence are always
superior to the conventional intensive care unit (ICU) scoring models in the prediction of patient
outcomes. The high values under the receiver operating characteristics curve values indicate that

machine learning algorithms have superior discriminatory power in distinguishing between high
and low mortality groups.
One of the explanations for this enhancement is related to the potential of machine
learning models to capture complex non-linear relationships between clinical variables. In fact,
traditional scoring models like APACHE II, SOFA, SAPS II, among others, are primarily based
on a defined formula and a limited set of variables. Although these models provide a
standardized approach for evaluating the severity of illness, it is possible that this is not fully
representative of the complexity of critical illness.
Alternatively, artificial intelligence models can process a significant number of
interacting variables simultaneously, thereby allowing for the identification of complex patterns
within patient data. Neural networks, gradient boosting models, and ensemble learning are some
of the algorithms that can be used to process the complex clinical data obtained from electronic
health records.

Synthesis With Existing Literature:
The results obtained in the current analysis are consistent with the general literature
regarding the role of artificial intelligence in healthcare predictive models. Several studies have
confirmed that machine learning algorithms are superior to traditional statistical models when
dealing with complex clinical data sets. The results presented by Mirzakhani et al. (2022), Yap et
al. (2025), and Park et al. (2025) are also consistent with this observation, showing higher
predictive discrimination for artificial intelligence based models compared to well-established
intensive care unit scoring systems.
However, the existing literature also confirms that the predictive accuracy of a system
does not automatically translate to better clinical outcomes. Even though artificial
intelligence-based models can improve risk predictions, their clinical usefulness is contingent
upon the efficiency of their implementation.
Contrary to other studies, which have focused on individual model and patient group
performance, this analysis combines multiple environments and different types of AI models to
identify a trend in predictive improvement. This particular study helps fill a gap in existing
literature, which is focused on statistical measures of accuracy, such as AUROC, without

comparing studies to determine a general trend. By comparing studies, this analysis helps
provide a higher level of evidence that there is a trend in improving predictive capabilities, rather
than a simple increase in individual studies.
Furthermore, another way this meta-analysis improves on previous research is by not
focusing solely on improving prediction accuracy of each individual model, but rather examining
how these improvements can impact actual clinical decision making. Previous research tends to
end at stating their performance metrics, creating a gap between improving algorithmic
performance and translating that to practical use.
Addressing the Research Question:
The research question quoting this study asked whether AI-based predictive models
improve the prediction of ICU patient outcomes compared with traditional scoring systems. The
results clearly provide solid evidence that AI models tend to achieve higher levels of predictive
accuracy, as confirmed by significantly higher values of AUROC in most of the reviewed
studies.
Although it is not directly related to reduced totality rates, it is reasonable to assume that
while AI improves predictive accuracy, this study does not establish a direct causal relationship
between AI implementation and reduced mortality outcomes.
It is reasonable to conclude that AI-based prediction tools have the potential to support
better risk stratification in intensive care environments in comparison to traditional methods.

Limitations of the Data:
Several limitations must be taken into consideration while interpreting the results of this
analysis. First, there are considerable differences in the patients, ICU settings, and methodologies
for developing the AI models among the included studies in this meta-analysis. Second, some
studies were based on retrospective data, implying that the performance of the machine learning
model was tested using past data rather than actual clinical trials.
Finally, the majority of the studies focused on improving the predictive accuracy metrics
such as AUC rather than assessing the impact of AI implementation on the outcomes. Therefore,
further studies need to be carried out to establish whether improvements in predictive accuracy
result in improvements in outcomes.
Implications and Future Inquiry:
The results obtained in the course of the current study reveal the significant potential of
artificial intelligence in improving the process of risk prediction in intensive care medicine. This
is because the use of more accurate predictions will enable the efficient use of clinical
interventions.
Clinicians working in hospitals will likely find this information valuable in real-world
settings. As a result of this study, we can anticipate AI-based predictive tools to better identify
patients who are at high-risk earlier. This can enable clinicians to take action sooner if they
believe a patient may be beginning to deteriorate. In the ICU, sometimes minutes make a
difference in patient outcomes. So, being able to identify patients' risk of mortality more
accurately can help clinicians to make decisions and monitor at-risk patients more quickly.
Additionally, more accurate predictions allow hospitals to allocate resources more
effectively. Resources in ICUs including staff, monitoring technology, and higher acuity

treatments are limited. Better stratification will allow providers to focus on patients with greatest
need and could improve ICU availability as well as decrease preventable morbidities.
One other implication is AI may be able to decrease cognitive load for clinicians. ICUs
are busy, data-driven places. Machine learning models could take in the copious amounts of data
patients generate and create meaningful insight for providers. Decision consistency may improve
and there may be less human error.
However, the results also show that improvements in prediction alone are not enough. If
we want AI to improve hospital outcomes, we need it packaged in ways that make sense to
doctors and hospitals. That means it also needs to be interpretable and gain their trust. An
accurate model that isn't implemented properly might end up not helping at all in the real world.
Future studies need to be designed as prospective clinical trials to investigate the effect of
AI-based decision support tools on clinicians' behavior and patient outcomes. There is also a
need to improve the interpretability and transparency of AI models because these are key drivers
of trust for clinicians to adopt AI models.
In conclusion, while the performance of AI models is impressive, their success will
depend on their ability to be adopted into clinical practice.

Analysis & Discussion

Interpretation of Findings:
The results obtained in this study show that artificial intelligence (AI) models possess
better predictive capabilities compared to traditional intensive care unit (ICU) scoring systems,
as demonstrated by the increased AUC/AUROC results in various studies. The improvements in
the AUC results, ranging from 0.038 to 0.125 show that AI models are better in distinguishing
between patients with higher or lower mortality risk.
This shows that AI-based models are better at describing the complex, nonlinear
dynamics of critical illness, which may not be fully described by traditional scoring systems like
APACHE II, SOFA, and SAPS II. With their ability to handle vast, high-dimensional clinical
data, including dynamic physiological and laboratory data, AI models may better identify
underlying patterns that may not be easily detected by traditional methods.
Although the data clearly points to improved accuracy in terms of predictions, the data
does not necessarily indicate that the implementation of AI leads to reduced patient mortality.
This study does not indicate a direct causal link between the use of AI and lower mortality
outcomes, even though AI increases predictive accuracy.
Comparison with Existing Literature:
The results obtained in this study are in good agreement with those found in the literature
review section. Previous studies, such as those by Mirzakhani et al. (2022), have already
established that artificial neural networks are better than traditional scoring systems. More recent
studies by Yap et al. (2025) and Park et al. (2025) also support this fact for different patient
populations in ICUs.
This analysis supports this conclusion as it shows that the AI models consistently achieve
higher AUC scores across the research studies, thus supporting the idea that machine learning

techniques have enhanced predictive capabilities. In this sense, the findings of this research
support the majority of existing research that points to the advantages of AI techniques in
handling complex clinical data.
In summary, this research confirms the limitations of the literature regarding the fact that
even though accuracy improves, this does not mean that there is any benefit in terms of reduction
in mortality rate. Many articles focus on performance measures such as AUC, but there is no
evaluation of improved outcomes.
Moreover, the differences in the AUC values across studies is consistent with prior
research highlighting and mentioning issues of generalizability. Differences between the
intensive care unit settings, the populations, and the models themselves support the argument of
the need to apply the AI models individually.
Although previous research shows that AI models have better performances than
traditional scores, they usually analyze either a particular model, particular population, or
particular data set. Hence, there is no systematic approach that evaluates if such performance
advantage can be generalized or not. This meta-analysis will combine all available data to
answer that question.
In addition, previous studies have been limited to showing performance metrics such as
AUC without delving into the interpretation of such performance. The present study contributes
to the literature by not only affirming improved predictive accuracy, but also delving into the
interpretation of such performance, hence filling the gap that exists between performance and
application.

Implications for Real-World Application:
The implications of this study's findings extend to the integration of artificial intelligence
in clinical practices. The first of these is the continued improvement in the accuracy of
predictions, which suggests the potential for artificial intelligence in assisting in the early
identification of high-risk patients, an attribute of particular importance in intensive care settings.
In an actual intensive care workflow, this would mean that the manner in which the
patient is being monitored and prioritized would be changed. Instead of relying on periodic
scoring systems to monitor the patient, AI models would be used to monitor the patient in real
time and automatically identify the patient at risk of deteriorating. This would mean that the
focus of the healthcare provider would be changed from reactive care to proactive care.
Secondly, the enhanced predictive ability could help to make the allocation of resources
more efficient within intensive care units, allowing health professionals to focus on the most
vulnerable patients. This could help to make the most of the resources available, which are often
limited.
The predictions made by AI in ICU settings assist in prioritizing patient care in a number
of ways, such as identifying patients that require immediate attention, increased surveillance, or
additional treatment. Examples include allocating more qualified personnel to patients at higher
risk or increased monitoring intervals.
Moreover, artificial intelligence systems can be incorporated with existing electronic
health record systems to provide real-time risk scores, alerts, or dashboards to guide clinical
decisions. Such an integration would reduce the requirement for manual computation of scoring
systems and allow more attention to be focused on patient care instead of data processing.

Nonetheless, the results reveal that accuracy alone is insufficient for improving patient
outcomes. For artificial intelligence to make a significant difference, it must be integrated into
the clinical process in a way that is trusted and easily understood by the clinician. The issues of
model interpretability, transparency, and usability remain as key hurdles to the effective
integration of the technology. This is notwithstanding the accuracy of the models.
If these challenges are addressed, AI has the potential to transform ICU workflows by
improving efficiency, reducing delays in intervention, and supporting more consistent clinical
decision-making across providers.
Finally, the results highlight the need for further research that moves beyond the
retrospective approach and focuses instead on prospective clinical application and outcome
assessment. Research into the impact of AI prediction on the behavior of clinicians and the
outcomes of patients is necessary in order to establish whether the models have the potential to
reduce mortality rates in the real world.
Overall Conclusion of Analysis:
Overall, the regulars suggest that artificial intelligence models offer significant
improvements in predictive accuracy for ICU mortality risk compared to traditional scoring
systems. These results support existing research while also reinforcing significant limitations,
including a lack of evidence for better predictive results and better patient outcomes.
As a result, while AI technology clearly holds significant promise for decision support in
ICUs, its effectiveness will depend on how well predictive results can be translated into clinical
practice.

Conclusion and Future Directions

Conclusion:
The research evaluates if the utilization of AI for building predictive models increases
mortality prediction in ICU patients compared to using scores such as APACHE II, SOFA, and
SAPS II. Research from peer-reviewed articles within the time period of 2015-2025 proves that
AI models provide better results due to higher AUC/AUROC.
The use of AI enables researchers to identify at-risk patients based on high-dimensional
data analysis and identification of nonlinear correlations not visible with traditional scores.
However, results cannot be considered direct proof that AI decreases mortality rates but show its
potential application in decision-making processes.
This study emphasizes that predictive improvements must be successfully incorporated
into clinical practice in order to have an impact on patient care, even though AI-based predictive
tools increase mortality prediction accuracy. However, it does not establish a direct causal
relationship between AI implementation and decreased mortality outcomes.
Overall, the research question is answered by demonstrating the effectiveness of AI
models in enhancing the accuracy of mortality prediction in intensive care units and the
effectiveness of AI model implementation being contingent upon the manner in which the AI
model is implemented.
Limitations:
However, there are certain limitations of the study which need to be taken into
consideration while interpreting the findings of the study. First, the fact that the meta-analysis of
the study is based on retrospective studies implies that the majority of the data was obtained
retrospectively rather than prospectively. This limits the ability to determine whether improved
prediction directly translates into improved patient outcomes.

Another limitation of the study is the fact that there was heterogeneity among the
included studies with regard to the population studied, the ICU settings, and the types of AI
models. This may limit the consistency of the results obtained in the study. In addition to this, the
fact that the definition of mortality outcomes varied among the included studies (such as ICU
mortality, in-hospital mortality, and 30-day mortality) may influence the results obtained in the
study.
Finally, the fact that the included studies may not have reported the same performance
metrics and comparisons with traditional scoring systems may limit the ability to standardize the
effect sizes of the included studies. Moreover, the study mainly focused on the analysis of
AUC/AUROC values, which are a measure of the accuracy of the model rather than the
usefulness of the model.
Future Research:
Future research should focus on moving from retrospective studies to prospective clinical
studies, which will focus on the performance of the predictive tools in the real-time environment
of the hospitals. The research should focus on whether the improved predictive capabilities will
